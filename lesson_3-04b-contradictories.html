CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning  |  ELA.11.C.5.1 — Use revision to strengthen reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the two contradictory pairs on the Square of Opposition (A–O and E–I), state the rule governing contradictories (cannot both be true; cannot both be false), and use the contradictory relationship to infer the truth value of one statement from the known truth value of its contradictory.

Where We Are

Lesson 3.04a introduced the Square of Opposition as a map of five inferential relationships. This lesson examines the most powerful of those relationships: the contradictory. The contradictory pairs — A with O, and E with I — occupy the two diagonals of the Square. They have a property no other pair shares: knowing the truth value of one member of a contradictory pair tells you with certainty the truth value of the other. There is no room for "undetermined" when contradictories are involved.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider the statement "All students passed the exam." Now consider "Some students did not pass the exam." These two statements cannot both be true — if all students passed, then it is false that some did not. And they cannot both be false — if it is false that all students passed, then at least one student did not pass, which means the particular negative is true. The two statements exhaust all possibilities. There is no logical space between them.

This exhaustiveness — the way contradictories divide the logical space completely — is what makes the relationship so powerful. It means that refuting one statement is equivalent to establishing the other. If you can show that "All swans are white" is false, you have shown that "Some swans are not white" is true. The O statement follows directly from the falsity of the A statement.

The Contradictory Pairs

On the Square of Opposition, there are exactly two contradictory pairs:

  • A and O: "All S are P" and "Some S are not P" — the universal affirmative and the particular negative, connected by the diagonal from the top-left to the bottom-right corner.
  • E and I: "No S are P" and "Some S are P" — the universal negative and the particular affirmative, connected by the diagonal from the top-right to the bottom-left corner.

In each pair, one statement is universal (making a claim about all members of S) and the other is particular (making a claim about some). One is affirmative and the other is negative. Together they represent every possibility: either the universal claim holds for all of S, or there is at least one counterexample. There is no middle ground.

The Rule of Contradictories:

Contradictory statements cannot both be true and cannot both be false.

— If the A statement is true, the O statement is false. If the A statement is false, the O statement is true.
— If the E statement is true, the I statement is false. If the E statement is false, the I statement is true.

The same inference works in reverse: from the particular to the universal. If O is true, A is false; if O is false, A is true. If I is true, E is false; if I is false, E is true.

Why Contradictories Always Determine Each Other

The reason the contradictory relationship is the most reliable on the Square is rooted in the structure of the statements themselves. The A statement claims that every member of S belongs to P. The O statement claims that at least one member of S does not belong to P. If every S is P, then the "at least one S that is not P" claim is impossible — the O is false. If the O is true (there is at least one exception), then the A cannot be true — there is no "all" when there is an exception.

The same structure operates in the E–I pair. The E statement claims that no S belongs to P. The I statement claims that at least one S does belong to P. If no S is P, the I is false. If at least one S is P (I is true), the E is false.

This is not a coincidence of content. It is a logical feature of the opposition between "all" and "at least one not" — and between "none" and "at least one." Aristotle recognized this in On Interpretation when he analyzed the logical structure of universal and particular affirmation and denial. The contradictory relationship is the formal version of the most basic logical principle: a statement and its full negation cannot both hold.

Worked Example 1 — A–O Pair

Given (TRUE): "All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd numbers." (A statement)

What follows for O: "Some prime numbers greater than 2 are not odd numbers." — This is False. Since the A statement is true, its contradictory O must be false. There is no prime number greater than 2 that is not odd.

Check: Could both be true? No — "all are odd" and "some are not odd" cannot coexist. Could both be false? No — if not all are odd (A is false), then at least one is not odd (O is true). The exhaustive opposition holds.

Worked Example 2 — E–I Pair (from falsity)

Given (FALSE): "No mammals are warm-blooded creatures." (E statement) — This is false; many mammals are warm-blooded.

What follows for I: "Some mammals are warm-blooded creatures." — This is True. Since the E statement is false, its contradictory I must be true. The particular affirmative is established by the falsity of the corresponding universal negative.

Common Error — Confusing Contradictories with Contraries

A student argues: "Since 'All ravens are black' is false, 'No ravens are black' must be true."

Analysis: This is wrong. "No ravens are black" is the contrary of "All ravens are black" — not its contradictory. Contraries can both be false: it is false that all ravens are black (some are albinos), and it is also false that no ravens are black (most ravens are black). The contradictory of "All ravens are black" is "Some ravens are not black" — and that is indeed true, given that some albino ravens exist. The distinction matters: inferring from falsity of A to truth of E is an error; inferring from falsity of A to truth of O is valid.

Connection to Argument

The contradictory relationship is the formal basis of the most important move in argument: refutation by counterexample. When you offer a counterexample to a universal claim, you are asserting the truth of its contradictory O (or I) statement. "All swans are white" is refuted not by showing that "No swans are white" but by showing that "Some swans are not white." One black swan is enough — not because it destroys the whole category, but because it makes the O statement true, which immediately makes the A statement false.

This is why the O statement, despite being the least obvious of the four types, is logically crucial: it is the statement form in which counterexamples are expressed. Mastering the A–O contradictory pair gives you the formal structure behind every counterexample in logic and argumentation.

Practice

Part A — Identifying Contradictory Pairs. For each statement below, write its contradictory. Identify which pair it falls into (A–O or E–I).

  1. "All acts of deception are morally wrong acts."
  2. "Some historians are not scientists."
  3. "No valid argument has a false conclusion given true premises."
  4. "Some geometric figures are regular polygons."

Part B — Inference from Truth Values. The following A statement is known to be TRUE: "All Euclidean triangles are figures whose interior angles sum to 180 degrees." Use the contradictory relationship to determine the truth value of each related statement. State your reasoning in one sentence each.

  1. "Some Euclidean triangles are not figures whose interior angles sum to 180 degrees." (O statement)
  2. "No Euclidean triangles are figures whose interior angles sum to 180 degrees." (E statement — what relationship does this pair have?)
  3. "Some Euclidean triangles are figures whose interior angles sum to 180 degrees." (I statement — what relationship?)

Part C — From Falsity. The following O statement is known to be TRUE: "Some arguments with true premises are not sound arguments." What can you conclude about the corresponding A statement? State the conclusion and explain using the contradictory rule.

Part D — Application. A student argues: "It is false that all politicians are dishonest. Therefore, no politicians are dishonest." In 3–4 sentences, identify the error. Which relationship on the Square is the student misapplying, and which relationship would actually produce a valid inference from the same premise?

Looking Ahead: Lesson 3.04c examines two relationships on the Square that are less powerful than the contradictory: contraries (A–E) and subcontraries (I–O). Unlike contradictories, these relationships produce "undetermined" in some cases — which means the inference gives you partial information, not complete information. Understanding when a relationship does and does not determine a truth value is essential for using the Square correctly.
