Lesson 2.04b — Amphiboly, Composition, and Division

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the fallacies of amphiboly, composition, and division; explain why grammatical ambiguity and part-to-whole confusion undermine an argument's logical structure; distinguish these fallacies from one another and from equivocation; and construct a brief argument that avoids each error.

Orientation: Where We Are

Lesson 2.04a introduced equivocation — the fallacy that exploits a word carrying two different meanings across the premises of an argument. This lesson completes the category of fallacies of ambiguity by examining three more. Amphiboly shifts the source of the confusion from a word's meaning to the grammatical structure of an entire sentence. Composition and division shift it further still, from language to the relationship between parts and wholes. Together, all four fallacies in this category rest on the same underlying problem: an argument generates an appearance of valid inference only because something crucial remains unclear. Completing these three fallacies closes out Week 4 and the full treatment of relevance and ambiguity.

Intuitive Entry: Two Puzzles Before the Definitions

Puzzle One: What Does This Sentence Mean?

Read the following sentence carefully:

I saw the man on the hill with a telescope.

Who has the telescope? Did you use a telescope to see the man, or does the man on the hill have a telescope? The sentence itself does not decide the question. Both readings are grammatically possible, and neither requires changing a single word. The ambiguity comes not from any one word but from the way the phrases attach to each other. A sentence like this would be a poor foundation for an argument: any conclusion drawn from it might rest on one reading, while the reader follows the other.

Puzzle Two: Does the Part Guarantee the Whole?

Now consider a different kind of puzzle. Suppose every ingredient in a dish costs less than two dollars. Does that mean the dish itself is inexpensive? Perhaps — or perhaps not, depending on how many ingredients there are, how much labor is involved, and the rarity of the combination. The price of the parts does not simply add up to a conclusion about the whole in the same terms.

Now reverse it. Suppose a reference library is considered comprehensive. Does every single article in it have to be comprehensive? Not necessarily — the collection as a whole may be complete while individual entries are brief or selective. Properties of a whole do not automatically transfer to each part.

Both puzzles point to real inferential errors. The formal names and definitions follow.

Amphiboly (amphibolia)

The word comes from the Greek amphíbolos, meaning "thrown on both sides" or "striking both ways" — from amphi- ("on both sides") and bállein ("to throw"). An amphibolous expression strikes two targets at once: its grammatical structure allows two different readings without any change to the words themselves.

The fallacy of amphiboly occurs when an argument draws a conclusion that depends on one particular reading of a grammatically ambiguous sentence, while the sentence itself does not force that reading. The arguer exploits the ambiguity — whether deliberately or not — to make the inference appear stronger than it is. The remedy is to rewrite the sentence so that its grammatical structure admits only one reading, then re-examine whether the argument still holds.

Note the distinction from equivocation. Equivocation turns on a single word that carries two meanings (e.g., "bank" meaning a financial institution or a riverbank). Amphiboly turns on sentence structure: the individual words may be perfectly clear, but their arrangement leaves the meaning open.

Example 1 — Classic Grammar Puzzle

Sign posted in a store window: "Save soap and waste paper."

Analysis: The sentence can be read as a two-part instruction: "save soap" and "waste paper" (that is, be careless with paper). It can also be read as "save soap" and "save waste paper" (meaning, set aside used paper for recycling). The verb "save" may carry forward to the second phrase, or it may not. An argument that drew a conclusion about customers' intended behavior from this sign would be building on a grammatically ambiguous foundation. Neither reading is forced by the words alone.

Example 2 — Attachment Ambiguity

"The committee voted to approve the proposal quickly."

Analysis: Did the committee vote quickly, or did they vote that the proposal should be approved quickly? "Quickly" can modify the act of voting or the required pace of approval. An argument relying on this sentence to establish a deadline — or to establish only that the vote was brisk — would be exploiting structural ambiguity. Before drawing any inference, the sentence must be made unambiguous: "The committee voted quickly to approve the proposal" (brisk vote) versus "The committee voted to approve the proposal on an expedited schedule" (expedited approval).

Fallacy of Composition

The fallacy of composition occurs when someone infers that what is true of each individual part of a whole must therefore be true of the whole itself. The term comes from the Latin componere, "to put together." The error is in the putting-together: properties that belong to parts individually do not automatically belong to the assembled result.

The fallacy arises because there are two genuinely different senses in which something can be "true of" a collection. A property may hold distributively — it applies to each member taken one by one — or collectively — it applies to the group taken as a unit. Composition confuses these by treating a distributive truth as though it were also a collective truth.

Example 1 — The Sports Team

"Every player on this roster has been rated as an excellent individual performer. Therefore, the team as a whole must be excellent."

Analysis: Individual excellence does not guarantee collective excellence. Team performance depends on factors that exist only at the level of the whole: how players' skills complement or duplicate each other, how well they communicate under pressure, whether their tendencies create gaps on defense. A roster of outstanding individual players may play poorly together. The inference from each part to the whole fails.

Example 2 — Parts and Price

"Each component used to build this machine costs under ten dollars. Therefore, the machine itself must be inexpensive."

Analysis: The cost of a product at the whole level depends on factors not present at the level of individual parts: assembly labor, tooling, quality control, packaging, and the sheer number of components. A machine with two hundred ten-dollar components is a two-thousand-dollar machine before any of those additional costs are added. What is true of each part — low unit cost — does not automatically transfer to the product as a whole.

Non-Example: A Valid Part-to-Whole Inference

"Every atom in this sample is carbon. Therefore, this sample is entirely carbon."

Why this is valid: Some properties do transfer cleanly from parts to wholes. Chemical composition is one of them: the elemental identity of every constituent atom directly determines the elemental identity of the whole sample. When a property is definitionally constitutive of the whole — when the whole just is the sum of its parts in the relevant respect — the inference holds. The test is whether the property in question can belong to the whole only if it belongs to each part, and vice versa. When that condition is met, moving from parts to whole is legitimate, not fallacious.

Fallacy of Division

The fallacy of division is the reverse of composition. It occurs when someone infers that what is true of a whole must therefore be true of each of its parts. From the Latin dividere, "to divide" or "to separate." Where composition moves illegitimately from parts to whole, division moves illegitimately from whole to parts.

The logical structure is symmetrical to composition: collective properties do not automatically distribute to individual members. A whole can have a property that none of its parts has, or that only some of its parts have.

Example 1 — The Team Again

"This team has won the regional championship for three consecutive years. Therefore, every player on the team must be exceptionally skilled."

Analysis: A team's championship record is a collective property of the group. It may have been achieved because two or three dominant players elevated the rest, because the coaching strategy was unusually effective, or because weaker players filled supporting roles that compensated for their individual limitations. Championship success does not license the inference that every individual member is exceptional. Some may be average contributors carried by the overall system.

Example 2 — The Reference Work

"This encyclopedia is widely regarded as comprehensive. Therefore, every article in it must provide a comprehensive treatment of its topic."

Analysis: Comprehensiveness at the level of a reference work means it covers a wide range of topics — the collection leaves few subjects out. That is a property of the whole set of entries taken together. It says nothing about the depth or completeness of any individual entry. A comprehensive encyclopedia may include brief, introductory articles on thousands of topics. The inference from the whole's coverage to each part's depth commits the fallacy of division.

Non-Example: A Valid Whole-to-Part Inference

"This alloy is heavier than water. Therefore, any sample taken from this alloy will be heavier than water."

Why this is valid: Density is a property that holds uniformly across a homogeneous substance — any sample drawn from it will share the same density as the whole, because the relevant property is uniform by definition. When a property is uniform and structural rather than emergent or collective, inferring from whole to part is legitimate. The distinction is whether the property in question emerges from the combination (collective) or simply characterizes each constituent uniformly (distributive).

Connection to Argument Evaluation and Construction

All three fallacies in this lesson produce the same surface effect: an argument that looks like it follows because the reader does not immediately notice the shift. In amphiboly, the shift is between two readings of a grammatically ambiguous sentence. In composition and division, the shift is between two levels — part and whole — that behave differently with respect to the property being attributed.

When evaluating an argument, attending to these patterns means asking three diagnostic questions. First: is any premise built on a sentence that can be parsed in more than one way? If so, identify which reading the argument requires and ask whether the sentence forces that reading. Second: does any inference move from a property of individual parts to a conclusion about the whole? Third: does any inference move from a property of a whole to a conclusion about each part? If the answer to any of these is yes, examine whether the property in question is the kind that actually transfers between levels.

When constructing an argument, these fallacies mark three obligations. Write sentences with unambiguous grammatical structure, especially in premises. When making a claim about a whole, confirm that you are not relying on a property that belongs only to individual parts. When making a claim about a part, confirm that you are not deriving it from a property that belongs only to the whole. Meeting these obligations makes an argument harder to attack and easier to evaluate fairly.

Practice Problems

Recognition. Identify the fallacy — amphiboly, composition, or division — in each of the following. If there is no fallacy, say so and explain why the inference holds.

  1. "Visiting professors are rarely stimulating." This sentence appeared in a college newsletter. A student argued from it that professors who are visitors tend not to be stimulating. Another student argued from it that stimulating professors rarely visit. Which argument, if either, is entitled to use this sentence as a premise? What is the grammatical ambiguity?
  2. "Each sentence in this paragraph is grammatically correct. Therefore, the paragraph as a whole is well-written." Identify the fallacy and explain which specific property fails to transfer.
  3. "This bridge is structurally sound as a whole. Therefore, each individual steel cable supporting it is structurally sound." Identify the fallacy.
  4. "Every brick in this wall weighs approximately five kilograms. Therefore, this wall weighs more than one thousand kilograms." Is this an instance of a fallacy? Explain your answer carefully.

Analysis.

  1. Explain in your own words the distinction between a distributive property and a collective property. Give one original example of each. Then explain why this distinction is the key to understanding both composition and division.
  2. A student says: "Composition and division are just opposites of each other — they're the same mistake in two directions." Write a 3–5 sentence response that affirms what is correct in this observation while also identifying what the student may be missing about how the two fallacies differ in practice.

Construction.

  1. Write an argument of your own that commits the fallacy of amphiboly. Include the ambiguous sentence, the conclusion drawn, and an explanation of which grammatical reading the argument requires. Then rewrite the sentence to eliminate the ambiguity and state whether the revised argument remains convincing.
  2. Write one argument committing the fallacy of composition and one committing the fallacy of division, each on a topic of your choosing. For each, identify the property being attributed, explain why it does not transfer between levels, and revise the argument so that it draws only what the premises actually support.
Looking Ahead: Week 5 turns to a third category of informal fallacies: fallacies of presumption. Where fallacies of relevance offered premises that were beside the point and fallacies of ambiguity exploited unclear language, fallacies of presumption take for granted something that still needs to be established. The first lesson will examine the concept of the warrant — the implicit assumption that licenses every inference — and what goes wrong when that assumption is unwarranted.
