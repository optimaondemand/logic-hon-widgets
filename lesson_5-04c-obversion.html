Lesson 5.04c
Obversion
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.3 — Analyze an author's choices in establishing and advancing a point of view; ELA.11.C.1.4 — Write arguments to support claims using valid reasoning; ELA.K12.EE.2.1 — Read and comprehend grade-level complex texts proficiently

Learning Objective: Students will be able to apply the operation of obversion to all four categorical statement types, correctly form the obverse of any given statement, and explain why obversion is always valid regardless of statement type.

Orientation

Conversion, which you studied in the previous lesson, is valid for only three of the four statement types — and A requires a weakened result. Obversion is more generous: it applies validly to all four types without weakening the conclusion. The operation is slightly more complex, involving two simultaneous changes to the original statement, but the result always has exactly the same truth value as the original. Understanding why requires a closer look at the logical role of term complements.

Intuitive Entry Point

In ordinary language we regularly express the same idea in both positive and negative forms. "All the lights are on" and "None of the lights are off" mean exactly the same thing — they describe the same situation from opposite angles. "Some of the containers are full" is equivalent to "Some of the containers are not empty." These are not rephrasing tricks; they reflect a genuine logical equivalence: affirming membership in a class is logically the same as denying membership in that class's complement. Obversion formalizes this equivalence as a precise operation.

The Complement of a Term

Before stating the rule, a critical concept: the complement of a term is the class of all things that are not in that class. The complement of "mammals" is "non-mammals" — everything in the universe of discourse that is not a mammal. The complement of "red objects" is "non-red objects." In formal notation, the complement of term S is written non-S.

Two important features of complements: (1) every object belongs to either a class or its complement, never both; (2) affirming that something belongs to class P is logically equivalent to denying that it belongs to non-P. It is these equivalences that make obversion valid.

The Obversion Rule

Obversion forms the obverse of a categorical statement by making two simultaneous changes:

  1. Change the quality — affirmative becomes negative, negative becomes affirmative (change "are" to "are not," or vice versa).
  2. Replace the predicate with its complement — substitute non-P for P, or P for non-P.

The subject term and the quantifier remain unchanged. The result always has the same truth value as the original. The word obversion comes from the Latin ob- (facing, against) + vertere (to turn) — it is a "turning to face" the complement.

Type Original Obverse Type of Obverse
A All S are P No S are non-P E
E No S are P All S are non-P A
I Some S are P Some S are not non-P O
O Some S are not P Some S are non-P I

Notice that obversion always changes the statement type in a systematic way: A↔E and I↔O. This is because changing quality toggles between affirmative and negative types, and the complementing of the predicate ensures the logical content is preserved.

Why Obversion Is Always Valid

The validity of obversion rests on the logical equivalence between "x is P" and "x is not non-P." To say that something belongs to class P is exactly to say it does not belong to non-P (the complement of P). No new claim is made; only the direction from which the claim is expressed has changed. Because this equivalence holds for any class P and any object x, it holds for all four statement types without exception and without requiring any weakening of the result.

This is a significant contrast with conversion: conversion fails for A and O statements because swapping subject and predicate can overstate what was claimed. Obversion never overstates because it works entirely within the resources already present in the original statement — it merely toggles the perspective from which the same fact is described.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — A Statement Obversion

Original (A): "All planets in our solar system are bodies that orbit the sun."

Obverse (E): "No planets in our solar system are not things that orbit the sun."

Analysis: Quality changed (affirmative → negative, "All...are" → "No...are"). The predicate has been replaced with its complement, formed correctly as a single compound unit: not "bodies that do not orbit the sun" (which would still assume the thing is a body, just one that fails to orbit) but "not things that orbit the sun" (everything that fails to belong to the entire category "things that orbit the sun," whether or not it is even a body). Subject and quantifier unchanged. The two statements say exactly the same thing and have the same truth value.

Example 2 — E Statement Obversion

Original (E): "No mammals are cold-blooded animals."

Obverse (A): "All mammals are not things that are cold-blooded animals."

Analysis: Quality changed (negative → affirmative, "No...are" → "All...are"). The predicate is complemented as a whole compound category — "not things that are cold-blooded animals" — rather than as "non-cold-blooded animals," which risks being misread as a narrower, already-classified category ("warm-blooded animals") instead of the full complement of everything that fails to be a cold-blooded animal. The obverse says the same thing: every mammal falls outside the entire class of cold-blooded animals.

Example 3 — O Statement Obversion

Original (O): "Some ancient bridges are not structures that remain standing."

Obverse (I): "Some ancient bridges are not structures that remain standing."

Analysis: Quality changed (negative → affirmative, "are not" as a negative copula → "are" as an affirmative copula). Read carefully: the word "are" here is affirmative, and "not structures that remain standing" is the complement of the predicate taken as a single compound unit — not "structures that do not remain standing" (which would still assume the thing is a structure, just one that fails to remain standing). Because the complement phrase itself begins with "not," the obverse's surface wording matches the original's wording, even though the logical role of "are" has changed from negative copula to affirmative copula attached to a negated predicate-class. This is precisely the kind of case where complementing a compound category can look deceptively like no change has occurred at all — which is exactly why checking the logical structure, not just the surface wording, matters.

Common Error — Changing the Quantifier

The most frequent error in obversion is also changing the quantifier ("All" to "Some" or vice versa). The quantifier must remain unchanged — obversion modifies only the quality and the predicate. "All S are P" obverts to "No S are non-P," not to "Some S are non-P." Changing the quantifier turns obversion into something else entirely and destroys the logical equivalence.

Common Error — Complementing a Compound Category Incorrectly

When the predicate is a compound category — a phrase that names a class by combining a noun with a modifying clause, such as "animals with backbones" or "structures that remain standing" — it is tempting to form the complement by negating only the modifying clause and leaving the head noun untouched. This is a mistake. The complement of "animals with backbones" is not "animals without backbones" (which still assumes the thing is an animal); it is "things that are not animals with backbones" (which includes every animal without a backbone, but also every non-animal in the universe of discourse — rocks, numbers, ideas, anything at all that fails to be a backboned animal).

The same error appears with any compound predicate: the complement of "bodies that orbit the sun" is not "bodies that do not orbit the sun" but "things that are not bodies that orbit the sun"; the complement of "structures that remain standing" is not "structures that do not remain standing" but "things that are not structures that remain standing." In each case, negating only the internal clause produces a narrower class than the true complement, because it silently keeps the assumption that the thing still belongs to the head-noun category. The correct complement negates the entire compound category as one unit.

Connection to Argument

Obversion is useful in two ways. First, it allows you to restate a premise in a logically equivalent but linguistically more convenient form for combining with other premises in a syllogism. Second, it is one of the steps in the more complex operation of contraposition, which you will study in the next lesson. Recognizing obversion in ordinary arguments helps you see when two statements that look different are actually saying the same thing — a useful diagnostic when evaluating whether an arguer has smuggled in an unstated assumption or whether two apparently different premises are actually redundant.

Practice Problems

Part A — Apply Obversion. For each statement, state its type, form the obverse, and state the obverse's type.

  1. "All vertebrates are animals with backbones."
  2. "No prime numbers are composite numbers."
  3. "Some historical treaties are agreements that were honored."
  4. "Some volcanic eruptions are not events that produced lava flows."
  5. "All equilateral figures are figures with equal sides."
  6. "No endothermic reactions are reactions that release heat."

Part B — Evaluate the Obversion. Each item shows an original statement and a claimed obverse. State whether it is correctly formed. If not, identify the error.

  1. Original: "All ferns are vascular plants." Claimed obverse: "Some ferns are not non-vascular plants."
  2. Original: "No amphibians are warm-blooded." Claimed obverse: "All amphibians are non-warm-blooded."
  3. Original: "Some geometric proofs are indirect proofs." Claimed obverse: "No geometric proofs are non-indirect proofs."
  4. Original: "Some sedimentary rocks are not fossils." Claimed obverse: "Some sedimentary rocks are non-fossils."

Part C — Construction and Comparison. Write one original statement of each type (A, E, I, O). For each, perform both conversion (where valid) and obversion, showing the result of each operation. In 2–3 sentences, explain how the two operations differ in what they change about the original statement.

Looking Ahead: Contraposition, the final operation in this week's sequence, combines conversion and obversion into a two-step procedure. Like obversion, it preserves the truth value of the original — but only for A and O statements. Understanding why requires you to apply everything from the past two lessons in a single operation.
