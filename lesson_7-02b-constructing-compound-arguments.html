Lesson 7.02b — Constructing Compound Arguments

Learning objective: Students will construct compound arguments by linking two or three valid syllogisms so that the conclusion of each supports a premise of the next, producing a multi-step argument for a philosophical position with a correctly structured argument tree.

Orientation

Lesson 7.02a built a single valid syllogism for a conclusion. Most philosophical positions worth arguing for, however, require more than one inferential step. The conclusion of one syllogism must be established as a premise of the next. This lesson extends the construction procedure to multi-syllogism compound arguments — the logical structure your Final Essay requires.

Intuitive Entry Point

When a lawyer makes a case, they rarely establish everything in a single inference. First they establish that a law applies to this type of action. Then they establish that the defendant performed this type of action. Then they draw the legal conclusion. Each step supports the next. The chain of reasoning is deliberate: no step is taken for granted, and each rests on what came before. Constructing a compound argument is learning to plan and execute that kind of deliberate inferential chain.

Design Principles for Compound Arguments

A compound argument is a set of valid syllogisms in which the conclusion of at least one syllogism serves as a premise of another. The main argument is the syllogism whose conclusion is the position you are establishing. Sub-arguments are syllogisms whose conclusions serve as premises in the main argument or in other sub-arguments.

Three design principles govern the construction of sound compound arguments:

  1. Each component syllogism must be independently valid. Validity does not transfer across linked syllogisms — each must pass the five rules on its own. A chain of two valid syllogisms is valid throughout; a chain where one link is invalid fails at that point regardless of what precedes or follows it.
  2. The link terms must be identical across syllogisms. When the conclusion of Syllogism 1 becomes a premise of Syllogism 2, the terms used must carry exactly the same meaning. If the term shifts in meaning between syllogisms, equivocation infects the chain.
  3. The strongest premise in each syllogism should be the one that is most defensible, not just the one that is most useful. A compound argument is only as strong as its most contestable premise, and constructing well means choosing premises that can be defended, not just premises that produce the needed conclusion.

Worked Example

Position: "Formal logic is indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry."

Main Argument (Target Conclusion): "Formal logic is indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry."

In categorical form: "All systems of formal logic are things indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry." (A statement; S = systems of formal logic; P = things indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry.)

Planning the Chain: To establish this, I need a middle term that connects formal logic to rigorous philosophical inquiry. Let M1 = "tools that guarantee valid inference." Then:

  • Sub-Argument 1 establishes: "All systems of formal logic are tools that guarantee valid inference." (This becomes the minor premise of the main argument.)
  • Main Argument uses: Major premise (A): "All tools that guarantee valid inference are things indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry." Minor premise (A, from Sub-Argument 1): "All systems of formal logic are tools that guarantee valid inference." Conclusion (A): "All systems of formal logic are things indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry." Mood-figure: AAA-1 (Barbara). Valid. ✓

Sub-Argument 1:
Major (A): "All systems of inference that eliminate formal ambiguity are tools that guarantee valid inference."
Minor (A): "All systems of formal logic are systems of inference that eliminate formal ambiguity."
Conclusion (A): "All systems of formal logic are tools that guarantee valid inference." (Barbara again) ✓

Argument Tree:

  • MC: All systems of formal logic are indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry.
  • ↑ [Main MP1 + Main MP2] (linked)
  • Main MP1 (major): All tools that guarantee valid inference are indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry.
  • Main MP2 = IC from Sub-Arg 1: All systems of formal logic are tools that guarantee valid inference.
  • ↑ [SP1 + SP2] (linked)
  • SP1: All systems that eliminate formal ambiguity are tools that guarantee valid inference.
  • SP2: All systems of formal logic are systems that eliminate formal ambiguity.

Most contestable premise: Main MP1 (the major premise of the main argument) — the claim that anything guaranteeing valid inference is thereby indispensable to philosophy is open to challenge. One might argue that philosophical inquiry can achieve rigor by other means.

Common Error — Term Drift Between Syllogisms

When a conclusion becomes a premise in the next syllogism, students sometimes restate it in slightly different words, changing the term in the process. If Sub-Argument 1 concludes "All formal logic systems are tools that guarantee valid inference" and the main argument's minor premise reads "All formal logic systems are reliable tools," the meaning of the middle term has shifted — "tools that guarantee valid inference" is not identical to "reliable tools." This is equivocation at the junction between syllogisms, and it is among the hardest errors to catch in compound argument construction.

Connection to Argument

The compound argument you construct in this lesson is, in skeletal form, the logical structure of your Final Essay. The essay requires three interlocking valid categorical syllogisms. The procedure here — plan the chain, verify each link, check for term drift at junctions, identify the most contestable premise — is the procedure for designing the essay's argumentative skeleton before writing a word of prose.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Recognition). The following two syllogisms are intended to form a chain. Identify whether they link correctly — that is, whether the conclusion of Syllogism 1 is identical (in meaning and form) to a premise of Syllogism 2. If not, describe the term drift.

Syllogism 1: All valid arguments preserve truth. All first-figure syllogisms are valid arguments. Therefore, all first-figure syllogisms preserve truth.

Syllogism 2: All truth-preserving forms of reasoning are reliable. All first-figure syllogisms are truth-preserving forms of reasoning. Therefore, all first-figure syllogisms are reliable.

Problem 2 (Analysis). Evaluate the following compound argument. Identify each syllogism, state its mood and figure, verify its validity with the five rules, and check for term drift at the junction between them.

Sub-argument: No merely habitual behaviors are freely chosen. All instinctive actions are merely habitual behaviors. Therefore, no instinctive actions are freely chosen.

Main argument: No freely chosen actions are beyond moral evaluation. No instinctive actions are freely chosen. Therefore, no instinctive actions are beyond moral evaluation.
