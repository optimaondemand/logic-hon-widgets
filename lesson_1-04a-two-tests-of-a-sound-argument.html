Lesson 1.04a
The Two Tests of a Sound Argument
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments; evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to apply the two tests of a sound argument — validity and truth of premises — and explain why both are required for an argument to fully succeed.

Orientation: Where We Are

We now have names for the three types of argument, and we know that deductive arguments make the strongest possible claim about their conclusions: the conclusion is supposed to follow necessarily from the premises, not merely probably. But knowing that an argument is deductive is not the same as knowing it succeeds. A deductive argument can be structured correctly and still fail — because its premises are false. Today we introduce the two-part test that every successful deductive argument must pass, and give the concept of soundness its precise definition.

Intuitive Entry: The Bridge Analogy

Suppose a builder constructs a bridge. Two distinct things can go wrong: the design could be structurally flawed (the blueprint is incorrect), or the materials could be defective (the steel is substandard). A bridge fails if either the design or the materials fail. A perfect blueprint built with bad steel is as dangerous as a faulty blueprint built with good steel. Both the form and the content must hold.

Arguments work the same way. The structure of an argument — its logical form — can be correct, but if the content of the premises is false, you still end up somewhere you should not be. Conversely, accurate premises do not rescue an argument with a broken structure; the conclusion still does not follow. Both tests must be passed. This lesson names those two tests precisely.

Test 1: Validity

The first test is validity, which you studied in Lesson 1.03a. A deductive argument is valid if and only if it is impossible for all the premises to be true while the conclusion is false. Validity is a structural property: it answers the question "does the conclusion follow from the premises?" without asking whether the premises are actually true.

This test evaluates the form of the argument. Think of it as checking the blueprint. The blueprint can be perfect even if the building is never built, and even if the materials described in it don't exist. Validity can be assessed from the form alone.

Test 2: True Premises

The second test is simpler to state: all the premises must actually be true. Not assumed true for the sake of argument, not true in some hypothetical scenario — actually true in the world. A valid argument built on false premises does not give you a reliable conclusion. You are following a correct blueprint with defective materials.

Truth of premises is typically an empirical or domain-specific question. Whether all mammals have hearts is a question for biology. Whether penguins are birds is a question for ornithology. Whether Caesar crossed the Rubicon is a question for history. Formal logic does not settle these questions; it tells you what to do with them once you have them.

Soundness: Passing Both Tests

An argument is sound if and only if it passes both tests: it is valid, and all of its premises are true. A sound argument guarantees a true conclusion. This is the strongest possible result in deductive logic.

The word "sound" comes from Old English gesund, meaning "healthy" or "whole." A sound argument is whole in both dimensions: its form is correct and its content is accurate. Remove either and the wholeness breaks.

A key consequence worth stating clearly: if an argument is sound, its conclusion must be true. This is not because you checked the conclusion directly; it is because a valid form guarantees that true premises produce a true conclusion, and the premises are in fact true. The truth of the conclusion is a result, not a starting point.

The Four Possible Combinations

Valid? Premises True? Sound? What we can say about the conclusion
Yes Yes Yes Guaranteed to be true
Yes No (at least one false) No Form is correct; conclusion may or may not be true
No Yes No Premises may be accurate, but the conclusion does not follow from them
No No No Both form and content fail

Study this table. The most important row for building intuition is the second: a valid argument with false premises is not sound, and its conclusion might be true or might be false — the form alone cannot tell you. The form only tells you what would happen if the premises were true. If they are not, you are on your own.

Why Formal Logic Focuses on Validity

Given that soundness requires both tests, why does formal logic spend most of its energy on validity? Because validity can be assessed without knowing whether the premises are actually true. We can evaluate the form of an argument about events on another planet, about hypothetical scenarios, or about mathematical objects, without any access to empirical facts. The form is an object of pure reasoning.

Truth of premises, by contrast, requires knowledge of the subject matter. Whether a historical claim is true is a question for a historian; whether a biological generalization holds is a question for a biologist. Formal logic is not equipped to settle those questions, nor does it try to. It studies the relationship between premises and conclusion — the structural dimension — and leaves the factual dimension to the relevant disciplines.

This is why a student of logic can evaluate an argument's validity without being an expert in whatever the argument is about. The structure is separable from the subject matter, and that separability is what makes logic a discipline of its own.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Valid and Sound

P1: All mammals have hearts.
P2: Dogs are mammals.
C: Therefore, dogs have hearts.

Valid? Yes — the form guarantees the conclusion given the premises. Premises true? Yes — both are accurate biological facts. Sound? Yes. The conclusion is guaranteed to be true.

Example 2 — Valid but Not Sound

P1: All birds can fly.
P2: Penguins are birds.
C: Therefore, penguins can fly.

Valid? Yes — IF the premises were true, the conclusion would have to follow. Premises true? No — P1 is false; not all birds can fly. Sound? No. The conclusion is false. This example is important: a valid argument can produce a false conclusion if a premise is false. Validity alone does not save you.

Example 3 — Premises True, but Invalid

P1: Some mammals live in water.
P2: Dolphins live in water.
C: Therefore, dolphins are mammals.

Premises true? Yes — both statements are accurate. Valid? No — the conclusion does not follow from these premises. Many non-mammals also live in water (fish, for instance). The fact that dolphins live in water, combined with the fact that some water-dwellers are mammals, does not guarantee that dolphins are mammals. The conclusion happens to be true, but not because of this argument. Sound? No — soundness requires validity, and this argument lacks it.

Example 4 — Invalid and Premises Not All True

P1: All insects have six legs.
P2: Spiders have six legs.
C: Therefore, spiders are insects.

Premises true? P1 is true; P2 is false (spiders have eight legs). Valid? No — even if both premises were true, the form would be invalid (the same structure as Example 3). Sound? No on both counts. This argument fails both tests independently.

Common Error — Evaluating Soundness by Looking at the Conclusion

Students often say an argument is "sound" because the conclusion is obviously true, or "unsound" because the conclusion seems wrong. This reverses the correct procedure.

Soundness is assessed by examining the form (validity) and the premises (truth), not by examining the conclusion directly. The conclusion of a sound argument will always be true — but you arrive at that fact by checking the form and the premises, not by starting with the conclusion and working backward. Starting with the conclusion tells you nothing about whether the argument that produced it is any good. A true conclusion can appear at the end of a terrible argument.

Two Lines of Attack on Any Deductive Argument

The two-test framework gives you two distinct ways to challenge any deductive argument you encounter — in a text, a debate, a legal brief, a philosophical dialogue.

The first attack: show the argument is invalid. Demonstrate that the form does not guarantee the conclusion — that it is possible to accept all the premises and still deny the conclusion without contradiction. This is a structural objection. It does not require you to dispute any factual claim; it requires you to show that the logical machinery does not work.

The second attack: show that at least one premise is false. Even if the form is impeccable, a false premise breaks the argument. This is a factual objection, and it requires knowledge of the relevant subject matter.

These two attacks are independent. An argument can be vulnerable to one but not the other, or to both. Recognizing which attack applies — and which you are in a position to mount — is the practical skill that follows from understanding the two-test framework.

Practice Problems

Recognition

  1. For each argument below, identify (i) whether it is valid or invalid, (ii) whether the premises appear true or false, and (iii) whether it is sound.
    • (a) P1: All squares have four sides. P2: This shape is a square. C: This shape has four sides.
    • (b) P1: All fish breathe through lungs. P2: Trout are fish. C: Trout breathe through lungs.
    • (c) P1: Some students pass every exam. P2: Jordan is a student. C: Jordan passes every exam.

Analysis

  1. A classmate says: "This argument must be sound — I know the conclusion is definitely true." What is wrong with this reasoning? Construct a counterexample: an argument where the conclusion is true but the argument is not sound.
  2. Why can we evaluate validity without knowing whether the premises are true? What does this tell us about the nature of formal logic as a discipline, compared to, say, biology or history?

Construction

  1. Construct one sound argument. It must be valid and have premises that are actually true. Label your premises and conclusion, and confirm explicitly that each condition for soundness is met.
  2. Construct one argument that is valid but unsound. Identify which premise is false, and explain what it would take to make the argument sound. (Note: you would need to replace the false premise with a true one — you cannot make a valid argument sound simply by adjusting the form.)
