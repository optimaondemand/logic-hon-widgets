Lesson 6.01a
What Is an Enthymeme?
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.3.3 — Analyze and evaluate the effectiveness of an author's argument; ELA.11.R.2.3 — Analyze an author's choices in establishing and advancing a point of view; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to define an enthymeme, identify its three orders (missing major premise, missing minor premise, missing conclusion), recognize enthymemes in ordinary language, and explain why enthymematic reasoning is the dominant form of argument in real discourse.

Orientation

Every syllogism you have studied so far has been presented in complete standard form — three statements, three terms, all elements explicit. Real arguments almost never look like this. In actual philosophical texts, scientific writing, legal reasoning, and everyday discourse, arguments are routinely stated with one element left out — assumed as so obvious that stating it would seem pedantic, or left implicit as shared common ground between speaker and audience. These incomplete arguments are called enthymemes, and learning to work with them is the bridge between formal logic as a discipline and logic as a tool for understanding real arguments.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider a conversation between two students after an exam. One says: "You must have studied — your score is excellent." The other immediately understands. But the argument was never made explicit. What was left out? The unstated premise: "Anyone who earns an excellent score studied." The stated premise is the conclusion's evidence: "Your score is excellent." The conclusion: "You studied." The full syllogism is there, but only one premise and the conclusion were spoken aloud — the other premise was simply assumed. This is an enthymeme. You encounter dozens of them every day, in every register of language, without noticing. The logical tradition gives this ubiquitous form a name and a method.

Definition and Etymology

An enthymeme (from the Greek enthumema, from en, "in," + thymos, "mind" or "heart") is a syllogism in which one element — a premise or the conclusion — is left unstated, held "in the mind" rather than expressed. The word captures the idea that part of the argument is carried implicitly by the speaker and audience rather than articulated explicitly.

Aristotle discussed enthymemes primarily in the Rhetoric, where he called them "the rhetorical syllogism" — the kind of argument appropriate for public discourse, where audiences do not require or want formal demonstrations, and where shared assumptions can be taken for granted. In the Prior Analytics, he also noted that syllogisms with suppressed premises are common in philosophical argument. The Scholastic tradition formalized the three orders of enthymeme into the classification still used today.

The Three Orders of Enthymeme

Order What Is Missing What Is Stated Example
First Order Major premise Minor premise + conclusion "Socrates is mortal, because he is a man." (Missing: "All men are mortal.")
Second Order Minor premise Major premise + conclusion "All humans are mortal, so Socrates will die." (Missing: "Socrates is a human.")
Third Order Conclusion Both premises "All humans are mortal, and Socrates is human." (Conclusion "Socrates is mortal" implied but not stated.)

The first order is by far the most common in ordinary discourse. People state why they believe something (the minor premise) and what they conclude, leaving the general principle (the major premise) implicit. The second order appears frequently in scientific writing, where the general principle is established first and the inference to a particular case is made without explicitly identifying the case as falling under the principle. The third order — where the conclusion is left for the audience to draw — is a rhetorical device, inviting the listener to complete the reasoning themselves.

Why Enthymemes Dominate Real Discourse

Fully explicit syllogisms are appropriate in formal demonstration — mathematical proofs, legal contracts, logical textbooks. In ordinary argument, they are rare for good reasons. First, many premises are so widely shared that stating them would insult the audience's intelligence or slow the argument unnecessarily. Second, rhetorical effectiveness often depends on allowing the audience to participate in the reasoning — an audience that draws a conclusion themselves is more persuaded than one that is merely told the conclusion. Third, the full syllogistic form requires translating natural language into categorical statements, which is a skill most speakers do not consciously deploy.

None of this means enthymemes are inferior to full syllogisms. A valid enthymeme — one whose suppressed premise, if stated, would produce a valid and sound syllogism — is a perfectly good argument. The problem comes when the suppressed premise is false, contestable, or when the argument would be invalid if fully stated. Making arguments explicit is how we detect these problems.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — First Order Enthymeme

Stated: "This mineral is a metal, so it conducts electricity."

Explicit elements: Minor premise: "This mineral is a metal." Conclusion: "This mineral conducts electricity."

Missing major premise: "All metals conduct electricity."

Full syllogism: All metals conduct electricity. This mineral is a metal. Therefore, this mineral conducts electricity. (Valid — Barbara, AAA-1.)

Example 2 — Second Order Enthymeme

Stated: "No valid syllogism has two negative premises, so this argument is invalid."

Explicit elements: Major premise: "No valid syllogism has two negative premises." Conclusion: "This argument is invalid."

Missing minor premise: "This argument has two negative premises."

Full syllogism: No valid syllogism has two negative premises. This argument has two negative premises. Therefore, this argument is not a valid syllogism. (Valid — Celarent, EAE-1.)

The Problem of the False Suppressed Premise

Enthymemes can be deceptive precisely because the missing premise is never examined. "He must be wealthy — he drives an expensive car" suppresses the premise "Everyone who drives an expensive car is wealthy," which is false. The argument sounds reasonable because the conclusion is plausible and only one step away from the stated evidence. Making the missing premise explicit immediately exposes the weakness. This is the practical value of enthymeme reconstruction: it forces hidden assumptions into the open where they can be evaluated.

Connection to Argument

Identifying enthymemes is one of the most practically valuable skills in this course. Most arguments you encounter in academic reading, public discourse, and professional life are enthymematic. The ability to ask "What is the unstated premise here?" and then evaluate that premise is what distinguishes a careful thinker from one who is merely persuaded by the surface form of an argument. The remaining lessons in this week build the technique for supplying the missing element and reconstructing the full argument — the foundation for all the argument analysis work in Weeks 8 and 9.

Practice Problems

Part A — Identify the Order. For each enthymeme, identify which order it is (first, second, or third) and state which element is missing.

  1. "Carbon is nonmetallic, so it doesn't conduct electricity well."
  2. "All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd. Therefore, 17 is odd."
  3. "Sedimentary rocks are formed by compaction of particles, and limestone is a sedimentary rock."
  4. "The specimen must be a mammal — it was observed nursing its young."
  5. "No valid syllogism violates the distribution rules; therefore this argument is valid."

Part B — Supply the Missing Element. For each enthymeme, supply the most plausible missing premise or conclusion as a categorical statement in standard form. Then write the complete syllogism.

  1. "Aristotle is a philosopher, so he valued careful reasoning."
  2. "All equilateral triangles have equal angles. Therefore, triangle ABC has equal angles."
  3. "No actions performed under genuine compulsion are truly voluntary, and he acted under compulsion."

Part C — Reflection. In 3–4 sentences, explain why the first order of enthymeme (missing major premise) is the most common in ordinary discourse. What feature of ordinary conversation or argument makes speakers most likely to leave the general principle unstated?

Looking Ahead: Now that you can identify an enthymeme and determine which element is missing, the next lesson develops the techniques for supplying that missing element correctly — particularly how to identify the missing premise charitably and how to find a hidden middle term when the argument's terms are disguised by paraphrase.
