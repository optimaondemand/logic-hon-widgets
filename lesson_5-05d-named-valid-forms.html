Lesson 5.05d
Named Valid Forms: Barbara, Celarent, and the Tradition
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze the structure an author uses to organize a text; ELA.11.C.1.4 — Write arguments to support claims using valid reasoning; ELA.12.C.1.2 — Write arguments using logical reasoning; ELA.K12.EE.2.1 — Read and comprehend grade-level complex texts proficiently

Learning Objective: Students will be able to recognize the traditional names of the valid syllogistic forms, decode the vowel-mnemonic system that encodes mood within each name, and correctly apply the Figure 1 names (Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio) and the major Figure 2 names to identify syllogisms encountered in analysis.

Orientation

The previous lesson established which mood-figure combinations are valid and explained why through the five rules. This lesson introduces the names by which those valid forms have been known in the Western logical tradition for nearly a thousand years. These names are not mere historical curiosities — they are still used by logicians, philosophers, and scholars to refer to specific syllogistic forms with precision and economy. Knowing them gives you access to a rich tradition of logical analysis and allows you to communicate about argument structure with a shared vocabulary that stretches from the medieval Scholastics to contemporary philosophy of logic.

Intuitive Entry Point

Mathematicians name important theorems and operations — the Pythagorean theorem, Euler's formula, Gaussian elimination — partly for historical honor and partly because a name is more useful than a description. Rather than saying "the relationship between the square of the hypotenuse and the squares of the two legs of a right triangle," you say "Pythagorean theorem." Logic does the same. Rather than saying "the valid first-figure syllogism with mood AAA," logicians say Barbara. The name is a compressed, memorable label for a well-defined logical form — and because the vowels in the name encode the mood, the name also functions as a partial description of what the form looks like.

The Mnemonic System: How Names Encode Mood

The traditional names were composed by medieval logicians — the precise attribution is uncertain, but the system appears fully formed in texts from the 13th century, including works associated with the Scholastic tradition that Aristotle's logic passed through via Boethius and later commentators. The system works as follows:

  • The vowels in the name encode the mood, in order: first vowel = major premise type; second vowel = minor premise type; third vowel = conclusion type. (A, E, I, O correspond exactly to the statement types.)
  • The initial consonant of names outside Figure 1 indicates which Figure 1 form the syllogism can be reduced to.
  • The other consonants encode what operations (conversion, obversion) are needed to perform the reduction — though students of this course are not expected to memorize the full reduction scheme.

For practical purposes, what matters most is the vowels. Barbara: A-A-A = mood AAA. Celarent: E-A-E = mood EAE. Ferio: E-I-O = mood EIO. The name is a compressed code.

Note that EIO appears as a valid form in three of the four figures — it is the most widely valid mood combination in this course's list, valid across most positions of the middle term. Barbara (AAA-1) is the paradigm case of categorical inference: "the most perfect syllogism" in Aristotle's characterization, because its validity is immediately evident from the meaning of universal affirmation.

Two traditional Figure 3 and 4 names — Darapti (AAI-3) and its Figure 4 counterpart Bramantip (AAI-4), along with Felapton (EAO-3) and its counterpart Fesapo (EAO-4) — are omitted from this list. As Lesson 5.05c explains, these four forms draw a weakened particular conclusion from two universal premises, which requires an additional existence assumption beyond what this course grants by default, so they are not included among the valid forms here.

Priority for This Course

You are expected to know the Figure 1 forms — Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio — by name and mood, and to recognize the Figure 2 forms — Cesare, Camestres, Festino, Baroco — when encountered. The Figure 3 and 4 names are part of the tradition you should recognize; you are not expected to produce them from memory without reference. What matters in all cases is your ability to decode the vowels and verify the mood-figure pair.

Worked Examples: Applying the Names

Identifying Barbara
All vertebrates are animals with a spinal column.
All fish are vertebrates.
Therefore, all fish are animals with a spinal column.

Mood: AAA. Figure: M (vertebrates) is subject of major premise, predicate of minor premise — Figure 1. Barbara (AAA-1). Decode: Barbara → A, A, A. ✓

Identifying Celarent
No inert gases are reactive elements.
All noble gases are inert gases.
Therefore, no noble gases are reactive elements.

Mood: EAE. Figure: M (inert gases) is subject of major, predicate of minor — Figure 1. Celarent (EAE-1). Decode: Celarent → E, A, E. ✓

Identifying Cesare (Figure 2)
No mammals are cold-blooded animals.
All reptiles are cold-blooded animals.
Therefore, no reptiles are mammals.

Conclusion: "No reptiles are mammals." S = reptiles; P = mammals; M = cold-blooded animals. M is predicate of both premises — Figure 2. Mood: E, A, E = EAE. Cesare (EAE-2). Decode: Cesare → E, A, E. ✓

Connection to Argument

The named forms are part of the shared vocabulary of Western intellectual culture. Aquinas, Leibniz, and Kant all reasoned explicitly in terms of syllogistic forms. When Kant distinguishes kinds of inference in the Critique of Pure Reason, he draws on the same Aristotelian framework these names represent. Recognizing a Barbara or a Ferio in a philosophical or scientific text is like recognizing a sonata form in a musical score — it locates the argument in a structural tradition and immediately clarifies what kind of logical work it is doing.

Practice Problems

Part A — Name the Form. For each valid syllogism, state the mood-figure pair and its traditional name (if it has one from the list above).

  1. All deciduous trees lose their leaves. Some trees in the park are deciduous trees. Therefore, some trees in the park lose their leaves.
  2. No amphibians are fully aquatic in their adult stage. All frogs are amphibians. Therefore, no frogs are fully aquatic in their adult stage.
  3. No mammals are cold-blooded. All whales are mammals. Therefore, no whales are cold-blooded.
  4. All equilateral triangles are equiangular. Some triangles in the figure are equilateral. Therefore, some triangles in the figure are equiangular.

Part B — Decode the Name. For each traditional name below, state the mood it encodes (using only the vowels) and the figure indicated. Then write one original syllogism instantiating that form.

  1. Darii
  2. Camestres
  3. Ferio
  4. Baroco

Part C — Reflection. In 3–4 sentences, explain why the Figure 1 forms were called "perfect" by Aristotle and privileged in the Scholastic tradition. What property of Figure 1 makes its valid forms immediately evident, in a way that Figure 2 and 3 forms are not? Hint: consider what Figure 1 says about the relationship between the middle term and the other terms, compared to other figures.

Looking Ahead: You have now mapped the full terrain of categorical syllogistic: figures, moods, valid forms, and the tradition's names for them. Week 6 brings a new challenge — enthymemes, the real-world syllogisms that appear in actual discourse with one element left unstated. Learning to reconstruct what is missing is the skill that bridges formal logic and the arguments you encounter every day.
