Lesson 2.02c
The Fallacies of Appealing to Force, Popularity, and Pity
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments, evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning  |  ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the author's use of rhetoric (ethos, pathos, logos)  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Some arguments try to win agreement by using emotion or pressure instead of offering real support. Once again, these may persuade, but they don't prove anything. Each of these fallacies involves premises that fail to be relevant to the conclusion: the premises don't actually show that the conclusion is true. We will deal with three fallacies of this type: Appeal to Force, Appeal to Popularity, and Appeal to Pity.

Appeal to Force (argumentum ad baculum)

This fallacy tries to make someone accept a conclusion by threatening them. The threat may involve physical harm, but it need not. For example, consider the following.

"If you don't vote for this bill, you'll lose your job."

This "argument" threatens you, but not physically (at least not directly). Nonetheless, it is a fallacy. The threat may be real—but it doesn't show that the bill is a good idea. It replaces reasoning with pressure.

Of course, it may be in someone's interest to avoid harm, as it usually is. The fallacy, however, consists in this. The argument claims to establish the truth of a statement by appealing to premises about how you might avoid harm. Here's the general idea.

"You can avoid harm by accepting this statement. Therefore, this statement is true."

Obviously, this is a mistake in reason. This statement (whatever it is) is not true or false because you believe it.

Appeal to Popularity (argumentum ad populum)

This fallacy claims something is true or good just because many people believe it.

Example:

"Everyone knows Johnson is the best candidate—so Johnson must be the best."

Popularity doesn't equal truth. Crowds can be wrong. An argument needs more than public opinion to support its conclusion.

Appeal to Pity (argumentum ad misericordiam)

This fallacy asks for sympathy instead of providing reasons.

Example:

"You shouldn't fail me—I've been going through a really hard time."

Pity may be deserved, but it doesn't change whether the person met the requirements. The issue is being replaced by emotion.

Why These Are Fallacies

All three fallacies distract from the real issue. They rely on pressure, emotion, or crowd approval, rather than offering reasons to believe the conclusion is true.

They may work in politics, marketing, or social situations—but they are not fitting for rational persuasion.
