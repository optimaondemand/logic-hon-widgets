The Complex Question: Assumptions Hidden in Questions

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the embedded assumption in a complex question, explain why answering such a question directly concedes the hidden presupposition, and rewrite loaded questions in a form that removes the unwarranted assumption.

Orientation: Where We Are

You have now seen how a premise can smuggle an assumption into an argument — in begging the question, the conclusion hides inside a premise. The complex question is a close relative: instead of a premise carrying the unwarranted assumption, a question does. The question is posed in a form that forces any direct answer to accept a claim that has not been established. Because the carrier is a question rather than an assertion, the fallacy can be harder to spot. Both fallacies, however, belong to the same family: they presuppose what needs to be established.

Intuitive Entry: The Forced Choice

You have almost certainly encountered this pattern without having a name for it. Someone asks you a question and you immediately sense something is wrong with it — but if you try to answer directly (yes or no, either option), you end up agreeing to something you never accepted. The question is engineered so that any direct response concedes a prior claim.

The most famous example in the history of logic is: "Have you stopped stealing from your employer?" Any direct answer — "Yes, I have stopped" or "No, I have not stopped" — accepts the premise that you were stealing in the first place. If that premise is false, or simply unestablished, then neither answer is honest, and yet the question's grammatical form makes it seem as though one of them must be chosen. This is the structure of the complex question: one question wearing the disguise of a single yes-or-no inquiry, when it is actually two questions, the first of which has not been addressed.

The Formal Concept: Plurium Interrogationum

The fallacy is named in Latin plurium interrogationum, from plurium, the genitive plural of plus ("more, many"), and interrogationum, the genitive plural of interrogatio ("a question," from interrogare, "to ask" or "to inquire"). The full phrase means "of many questions" — the point being that what is presented as a single question is actually multiple questions fused together, with only one of them made explicit.

The fallacy is also called the loaded question, a term that emphasizes the rhetorical mechanism: the question is loaded with a concealed assumption that the questioner has no right to assume. The embedded assumption functions as a presupposition — something the question takes for granted rather than stating openly, and which the grammatical form of the question makes it difficult to reject without appearing evasive or uncooperative.

Aristotle addressed this in Sophistical Refutations (Chapter 17), identifying the problem of a questioner combining multiple distinct issues into a form that appears to demand a single unified answer. He recommended refusing to answer such questions as posed, a diagnostic response that remains the correct one.

Why This Is a Fallacy of Presumption

Recall that fallacies of presumption involve taking for granted what needs to be established. The complex question does this at the level of the question's form rather than at the level of a stated premise. The questioner presupposes — without arguing for — a claim that may be false, unproven, or contested. The structure of the question then traps the respondent: a direct answer either affirms or denies the apparent subject of the question, but in doing so it silently accepts the hidden assumption that the question was built on.

The question itself is not an argument, so there is no premise to inspect. That is precisely what makes this fallacy a distinct type. The assumption is not in a statement but in the presupposition embedded in the question's grammatical form. To identify it, you must ask: "What must be true for this question to make sense? What is the questioner taking for granted before the question can even get started?"

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Clear Case

Question: "Are you going to keep wasting your potential?"

Embedded assumption: You are currently wasting your potential.

Analysis: If you answer "No, I am going to stop wasting it," you accept that you were wasting it. If you answer "Yes, I am going to keep wasting it," you accept the same. Neither answer challenges the embedded claim. The only adequate response is to refuse the question's framing and address the assumption directly: "I don't accept the premise that I have been wasting my potential. What specifically do you mean?"

The two questions inside: (1) Have you been wasting your potential? (2) Will you continue?

The question posed addresses only (2), while taking (1) as already settled.

Example 2 — Subtler Case

Question: "What made you decide that your other coursework is more important than this class?"

Embedded assumption: You made a decision to treat other coursework as more important than this class.

Analysis: The question assumes both that a decision was made and that its content was a ranking of courses by importance. A student who simply had a demanding week with multiple deadlines may not have made any such decision or ranking. The question forecloses that possibility by treating the decision as a given and asking only about its cause. A student who answers "I decided that way because of X" has accepted the assumed decision; a student who says "I didn't decide that" sounds evasive, even though that is the accurate response.

Example 3 — In a Professional or Legal Context

Question: "When did you first realize that your earlier account of the incident was inaccurate?"

Embedded assumption: Your earlier account of the incident was inaccurate.

Analysis: This type of question is common in legal cross-examination, investigative interviews, and formal inquiries. It is effective precisely because answering it at all — even to deny the implication — can be taken as an implicit concession. The correct response is to identify the presupposition: "My earlier account was not inaccurate. The question assumes something that has not been established." Identifying complex questions in professional and legal contexts is a practical application of logical training, not merely an academic exercise.

Common Confusion — Complex vs. Difficult

The word "complex" in this fallacy does not mean intellectually difficult or hard to answer. It means that the question has a hidden component: it is composed of more than one question, though it appears to be one. A genuinely difficult question — "What are the long-term ecological consequences of urban heat islands?" — may require extensive research to answer, but it contains no hidden assumption. The person asking it is not presupposing a contested claim; they are simply requesting information on a complicated topic.

A complex question in the logical sense can often be quite short and simple-sounding: "Are you still careless with borrowed items?" has only eight words but contains a hidden accusation. The test is not length or difficulty but structure: does the question presuppose a claim that has not been established?

The Correct Response: The Unpack Technique

When you identify a complex question, the appropriate response is to refuse to answer as posed and to decompose the question into its components. This is called unpacking the question. The procedure is straightforward.

First, identify the hidden assumption embedded in the question's form. State it explicitly as a separate claim. Second, address that claim on its own terms: is it true? Has it been established? Is it something you accept? Third, if the assumption is false or unestablished, say so directly and decline to answer the fused question until the assumption is addressed. If the assumption turns out to be true, you can then answer the surface question on its own.

Applied to Example 1 above: "Your question assumes I have been wasting my potential. That claim hasn't been established, and I don't accept it. If you want to discuss whether I have been using my abilities well, I'm willing to have that conversation directly." This response is more assertive than a simple yes or no, but it is the only honest one. A willingness to challenge the framing of a question is a mark of careful thinking, not evasiveness.

Connection to Begging the Question

Both begging the question and the complex question are fallacies of presumption: both take for granted what needs to be established. In begging the question, the unwarranted assumption appears in a stated premise, where it can at least be inspected. In the complex question, the assumption is embedded in the form of a question before any argument is offered. In this sense, the complex question is in some ways more aggressive: it attempts to establish a claim not by offering any support but by treating it as so obviously settled that it does not even require statement.

Both fallacies share a common remedy: make the hidden assumption explicit, treat it as a separate claim, and evaluate it on its own merits before proceeding. The standard of intellectual honesty is the same in each case: claims require support, regardless of whether they arrive as premises or as presuppositions.

Practice Problems

Recognition. For each question below, identify the embedded assumption. State it as a separate, explicit claim.

  1. "Why do you always arrive late to meetings?"
  2. "When did the research team decide to ignore the contradictory data?"
  3. "Have you finally accepted that your original analysis was flawed?"
  4. "What made you choose to prioritize short-term results over long-term stability?"

Analysis.

  1. For each question in problems 1–4, write the "unpacked" version: separate the complex question into its two component questions and write them out individually. Then explain which of the two components the original question was taking for granted.
  2. Explain why refusing to answer a complex question as posed is not evasion. What is the person doing when they decline to answer directly, and how does this serve intellectual honesty?

Construction.

  1. Rewrite each of the four questions in problems 1–4 so that the hidden assumption is removed. The rewritten question should still address the same general subject but should not presuppose any contested claim. Label the assumption you removed in each case.
  2. Write one original complex question on a neutral topic (academics, everyday life, science, history). Identify its embedded assumption, write the unpacked version showing its two components, and write the fair version with the assumption removed.
Looking Ahead: Lesson Group 2.06 introduces three more fallacies of presumption that work differently from the circular ones studied this week: false cause (post hoc ergo propter hoc), false dilemma (bifurcatio), and appeal to unreliable authority (argumentum ad verecundiam). These fallacies do not involve circularity — instead, each assumes a relationship (causal, exhaustive, or authoritative) that has not been established.
