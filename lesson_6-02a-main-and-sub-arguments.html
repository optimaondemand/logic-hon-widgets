Lesson 6.02a
Main Arguments and Sub-Arguments

Learning Objective: Students will be able to distinguish the main argument of a complex text from its sub-arguments, identify which sub-arguments support which premises of the main argument, and represent the overall structure as an argument tree.

Orientation

The previous week extended your analytical toolkit to enthymemes — arguments with unstated elements. Both there and throughout the course, you have been working with single syllogisms in isolation. Real argumentation — in philosophical essays, scientific papers, and extended public discourse — is almost never a single syllogism. It is a structure of multiple arguments nested within one another, where the conclusions of some arguments serve as premises for others. This week introduces the concepts and vocabulary needed to analyze that extended structure. The goal is not new logical content but a new scale of analysis: from the single inference to the web of inferences that makes up a complete argument.

Intuitive Entry Point

Think about how a building is constructed. There is a main structure — the load-bearing framework that defines the building's shape and function. Supporting that framework are sub-structures: the foundations, the floor systems, the lateral bracing. Each sub-structure exists to make the main structure work. If you want to know whether the building is sound, you examine both the main structure and each sub-structure on its own terms, and you check how they connect.

An extended argument works the same way. There is a main conclusion — the central claim the arguer wants you to accept. Supporting that conclusion are premises. But those premises are not always just asserted; some of them are themselves the conclusions of sub-arguments, which exist to make those premises believable. Understanding a complex argument means identifying the main conclusion, tracing the premises, and then asking, for each premise, whether it is simply asserted or itself argued for.

Main Argument and Sub-Arguments: Definitions

In a compound argument:

  • The main argument is the argument whose conclusion is the overall claim the text or passage is trying to establish — the "final destination" of the reasoning.
  • A sub-argument (or subordinate argument) is an argument whose conclusion serves as a premise in the main argument (or in another sub-argument). Its purpose is to provide support for a claim that is then used to advance the main argument.
  • A statement that serves as both a conclusion (of a sub-argument) and a premise (of the main argument) is called an intermediate conclusion.

The relationship between main argument and sub-arguments can be represented as an argument tree: the main conclusion sits at the top (or bottom, depending on convention); the main premises branch from it; sub-argument conclusions occupy intermediate nodes; and the most basic premises — those simply asserted without further support — sit at the leaves.

Linked Premises

Before mapping an argument tree, it is worth being explicit about how the two premises of a categorical syllogism relate to its conclusion: they are linked. Linked premises work together — each one alone is insufficient to support the conclusion, but together they license it. Every categorical syllogism you have studied has this structure: neither the major nor the minor premise alone would establish the conclusion. Remove either one, and the inference collapses entirely.

This matters for evaluating an argument tree: attacking one of a syllogism's two linked premises collapses that entire inference. You do not need to defeat both — defeating either one is sufficient, because neither does any work without the other.

(You may encounter, outside this course, the idea of "independent" or "convergent" premises — separate pieces of evidence that each make a conclusion somewhat more likely on their own. That pattern belongs to inductive argument, not to the categorical syllogisms this course builds on, so it is set aside here. What a compound argument tree in this course can contain is multiple separate sub-arguments, each a complete syllogism in its own right, both concluding to the same claim — a different, fully deductive pattern you will see in Lesson 6.02c.)

Worked Example

Example — Identifying Main Argument and Sub-Arguments

Passage: "The coral reef in this region is dying. We know this because the water temperature has risen significantly above the tolerance range of reef-building corals, and all reef-building corals die when water temperature rises above their tolerance range. Moreover, the water temperature has risen because of increased industrial runoff, which is well-documented in the regional environmental reports."

Main argument (conclusion): "The coral reef in this region is dying."

Main premises:
(P1) All reef-building corals die when water temperature rises above their tolerance range.
(P2) The water temperature has risen significantly above the tolerance range of reef-building corals. ← This is also an intermediate conclusion.

Sub-argument supporting P2:
Sub-premise: Increased industrial runoff raises water temperature above the tolerance range of reef-building corals in this region.
Sub-premise: Industrial runoff in this region has increased significantly (documented in environmental reports).
Sub-conclusion (= P2): The water temperature has risen significantly above the tolerance range.

Observation: The main argument is a Barbara-type structure (AAA-1): All corals die above tolerance / This reef's water is above tolerance / Therefore this reef's corals are dying. Its own two premises are linked, as always. The sub-argument's role is different: it establishes P2 by supplying a separate, complete syllogism of its own, feeding its conclusion up into the main argument as a premise.

Connection to Argument

Identifying the main argument and its sub-arguments is the first step in evaluating any extended piece of reasoning. An argument can be locally valid — each individual inference follows correctly — yet fail as a whole because a premise at a crucial node is false or itself poorly supported. Conversely, identifying where a sub-argument is weakest tells you where the most effective objection can be lodged. Aristotle's dialectical method, as developed in the Topics, is fundamentally about finding the node in an argument tree where a concession is most damaging. This week's skills are the modern form of that same practice.

Practice Problems

Part A — Identify Main and Sub-Arguments. Read the following passage and answer the questions below it.

"Migratory birds navigate using the Earth's magnetic field. We know this because experiments have shown that disrupting the magnetic field with artificial magnets causes birds to lose their way, and only creatures that detect magnetic fields would be affected by such disruptions. Furthermore, the mechanism for this detection has been identified at the cellular level: birds possess magnetite crystals in their beak cells, and all creatures with magnetite crystals in sensory cells have the biological substrate for magnetic field detection."

  1. What is the main conclusion of this passage?
  2. Identify two sub-arguments and state the intermediate conclusion each one establishes.
  3. Apply the removal test to the main argument's two premises: what happens to the inference if either one is removed? Confirm that they are linked.

Part B — Removal Test. For each pair of premises below, apply the removal test and confirm that the premises are linked — state in one sentence what happens to the inference when each premise is removed in turn.

  1. Conclusion: "This triangle is equilateral."
    Premise 1: All three sides of this triangle are equal in length.
    Premise 2: A triangle with all three sides equal in length is equilateral by definition.
  2. Conclusion: "Seafloor spreading confirms plate movement in this region."
    Premise 1: Direct measurement shows the seafloor in this region is spreading at a measurable rate.
    Premise 2: Any region where the seafloor spreads at a measurable rate has plates in motion.

Part C — Construction. Write a short compound argument (4–6 sentences) on a topic from science, mathematics, or history. Include one main argument and at least one sub-argument. Then identify in labeled form: main conclusion, main premises, intermediate conclusion, and sub-argument premises.

Looking Ahead: With the concept of the argument tree in place, the next lesson consolidates the removal test and applies it, along with the reconstruction and Venn-diagram skills from previous weeks, to a set of extended practice problems — confirming, case by case, that a syllogism's premises are always linked.
