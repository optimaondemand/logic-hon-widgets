Lesson 2.03c
The Red Herring

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the red herring fallacy, explain why introducing an irrelevant topic constitutes a failure of relevance, and distinguish the red herring from the straw man.

Orientation: Where We Are

This is the third fallacy of relevance in Lesson 2.03. You have already studied the argument from ignorance (2.03a), which draws an unwarranted conclusion from a lack of evidence, and the straw man (2.03b), which misrepresents an opponent's position before attacking it. Both of those fallacies fail the relevance test: the premises do not genuinely support the conclusion being argued. The red herring belongs to the same family, but it works differently. Rather than distorting or misrepresenting an argument, it abandons the argument altogether and substitutes a different topic entirely.

Intuitive Entry: The Conversation That Went Somewhere Else

Think of a conversation you have had — or witnessed — that started on one topic and ended, somehow, somewhere completely different. Two people begin discussing whether a project was handled well. Within a few exchanges, one person is defending their general character, the other is listing unrelated accomplishments, and nobody is talking about the project anymore. The original question has evaporated. Both parties may even feel that something was resolved.

This experience is nearly universal, and it is not accidental. When a question is uncomfortable or difficult to answer, one natural response is to shift attention toward something easier, something adjacent, or something emotionally engaging. The topic changes; the conversation continues; the original issue is never addressed. When this happens deliberately in an argument, it has a name. But the pattern is recognizable before any formal definition is needed, because you have already lived it.

The Formal Concept

The red herring fallacy occurs when a speaker introduces an irrelevant topic in order to divert attention from the issue actually under discussion. The Latin name, ignoratio elenchi, means "ignorance of the refutation" — that is, a failure to address what actually needs to be addressed. (Note that the straw man is sometimes classified under the same Latin name, since both fallacies involve not engaging with the real argument. The red herring is the purer case: instead of misrepresenting the argument, the speaker sidesteps it entirely.)

The name "red herring" comes from an old practice in fox hunting. A red herring — a fish that has been cured and smoked until it turns reddish-brown and develops a powerful smell — was sometimes dragged across a trail to confuse hunting dogs. The dogs would follow the strong scent of the herring rather than the fox's trail. In argument, the "herring" is whatever topic is introduced to pull the audience away from the original question. The herring may be interesting, it may even be true, but it does not address the matter at hand.

Aristotle identified this fallacy in the Sophistical Refutations, where he catalogued the ways an argument can appear to succeed while actually failing. The red herring is a failure of relevance: the new topic has no bearing on whether the original claim is true or the original argument is sound.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — The Clearest Case

A journalist asks a company's chief executive about the company's environmental record. The executive responds: "This company employs five thousand people, supports local charities, and has been part of this community for forty years."

Analysis: Everything the executive says may be true. Employment numbers, community involvement, and longevity are all real facts. But none of them address the environmental question. Whether the company harms or protects the environment is a separate matter from how many people it employs or how long it has existed. The executive has not answered the question. The audience's attention has been redirected toward something favorable and emotionally resonant, but the original topic has been abandoned entirely. This is the red herring in its most straightforward form.

Example 2 — A Subtler Case

Two students are discussing whether a particular historical policy was effective. One asks: "But did the policy actually achieve its stated goals?" The other responds: "The people who implemented it worked incredibly hard, and they genuinely cared about solving the problem."

Analysis: The effort and good intentions of policymakers are real considerations in some discussions. But the question posed was about effectiveness: did the policy achieve its stated goals? That is an empirical question about outcomes. Whether the implementers worked hard or cared deeply about the problem is a separate question entirely. A policy can be well-intentioned and still fail. A policy can succeed despite being implemented by people with mixed motives. The response has shifted from outcomes to intentions, and the original question about effectiveness remains unanswered.

Common Confusion — Red Herring vs. Straw Man

Both the straw man and the red herring involve not engaging with the actual argument. Students often conflate them. The distinction is important.

In a straw man, the speaker does engage with what appears to be the opponent's argument, but the argument being addressed is a distorted or weakened version of the real one. The original argument is misrepresented and then attacked. The opponent's position is still the nominal subject of the response.

In a red herring, the speaker does not even attempt to address the argument. A different topic is introduced entirely. There is no distortion of the original argument because the original argument is simply set aside.

A useful test: ask whether the speaker is responding to anything the other party actually said or argued. If the response is a twisted version of that argument, it may be a straw man. If the response has nothing to do with that argument, it is likely a red herring.

Why This Counts as a Fallacy of Relevance

The red herring earns its place in the family of relevance fallacies because the introduced topic fails the relevance test entirely. Recall from Lesson 2.02a that a premise is relevant if it gives genuine reason to accept or reject the conclusion under discussion. In a red herring, the new material does not support or undermine the original conclusion. It simply replaces the original question with a different one.

This makes the red herring distinct from a legitimate change of subject. Speakers do sometimes move to a new topic by mutual agreement, or because the new topic turns out to be more fundamental and genuinely clarifies the original question. What distinguishes the red herring is that the shift is presented as if it were a response to the original question, and the original question is left unresolved.

The red herring is particularly common in public discourse and debate because audiences can be genuinely engaged by the new topic without noticing that the original question was never answered. An emotionally compelling substitution is often more persuasive than a careful argument would be.

Tracking the Topic in Public Discourse

The practical skill this fallacy develops is topic-tracking: the ability to monitor what question is actually on the table, and to notice when the conversation has moved away from it. This requires active attention. Debates, press conferences, editorial exchanges, and everyday conversations all drift, and they do not always drift by accident.

When you notice a potential red herring, the right move is not simply to name the fallacy. The more productive response is to return to the original question directly: "That may be true, but the question was whether..." This keeps the discussion anchored and puts pressure on the speaker to address the actual matter. The ability to do this without losing the thread — especially in fast-moving exchanges — is one of the most practical benefits of studying informal fallacies.

Practice Problems

Recognition

  1. A student asks a school administrator why a particular course will no longer be offered. The administrator responds: "We have added three new electives this year and expanded the library collection significantly." Identify the fallacy, if any, and explain your reasoning.
  2. A reviewer criticizes a book for factual errors in its third chapter. The author responds: "My previous two books received excellent reviews and were adopted by several universities." Is this a red herring, a straw man, or neither? Explain what makes it the type of fallacy you identified.

Analysis

  1. Read the following exchange carefully:
    Councilmember A: "The proposal to expand the bus route would reduce traffic congestion on the main corridor."
    Councilmember B: "Public transportation is not perfect anywhere in the world, and there are many cities that have tried to expand transit and struggled with costs."
    (a) Is B's response a red herring, a straw man, or a legitimate counterargument? Explain your reasoning. (b) What would a direct response to A's claim look like? Write one or two sentences that would actually engage with A's argument.
  2. Explain in your own words why the red herring is a fallacy of relevance rather than, say, a fallacy of presumption. What specifically about the new topic fails the relevance test?

Construction

  1. Write a short exchange (three to five lines) in which one speaker commits a clear red herring in response to a direct question. The exchange should involve a neutral, everyday scenario. Below the exchange, identify the original question and the unrelated topic introduced.
  2. Take the exchange you wrote for Problem 5. Rewrite the second speaker's response so that it addresses the original question directly, without fallacy. Then write a third version of the response that commits a straw man instead of a red herring. Label all three versions clearly and note what distinguishes the straw man version from the red herring version.
Looking Ahead: With the red herring, you complete the survey of fallacies of relevance: arguments that fail because their premises simply do not connect to the conclusion. The next section of the course turns to a different category of failure, fallacies of ambiguity, in which the problem is not irrelevance but instability of meaning. A word or phrase shifts in sense between premises, and the argument breaks down as a result. Lesson 2.04a introduces this new category.
