Lesson 2.08a
Intellectual Virtues: What Does Reasoning Well Look Like?
CPALMS Benchmark: ELA.K12.EE.3.1 — Demonstrate logical understanding and critical reasoning in written and spoken communication.
Also aligned: ELA.11.C.5.1, ELA.11.R.3.4

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the five core intellectual virtues, explain how each functions as an antidote to a specific fallacy or reasoning error, and evaluate sample arguments for the presence or absence of those virtues.

Orientation

This is the final lesson in Module 2 on informal fallacies. Over the past several lessons, we have catalogued the ways reasoning can go wrong — from the ad hominem attack that ignores the argument itself, to the straw man that misrepresents an opponent's position, to the false dilemma that artificially narrows the available choices. We have studied reasoning errors in advertising, political speech, and online discourse.

This lesson shifts the lens. Instead of asking what to avoid, we ask what excellent reasoning looks like. Intellectual virtues are the positive habits of mind that make a reasoner trustworthy — both to others and to oneself. Understanding them completes the picture: fallacies are departures from good reasoning, and intellectual virtues are the standard from which they depart.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider two students working through a difficult proof. The first student, halfway through, realizes the approach she chose is not going to work. She quietly abandons it and tries a different route, never acknowledging the dead end. The second student reaches the same dead end and says, "I was wrong about that step — let me back up and explain why." Both students eventually find the answer. Which one do you trust more as a reasoner?

Most people find the second student more trustworthy, and for a specific reason: she does not hide the structure of her thinking. She acknowledges where she went wrong. This quality — the willingness to follow reasoning honestly even when it reflects badly on your initial position — is an intellectual virtue. It is not a talent or an intelligence level; it is a habit, one that can be cultivated and that makes all the difference in whether someone reasons reliably over time.

Formal Concept: Intellectual Virtue

The word virtue derives from the Latin virtus, which in turn translates the Greek aretē — excellence, or the condition of performing one's proper function well. For Aristotle, virtues are stable character traits that enable a person to act well in a given domain. Moral virtues — courage, justice, temperance — govern action and emotion. Intellectual virtues govern the activity of the mind, specifically the habits of inquiry, judgment, and communication that make reasoning reliable.

Aristotle distinguished two major families of intellectual virtue in the Nicomachean Ethics: virtues of theoretical reason (such as scientific knowledge and wisdom) and virtues of practical reason (such as good judgment in particular situations). The intellectual virtues treated in this lesson belong to a third, overlapping category that medieval and contemporary philosophers have developed in detail: the habits of mind that govern how a person engages with evidence, arguments, and disagreement. These are sometimes called the epistemic virtues (from the Greek epistēmē, knowledge).

Aristotle also held that virtues are means — they occupy a middle ground between two corresponding vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage, for example, is the mean between recklessness and cowardice. The same structure applies to intellectual virtues: open-mindedness is the mean between a mind too open — one that refuses to exclude anything as false, even claims that have been shown to be false — and closed-mindedness (accepting nothing new).

The Five Core Intellectual Virtues

Virtue Definition Vice of Deficiency Vice of Excess
Intellectual Humility Recognizing the limits of one's own knowledge; acknowledging what one does not know Intellectual arrogance (overclaiming knowledge) Intellectual self-deprecation (dismissing one's own justified beliefs)
Intellectual Courage Following an argument to its conclusion even when that conclusion is uncomfortable or unpopular Intellectual cowardice (retreating from conclusions to avoid criticism) Intellectual recklessness (accepting conclusions without adequate reasoning)
Intellectual Honesty Acknowledging evidence that counts against one's own position; not selectively citing only favorable evidence Intellectual dishonesty (suppressing contrary evidence) Excessive self-doubt (discounting one's own genuine evidence)
Intellectual Perseverance Sustaining inquiry on a difficult question rather than settling prematurely for a convenient answer Intellectual laziness (abandoning inquiry too soon) Obsessive doubt (refusing to reach any conclusion at all)
Open-Mindedness Genuinely entertaining alternative positions; being responsive to evidence rather than committed in advance to a conclusion Closed-mindedness (refusing to consider alternatives) The overly open mind (refusing to exclude what is false as false, even once it has been shown to be false)

Connection to Optima's Seven Virtues

Three of Optima's seven character virtues — courage, honesty, and perseverance — appear directly in this list, applied specifically to the life of the mind. This is not a coincidence. The classical tradition held that virtues are unified: the person who is genuinely courageous in action is likely to be courageous in thought as well, because both expressions draw on the same underlying character. A course in logic is, among other things, a course in the application of general virtues to the specific domain of reasoning.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Intellectual Humility vs. Appeal to Unreliable Authority

Intellectually vicious version: A student is asked whether a historical figure's economic policy was effective. He cites one economist who agrees with his view and presents that citation as settling the matter. When the teacher points out that most economists in the field disagree, he says, "Well, mine is more recent."

What went wrong: The student is exhibiting intellectual arrogance — overclaiming certainty on the basis of a single favorable source while dismissing the weight of contrary expert opinion. This is also related to the fallacy of appealing to unreliable or selective authority.

Intellectually virtuous version: The student cites his source, then adds: "I should note that most economists in this period disagree. I find this particular argument more convincing because of X, but I acknowledge the question is contested." This response demonstrates intellectual humility — it does not hide the limits of the student's knowledge behind a false show of certainty.

Example 2 — Intellectual Courage vs. Intellectual Cowardice

Intellectually vicious version: A student has been arguing that a certain argument is valid. Midway through the analysis, the teacher shows that one of the premises is false. The student says, "I see your point — maybe the argument has problems. I just meant it could be interpreted different ways." He abandons his position not because of a new argument but because of social pressure.

What went wrong: The student is exhibiting intellectual cowardice — retreating from a position not because new evidence or a new argument changed his mind, but because he found disagreement uncomfortable. Changing one's mind is appropriate when the argument changes; it is a vice when the social situation changes.

Intellectually virtuous version: The student says, "I need to think about that. The premise you've identified does look false — let me check whether the conclusion can be supported another way, or whether I need to revise my position." This response shows both intellectual courage (taking the challenge seriously) and intellectual honesty (not pretending the problem does not exist).

Example 3 — Intellectual Honesty vs. Cherry-Picking

Intellectually vicious version: A student writing an argument paper cites four studies that support her thesis and omits six studies that contradict it. She knows the six exist; she simply does not mention them.

What went wrong: This is a form of intellectual dishonesty — not lying, but selective presentation of evidence that creates a false impression of the state of the question. It is structurally related to the fallacy of suppressed evidence and to the informal fallacy of begging the question (stacking the inquiry in advance).

Intellectually virtuous version: The student acknowledges the contrary studies in her paper: "Six studies have found the opposite effect. I believe this discrepancy is explained by differences in methodology; specifically, studies that control for X tend to support my thesis." This response is more honest and, ultimately, more persuasive — because it shows the reader that the student has actually engaged with the difficulty.

Intellectual Virtues as Antidotes to Fallacies

Each intellectual virtue functions as a specific corrective to one or more informal fallacies. The following connections are not exhaustive, but they illustrate the pattern:

  • Intellectual humility corrects the appeal to unreliable authority and ad hominem: the humble reasoner does not overclaim expertise and does not dismiss an argument by attacking its source.
  • Intellectual courage corrects red herring and straw man: the courageous reasoner does not dodge a hard question by changing the subject, and does not weaken an opponent's position to make it easier to attack.
  • Intellectual honesty corrects suppressed evidence, false cause, and begging the question: the honest reasoner does not hide unfavorable data, does not mistake correlation for causation when better evidence is available, and does not assume the conclusion at the outset.
  • Intellectual perseverance corrects false dilemma and appeal to ignorance: the perseverant reasoner does not artificially narrow options when more work would reveal more possibilities, and does not conclude that something is false merely because it has not yet been proved true.
  • Open-mindedness corrects argument from ignorance and closed-minded appeals to tradition or authority: the open-minded reasoner evaluates alternatives on their merits rather than rejecting them in advance.

Connection to Argument

Understanding intellectual virtues is directly useful for argument analysis and construction. When you assess someone else's argument, you are not only asking whether it is formally valid and whether the premises are true — you are also asking whether the argument has been honestly constructed. Has the arguer acknowledged contrary evidence? Have they engaged with the strongest version of the opposing position? These questions belong to the same discipline as formal logic; they complete it.

When you construct your own arguments, intellectual virtue is the disposition that keeps you honest at every step: choosing premises you actually believe, acknowledging the limits of your evidence, following the logic where it leads rather than stopping when you reach a comfortable conclusion. The logician who lacks intellectual virtue will eventually construct arguments that are formally correct but epistemically dishonest — technically valid, but built on selectively chosen premises. Formal logic and intellectual virtue are partners, not substitutes.

Practice Problems

Part A — Recognition (identify the missing virtue)

For each scenario below, name the intellectual virtue that is absent and the corresponding fallacy or reasoning error it relates to.

  1. A writer argues that a new medical treatment is safe because no studies have proven it harmful — ignoring that the treatment has not yet been studied at all.
  2. A debater, when confronted with a strong counter-argument, says, "Well, that's just one way of looking at it," and pivots to a different topic without addressing the argument.
  3. A student confidently presents a conclusion about a scientific question as if it were settled, but has read only one introductory article on the subject.
  4. A researcher summarizes the literature on her topic but omits a meta-analysis that undermines her thesis, though she read it two weeks ago.
  5. A student stops working on a logic problem midway through because "it's too complicated," even though the problem is solvable with additional effort.

Part B — Analysis (evaluate the reasoning)

Read each pair of responses to the same situation. For each pair, identify which response demonstrates the relevant intellectual virtue, which demonstrates a vice, and explain the distinction in one or two sentences.

  1. Situation: A student has argued that all sound arguments have true premises. Another student points out a counterexample.
    Response A: "That's a good point. I think my original claim was too strong — I should have said that sound arguments require true premises, not that they always have them in practice. Let me revise."
    Response B: "I see what you mean. I guess it depends on how you define 'sound.' There are many ways to look at it."
  2. Situation: A student is writing a paper arguing for a historical interpretation. He finds three sources supporting his view and two sources that contradict it.
    Response A: He cites all five sources, acknowledges the disagreement, and explains why he finds the supporting sources more convincing.
    Response B: He cites only the three supporting sources, reasoning that the contradicting ones are "less relevant."

Part C — Construction (apply the concept)

Choose one of the five intellectual virtues. Write a short paragraph (75–100 words) doing the following: (a) define the virtue in your own words; (b) identify the specific fallacy or reasoning error it corrects; (c) give an original example — not one from this lesson — showing the virtue in action.
