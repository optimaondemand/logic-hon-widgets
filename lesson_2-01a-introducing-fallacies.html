Lesson 2.01a — Introducing Fallacies

What Is a Fallacy?

A fallacy is a mistake in reasoning. It occurs when an argument fails to do what arguments are supposed to do: offer support for a conclusion. More than this, however, fallacies can be tricky. They are not just any mistake in reasoning. Some mistakes are so obvious, they don't get title of "fallacy."

Roses are red.
Violets are blue.
Therefore, the Phillies will win the pennant.

Obviously, statement 3 is not supported by statements 1 and 2. Anyone can understand that it would be a mistake to argue this way. Fallacies, by contrast, are tricky. And because they are tricky, they are also common.

A fallacy is a mistaken pattern of reasoning common enough to have a name.

In logic, we don't just look at whether a conclusion is true—we ask whether the reasoning supports the conclusion. A fallacy is a kind of flawed argument that fails to do this, even if it sounds convincing. In fact, fallacies often do seem persuasive—that's what makes them dangerous.

This is why it's important to remember what you learned earlier about the difference between argument and persuasion. It is quite possible to be persuaded to believe something irrationally. Fallacies often succeed in persuading people without actually providing rational support.

In Module 2, we'll learn to recognize these patterns, understand why they don't work, and give them their proper names. Some fallacies are obvious once you see them. Others take careful attention to detect. But all fallacies share one thing in common: they pretend to be good reasoning when they're not.

But first, let's make one further point about fallacies in general.

Formal vs. Informal Fallacies

Some fallacies involve invalid argument forms. Other fallacies, however, are fallacious by their content rather than their form.

Formal Fallacies

A formal fallacy is a mistake in the logical form or structure of a deductive argument. Even if the content sounds reasonable, the conclusion doesn't logically follow.

Example (invalid structure):

If it is raining, the ground is wet.
The ground is wet.
Therefore, it is raining.

This looks okay at first, but it's a formal fallacy called affirming the consequent. The structure is invalid—there could be other reasons the ground is wet (like a sprinkler).

In formal fallacies, the reasoning fails regardless of the content of the statements.

Informal Fallacies

An informal fallacy is a mistake in reasoning that has to do with the content, not the form. These usually occur in ordinary language—arguments that seem persuasive but break down under careful analysis.

Here is an example of a fallacy that you might encounter in a conversation:

"You shouldn't listen to his argument about immigration policy—he didn't even finish college."

This is an ad hominem fallacy. The person's educational background may or may not be relevant, but it doesn't address the argument itself. It attacks the person, not the reasoning.

Informal fallacies are often persuasive because they play on emotion, bias, or distraction instead of logic.

Summary

Can you see the difference between formal and informal fallacies? Here is one way to think about it. Let's take both arguments, and translate them into a "formal" structure, using capital letters. So,

If it is raining, the ground is wet.
The ground is wet.
Therefore, it is raining.

Turns into,

If X, then Y.
Y.
Therefore, X

Even in this version, the argument is still a fallacy. That's because the fallacy is present in the form of the argument, not the content. In other words, the fallacy has nothing to do with rain or the ground being wet.

On the other hand, consider the other argument. We might put the bad argument like this.

If he didn't finish college, then you shouldn't listen to his argument about immigration policy.
He didn't finish college.
Therefore, you shouldn't listen to his argument about immigration policy.

If we translate this into a formal structure, we get,

If X, then Y.
X.
Therefore Y.

Now, this is a valid form. So there is no "formal" fallacy in this argument! Nonetheless, there is a fallacy in the first premise. (We will talk about this in a future lesson). In other words, the fallacy involves the content of the statements.
