Lesson 5.02c — Testing Validity with Venn Diagrams

CPALMS Benchmarks:
ELA.11.R.2.3 — Analyze how an author uses and refines the meaning of a key term or concept over the course of a text.
ELA.11.C.1.3 — Write arguments to support claims using valid reasoning and relevant evidence.

Learning objective: After completing this lesson, you will be able to diagram both premises of a categorical syllogism in a three-circle Venn diagram and use the completed diagram to determine whether the syllogism is valid or invalid — identifying precisely which region, if any, reveals the argument's failure.

Orientation

In Lesson 5.02a you learned that the three-circle Venn diagram maps three categories — subject (S), predicate (P), and middle term (M) — onto a spatial field in which every possible combination of membership is visible at once. In Lesson 5.02b you worked through the conventions of the diagram itself: the eight regions, what shading represents (a region is empty), and what an X represents (at least one member exists there). You also practiced the ordering rule: diagram universal premises first, then particular ones.

This lesson is where those tools earn their keep. You will now use a completed diagram as a validity test — a formal procedure that tells you, with geometric certainty, whether a syllogism's conclusion follows from its premises. Everything you have built so far in Q2 points toward this test.

Intuitive Entry

Consider a simple situation. You learn two things: (1) every room in a particular building has been painted white, and (2) the library is one of those rooms. Without doing any additional investigation, you already know the library is white. No new premises were needed. The conclusion was already contained in what you knew.

This is the core intuition behind the Venn diagram validity test. When you diagram both premises of a syllogism, you are encoding everything the argument claims. If, after that encoding, the conclusion's information is already visible in the diagram — already spatially present — then the argument is valid. The conclusion was never genuinely new; it was always already implied. If the diagram does not show the conclusion, then the conclusion is making a claim the premises never established, and the argument is invalid.

The Venn diagram makes this containment-or-not spatially unmistakable. Instead of reasoning through the implication in your head and perhaps missing something, you read it off the picture.

The Validity Test

The procedure has three steps, applied in order.

  1. Diagram the major premise. Using the conventions from Lesson 5.02b, shade or place an X to represent the major premise (the premise containing the predicate term P).
  2. Diagram the minor premise. Apply the same conventions to the minor premise (the premise containing the subject term S). Remember: universal premises are always diagrammed before particular ones, regardless of the order in which they appear in the syllogism.
  3. Inspect the diagram for the conclusion. Ask whether the conclusion's information is already present:
  • If the conclusion is a universal statement (A or E), check whether every region that the conclusion says is empty is already shaded. If every required region is shaded, the conclusion is already shown — the syllogism is valid. If any required region remains unshaded, the syllogism is invalid.
  • If the conclusion is a particular statement (I or O), check whether an X already appears in the region the conclusion requires. If the X is there, the syllogism is valid. If the region is empty or only shaded, the syllogism is invalid.

The critical point is that you do not add anything to the diagram when checking the conclusion. You only read what is already there. A valid argument commits you to its conclusion the moment you accept its premises; the diagram makes that commitment visible.

Worked Example 1 — Valid Syllogism (Barbara)

The syllogism called Barbara — a name coined by medieval Scholastic logicians to capture its AAA mood — is the oldest and most important valid syllogistic form in Aristotle's Prior Analytics. It has the following structure:

Major premise: All M are P.
Minor premise: All S are M.
Therefore: All S are P.

Every term is used universally; both premises and the conclusion are A-statements (universal affirmatives). Work through the diagramming steps below.

Step 1 — Draw the blank diagram. Three overlapping circles: S at the left, P at the right, M at the bottom-center. This gives eight regions: S-only, P-only, M-only, S∩P (excluding M), S∩M (excluding P), P∩M (excluding S), the triple-overlap S∩P∩M, and the region outside all three circles.
Step 2 — Diagram the major premise: "All M are P." An A-statement says that every member of the subject class (here M) is also in the predicate class (here P). Equivalently, no M exists outside P. Shade every region that is part of M but not part of P: that is the M-only region (bottom-center, touching neither S nor P) and the S∩M region that excludes P (the left lower-overlap between S and M). Both of these regions are now shaded.
Step 3 — Diagram the minor premise: "All S are M." Again an A-statement: no S exists outside M. Shade every region that is part of S but not part of M: the S-only region (far left, touching neither M nor P) and the S∩P region that excludes M (the upper overlap between S and P, but not the triple center). Both of these regions are now shaded.
Step 4 — Check the conclusion: "All S are P." This A-statement requires that every region belonging to S but not to P is empty (shaded). The regions of S that fall outside P are: S-only and S∩M-without-P. Check each:
  • S-only: shaded in Step 3. ✓
  • S∩M-without-P: shaded in Step 2. ✓
Every part of S that falls outside P is already shaded. The conclusion is already shown in the diagram.
Shaded region = empty (no members exist here). Unshaded = possibly occupied.

Every region of S that falls outside P is shaded. The conclusion "All S are P" was not smuggled in — it was already encoded in the premises the moment they were diagrammed.

VALID

Barbara is the paradigm of Aristotelian syllogistic. Its validity is not a matter of convention or interpretation; it is a structural fact made visible by the diagram. Any argument with this form — All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P — is valid regardless of what S, P, and M actually refer to.

Worked Example 2 — Invalid Syllogism (Undistributed Middle)

Now consider a superficially similar argument. It also has two A-statement premises and an A-statement conclusion, but the middle term is placed differently:

Major premise: All P are M.
Minor premise: All S are M.
Therefore: All S are P. (INVALID)

The conclusion looks the same as in Barbara, and both premises are universal affirmatives. Yet this argument is invalid. The diagram will show exactly why.

Step 1 — Draw the blank diagram. Same layout as before: S at the left, P at the right, M at the bottom-center.
Step 2 — Diagram the major premise: "All P are M." No P exists outside M. Shade every region that is part of P but not part of M: the P-only region (far right) and the S∩P region that excludes M (the upper overlap between S and P, above M's boundary).
Step 3 — Diagram the minor premise: "All S are M." No S exists outside M. Shade every region that is part of S but not part of M: the S-only region (far left) and the S∩P region that excludes M (the same upper S∩P region — if it is already shaded from Step 2, it remains shaded; shade it only once).
Step 4 — Check the conclusion: "All S are P." This requires that every region belonging to S but not to P is empty (shaded). The regions of S outside P are: S-only and S∩M-without-P. Check each:
  • S-only: shaded in Step 3. ✓
  • S∩M-without-P: not shaded by either premise. ✗
The S∩M region that excludes P has not been touched. It is unshaded — the diagram leaves open the possibility that some members exist there, members who are in S and in M but not in P. Because that possibility is not ruled out, the conclusion cannot be read off the diagram. The conclusion is NOT already shown.
Shaded region = empty. The red label marks the region the conclusion requires to be shaded but is not.

INVALID

This is the classic fallacy of the undistributed middle. Both S and P are connected to M — both are subsets of M — but M does not thereby connect S to P. It is entirely possible that S and P occupy completely different parts of M without overlapping each other at all. The premises establish that S fits within M and P fits within M; they say nothing about how S and P are situated relative to each other. The diagram shows this gap precisely: the S∩M-without-P region is open and could contain members, which would make the conclusion false.

A concrete illustration: suppose M = mammals, P = dogs, S = cats. "All dogs are mammals" and "all cats are mammals" are both true, but "all cats are dogs" does not follow. The middle term (mammal) is common to both, but it was never distributed in a way that forces cats into the dog category. The diagram would show exactly this: the S∩M region outside P would be unshaded — the region where cats live, within mammals, but outside dogs.

Connection to Argument

The Venn diagram validity test does something that a verbal inspection of an argument cannot do as reliably: it makes the structure of the argument visible in space, so that the question "does the conclusion follow?" becomes "is this region shaded?" That spatial question has a definite answer. Verbal arguments can be worded in ways that obscure the logical structure; diagrams cannot.

When an argument is invalid, the diagram does not merely report that fact — it shows where the failure is. In the undistributed middle example, you can point to a specific unshaded region and say: here is the gap. Here is the scenario the premises allow but the conclusion rules out. This is the kind of analytical precision that separates rigorous argument evaluation from vague impressionistic judgment.

The test also reinforces an insight at the core of Aristotelian logic: validity is entirely a matter of form. The same three-circle diagram, with the same shading pattern, establishes validity for every argument of the Barbara form — regardless of what the subject, predicate, and middle terms actually refer to. Structure is what matters. Content can vary without affecting the logical verdict.

Practice Problems

Level 1 — Recognition. In each of the following, you are told which regions of a three-circle Venn diagram (S at left, P at right, M at bottom) are shaded after both premises have been diagrammed. Determine which of the listed statements is already shown in the diagram — that is, which statement's truth is entailed by the shading described.

  1. Shaded regions: S-only; S∩M-without-P.
    Which of these is already shown?
    1. All S are P
    2. No S are M
    3. All S are M

    Think: "All S are P" requires every part of S outside P to be shaded. "No S are M" would require S∩M to be shaded entirely. "All S are M" requires every part of S outside M to be shaded.

  2. Shaded regions: S-only; S∩P-without-M; S∩M-without-P; M-only.
    Which of these is already shown?
    1. All S are P
    2. No M are S
    3. Some S are P

    Consider carefully which regions each statement requires to be empty or occupied.

  3. Shaded regions: S∩P-without-M; S∩P∩M. (That is, the entire S∩P lens is shaded.)
    Which of these is already shown?
    1. No S are P
    2. No S are M
    3. All P are S

Level 2 — Analysis. Work through the following syllogism using the three-step diagramming procedure. You do not need to draw an SVG; describe your shading in words for each step, then state your validity verdict and explain it.

Syllogism to analyze Major premise: No M are P. (E-statement)
Minor premise: All S are M. (A-statement)
Conclusion: Therefore, no S are P. (E-statement)

Recall: an E-statement (universal negative) requires that the entire overlap between two circles be shaded. An A-statement requires that the part of the subject circle falling outside the predicate circle be shaded.

After completing your diagram description, answer: is the conclusion already shown? Identify the specific regions relevant to the conclusion and whether they are shaded.

This form is known as Celarent (EAE-1) and is one of the four valid moods in Figure 1. Your diagram should confirm this.

Level 3 — Construction. Construct a categorical syllogism that is invalid — one that can be shown to fail by the Venn diagram test. Your syllogism must:

  • Have two premises and one conclusion, each a standard categorical statement (A, E, I, or O).
  • Use three distinct terms (S, P, M) with M as the middle term.
  • Not repeat either of the two examples worked in this lesson.

After stating your syllogism, identify: (a) which regions are shaded when both premises are diagrammed, and (b) which specific region the conclusion requires to be shaded (or marked with an X) but is not. Explain in one sentence why that gap makes the argument invalid.
