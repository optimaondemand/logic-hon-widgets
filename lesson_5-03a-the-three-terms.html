Lesson 5.03a — The Three Terms of a Syllogism

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze the structure an author uses to organize a text; ELA.11.C.1.4 — Write arguments to support claims using valid reasoning and relevant evidence

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the major term, minor term, and middle term in any standard-form categorical syllogism and explain the logical function each term performs.

Orientation

You have now learned to diagram individual categorical statements and to test two-premise syllogisms for validity using three-circle Venn diagrams. This week, we slow down and examine the internal structure of the syllogism in closer detail. Before you can work efficiently with figures, moods, and valid forms, you need a precise understanding of what the three terms of a syllogism are and how each one functions. That is the work of this lesson.

Intuitive Entry

Consider how a detective reaches a conclusion. She does not simply observe two unrelated facts and announce a verdict. She connects them through a shared element — something that appears in both her evidence streams and links them together. "The suspect was in London on Tuesday. The crime occurred in London on Tuesday. Therefore, the suspect was present at the time of the crime." The shared element — "in London on Tuesday" — is what makes the two pieces of evidence speak to each other.

A categorical syllogism works the same way. It connects two statements through a shared term, and that shared term is what makes the inference possible. Without it, the two premises would simply be unrelated facts sitting next to each other. Understanding the three terms of a syllogism means understanding what each one contributes to this act of connection.

The Three Terms: Definitions

Every standard-form categorical syllogism contains exactly three terms, each appearing exactly twice across the two premises and the conclusion. The word term comes from the Latin terminus, meaning "boundary" or "limit." In logic, a term names the class of things a statement is about — it marks the boundary of what is being discussed.

The three terms are identified by their roles relative to the conclusion:

Term Symbol Role in Conclusion Role in Premises
Major term P Predicate of the conclusion Appears in the major premise
Minor term S Subject of the conclusion Appears in the minor premise
Middle term M Does not appear in the conclusion Appears in both premises

Notice that the middle term is the only term that does not appear in the conclusion. Its entire function is to serve as the connecting link between the major and minor terms in the premises. Once the connection has been established, the middle term drops out, and the conclusion states the relationship between S and P directly.

Examining Each Term

The major term (P) gets its name from its position: it is the predicate of the conclusion — the larger or broader class in typical examples, though not always. It is called "major" because in the most common figures of the syllogism, the major term names the class that the conclusion affirms or denies about the subject. The premise in which P appears is called the major premise.

The minor term (S) is the subject of the conclusion — the class about which the conclusion makes a claim. The premise in which S appears is called the minor premise. Together, the conclusion says something about S in relation to P.

The middle term (M) is the logical workhorse of the syllogism. It does not appear in the conclusion at all. Its function is entirely structural: it links the other two terms by appearing in both premises, so that whatever relationship holds between M and P, and between S and M, can license an inference about the relationship between S and P. The Latin tradition sometimes called the middle term terminus medius — the "middle boundary." A syllogism without a proper middle term connecting the premises cannot produce a valid inference, as we will see when we study the fallacy of the undistributed middle.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Identifying All Three Terms
Major premise: All reptiles are cold-blooded animals.
Minor premise: All lizards are reptiles.
Conclusion: Therefore, all lizards are cold-blooded animals.

Analysis: The conclusion is "All lizards are cold-blooded animals." The subject of the conclusion is lizards — this is the minor term (S). The predicate of the conclusion is cold-blooded animals — this is the major term (P). The remaining term, reptiles, appears in both premises but not in the conclusion — this is the middle term (M).

The major premise is the one containing the major term (P): "All reptiles are cold-blooded animals." The minor premise is the one containing the minor term (S): "All lizards are reptiles."

Example 2 — A Different Configuration
Major premise: No mammals are cold-blooded.
Minor premise: All whales are mammals.
Conclusion: Therefore, no whales are cold-blooded.

Analysis: Conclusion subject: whales = minor term (S). Conclusion predicate: cold-blooded = major term (P). Term appearing in both premises: mammals = middle term (M). The syllogism is in standard form: major premise first, minor premise second.

Common Error — Identifying Terms by Position Rather Than Role

Students sometimes assume the first term mentioned in a syllogism must be the major term, or that the largest-sounding class must be the major term. Both assumptions are wrong. The major and minor terms are defined by their role in the conclusion, not by the order in which they appear or the size of the class they name.

Always start by finding the conclusion, then identify the subject (S = minor term) and predicate (P = major term). Whatever remains is the middle term (M).

Why the Middle Term Matters

The middle term is what distinguishes a syllogism from two unrelated premises placed side by side. Without a genuine middle term — a term that bridges both premises — no inference can legitimately follow. This is why logicians developed the concept of "distribution" of the middle term: for a syllogism to be valid, the middle term must be distributed (i.e., refer to all members of its class) in at least one premise. When it is not, we have the fallacy of the undistributed middle — an argument that looks like a syllogism but lacks the structural connection needed to support its conclusion.

Aristotle recognized this in the Prior Analytics (part of the Organon): the middle term is the key to syllogistic reasoning. He called the syllogism a form of reasoning in which "through two premises a third follows of necessity" — and the "through" is only possible because of the middle term's bridging function. Every valid syllogism, in every figure and mood, depends on this structural feature.

Practice Problems

Part A — Term Identification. For each syllogism below, identify the major term (P), minor term (S), and middle term (M). Then label which premise is the major and which is the minor.

  1. All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd. All numbers in set Q are prime numbers greater than 2. Therefore, all numbers in set Q are odd.
  2. No amphibians are warm-blooded. All frogs are amphibians. Therefore, no frogs are warm-blooded.
  3. Some philosophers are logicians. All logicians are careful thinkers. Therefore, some philosophers are careful thinkers.
  4. All perennial plants survive winter. No annual plants are perennial plants. Therefore, no annual plants survive winter.

Part B — Analysis. Consider this argument: "Every triangle has interior angles that sum to 180°. This shape is a triangle. Therefore, this shape has interior angles that sum to 180°." In 2–3 sentences, identify the three terms and explain what makes the middle term the logical "engine" of this inference — why would the conclusion not follow without it?

Part C — Construction. Write a valid categorical syllogism of your own. Label each of the three terms (major, minor, middle) and identify which premise is major and which is minor. Use a topic from science, history, or mathematics. Avoid politically charged or emotionally loaded subject matter.

Looking Ahead: Now that you can identify all three terms, the next lesson examines how the major and minor premises are structured — and how the position of the middle term within those premises determines the figure of the syllogism.
