Lesson 7.02c — From Argument to Essay

Learning objective: Students will translate a constructed valid compound argument into a well-formed philosophical essay, accurately expressing the logical structure in academic prose while preserving formal rigor and anticipating the most significant objection to the argument.

Orientation

The previous two lessons taught how to construct a valid argument from scratch. This lesson addresses how to write it. A valid argument expressed in natural language is the foundation of a philosophical essay, but the essay is more than the argument. It introduces the position and its significance, lays out the argument's steps in readable prose, anticipates the strongest objection, and responds to it. This lesson bridges formal logic and argumentative writing — the two disciplines that, together, are the aim of this course.

Intuitive Entry Point

You have almost certainly read something — a science article, a history essay, a philosophical tract — where you could feel the argument clearly through the prose, even though the author never labeled premises or wrote in standard form. The writing was clear because the author knew exactly what they were arguing and in what order. Clarity of logical structure produces clarity of prose. A muddy argument produces muddy writing. The lesson here is that getting the logic right first is the best preparation for writing well.

Five Elements of a Philosophical Essay Built on a Valid Argument

Essay Structure

  1. Introduction and thesis. State the position to be argued clearly in the first paragraph. A philosophical thesis is a claim, not a question or a topic. "This essay will argue that formal logic is indispensable to rigorous philosophical inquiry" is a thesis. The introduction should also briefly indicate the structure of the argument — how many steps it takes and what they establish.
  2. Sub-argument development. If a premise of the main argument requires support, develop that sub-argument before the main argument. Each sub-argument should have its own paragraph or section. The conclusion of the sub-argument (the intermediate conclusion) should be explicitly stated — do not leave the reader to infer it.
  3. Main argument. Present the main argument, naming the intermediate conclusion from the sub-argument as the premise it is. Then state the main conclusion explicitly. Use transitional language (therefore, it follows that, consequently) to mark the inferential step — do not bury the conclusion in the middle of a paragraph.
  4. Objection and response. Identify the most contestable premise of the entire compound argument and present the strongest version of an objection to it. Then respond. A response either (a) shows the objection misunderstands the premise, (b) defends the premise with additional evidence or reasoning, or (c) acknowledges the objection has force and qualifies the premise to limit the conclusion's scope. Do not choose a weak objection to knock down easily — the intellectual credibility of the essay depends on taking the best objection seriously.
  5. Conclusion. Restate the main conclusion in light of the argument and the objection-response. A one- to two-sentence closing statement is sufficient. Do not introduce new arguments in the conclusion.

Mapping Standard Form to Prose

The translation from standard-form syllogism to prose follows a reliable pattern:

  • Universal affirmative (A) — "All S are P" becomes: "Every [S] is [P]," or "S is, in all its instances, P," or "[S] always involves [P]."
  • Universal negative (E) — "No S are P" becomes: "No [S] is [P]," or "[S] and [P] are mutually exclusive," or "Nothing that is [S] can be [P]."
  • Particular affirmative (I) — "Some S are P" becomes: "At least some [S] are [P]," or "There are [S] that count as [P]."
  • Particular negative (O) — "Some S are not P" becomes: "Not all [S] are [P]," or "At least some [S] fall outside the class of [P]."

Preserve the logical content precisely while allowing natural phrasing. The goal is prose that a reader would not recognize as formal logic — but that a logician could reconstruct into standard form without any loss.

Common Error — Losing the Universal in the Prose

When students translate a universal affirmative premise into prose, they sometimes soften it: "Valid arguments tend to preserve truth" instead of "All valid arguments preserve truth." The word "tend" converts an A statement into something weaker, changing the logical content of the premise. If your argument depends on an A premise, write it as an A premise — every instance, not most.

Connection to Argument

This lesson closes the full arc of the course. You began by learning what an argument is. You have spent a semester learning the formal conditions that make arguments valid, the operations that transform statements, the structures that connect syllogisms into chains, and the skills to find and evaluate arguments wherever they appear. Now you can do all of it in the constructive direction: begin with a position, build a valid compound argument for it, and write it into an essay that a careful reader can follow and a rigorous critic can evaluate. That is the complete skill of the course.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Recognition). The following paragraph is a translation of a valid syllogism into prose. Identify the major premise, minor premise, and conclusion as they appear in the prose. Then write the argument in standard categorical form.

"Any reasoning process that can produce contradictory conclusions from the same premises is unreliable as a guide to truth. The method of pure introspection can produce contradictory conclusions from the same premises, since different individuals introspecting about the same experience regularly reach opposite results. Consequently, pure introspection is unreliable as a guide to truth."

Problem 2 (Analysis). The following essay paragraph contains a logical error in the prose translation. Identify it: is the error a term drift between syllogisms, a weakened universal, or a misplaced conclusion indicator?

"All definitions that clarify the boundaries of a concept are useful for logical analysis. Aristotle's definition of the syllogism clarifies what distinguishes valid inference from merely persuasive speech. Therefore, all persuasive speech is a failed attempt at valid inference."

Problem 3 (Construction). Take the compound argument you constructed in Problem 3 of Lesson 7.02b. Write a short philosophical essay (three to four paragraphs) presenting the argument: (1) an opening paragraph stating the thesis and signaling the argument's structure; (2) a paragraph developing the sub-argument; (3) a paragraph presenting the main argument and stating the conclusion; (4) one to two sentences identifying the most contestable premise and your response to the best objection to it.
