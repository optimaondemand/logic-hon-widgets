What Is an Argument?

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify whether a passage contains an argument and explain what distinguishes an argument from other uses of language.

Orientation: Where We Are

Every day, you are surrounded by people trying to get you to believe things — that a product works, that a policy is wise, that a claim about the world is true. Some of these appeals give you genuine reasons to believe. Others merely apply pressure: repetition, emotional appeal, the weight of a confident tone. Being able to tell the difference is not a narrow academic skill. It is a condition for thinking for yourself. An argument, in the sense this course studies, is language doing the first kind of work: offering reasons in support of a claim, and inviting you to judge whether those reasons hold up. Learning to recognize that structure — and eventually to test it, and to build it yourself — is the whole business of this course.

This is the first lesson of the course, so there is nothing behind us yet — but there is a great deal ahead. Before we can assess whether an argument is valid or sound, before we can diagnose a fallacy, before we can construct a syllogism, we need to know what an argument is. That turns out to be a more precise and more interesting question than it first appears. Everything in this course — every tool, every test, every formal rule — will be applied to arguments. This lesson is where the work begins.

Intuitive Entry: You Already Do This

Think of a time you tried to convince someone of something — a family member, a friend, a teacher — and you did not simply repeat yourself louder or give up. You gave them reasons. Maybe you were arguing that a rule was unfair, or that a particular explanation of an event was wrong, or that a decision someone was making was likely to go badly. Whatever the specific case, you were doing something with a definite structure: you were saying, in effect, "Believe this — and here is why."

That structure — a claim, supported by reasons — is an argument in the logical sense. You have been making arguments for as long as you have been reasoning. The formal study of logic takes that everyday practice and makes its structure visible, precise, and examinable. By the end of this course, you will be able to analyze the structure of an argument the way a skilled mechanic can analyze the structure of an engine — not just to say whether it "works," but to explain exactly why it works or where it fails.

The Formal Concept: What an Argument Is

The word argument comes from the Latin argumentum, derived from arguere, meaning "to make clear" or "to prove." In ordinary speech, the word often means a dispute or quarrel — two people shouting at each other about whose turn it is. That usage is common but logically irrelevant. In the tradition of formal logic, an argument has a precise and different meaning:

An argument is a set of statements in which some statements (the premises) are offered as reasons for accepting another statement (the conclusion).

Every genuine argument has two kinds of parts. The premises (from Latin praemissa, "sent before") are the statements that do the supporting work — they are the reasons being offered. The conclusion (from Latin concludere, "to close" or "to enclose") is the statement the argument is trying to establish — the claim the reasoning closes in on. The logical relationship between them is one of support: the premises are put forward as grounds for believing the conclusion.

Notice what this definition does not say. It does not say the premises must be true. It does not say the reasoning must be correct. An argument is identified by its structure — the attempt to support one claim with others — not by whether it succeeds. We will spend much of this course learning the tools for evaluating whether arguments succeed.

What an Argument Is Not

Not every set of statements is an argument. Language has many purposes, and reasoning through a claim is only one of them. Here are the most common uses of language that are not arguments:

  • Bare assertions: A single claim, or a set of claims, offered without any supporting reason. "The climate is changing." "That was a significant battle." These may be true, important, or worth discussing — but without reasons offered in support, they are not arguments.
  • Questions: "What caused the decline of the Western Roman Empire?" A question invites an answer; it does not itself advance a claim or offer support for one.
  • Commands and instructions: "Read the first three chapters by Thursday." "Do not exceed the recommended dosage." Commands tell you what to do; they do not give you reasons to believe something is true.
  • Expressions of feeling or attitude: "I find that prospect alarming." "What a remarkable discovery." These convey a speaker's state of mind, but they are not attempts to establish a conclusion through reasoning.
  • Descriptions and narratives: Recounting what happened, listing features of something, or telling a story — none of these constitute an argument unless they are organized to support a conclusion.

The key test: does this passage contain a claim that the author is trying to establish, and is there an attempt to give reasons for that claim? If both conditions are met, there is an argument. If either is missing, there is not.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — A Clear Argument

"The population of honeybees in North America has declined sharply over the past two decades. Since honeybees are essential pollinators for many food crops, this decline poses a genuine risk to the food supply."

Analysis: This is an argument. There are two premises: (1) honeybee populations have declined sharply, and (2) honeybees are essential pollinators for food crops. These are offered in support of a conclusion: the decline poses a risk to the food supply. The word "since" signals that what follows is a reason.

Example 2 — Not an Argument (Description)

"The Roman Colosseum was completed around 80 CE. It could hold between 50,000 and 80,000 spectators. The arena floor was made of wood and covered with sand."

Analysis: This passage contains only descriptions and facts. There is no claim being established through reasoning — no conclusion that the reader is being asked to accept on the basis of the other statements. It is informative, but it is not an argument.

Common Error — Mistaking Assertion for Argument

"Exercise is clearly beneficial. Everyone should exercise regularly. The evidence is overwhelming."

Analysis: This passage makes claims — some of them strong claims — but it does not give reasons for accepting them. Phrases like "clearly" and "the evidence is overwhelming" gesture at reasons without actually providing any. Asserting a claim forcefully, or asserting that evidence exists, is not the same as presenting that evidence as a premise. This is a common error: confident-sounding language can mimic argument without actually constituting one.

Why This Matters: Arguments and the Pursuit of Truth

Arguments are not merely rhetorical devices. They are the formal mechanism through which rational beings pursue truth together. When you give an argument, you are not just trying to cause a change in someone's behavior — you are presenting reasons and implicitly inviting evaluation: "Here is why I think this is true. Do these reasons hold up?" This is fundamentally different from persuasion through force, emotional pressure, or social repetition. An argument addresses the intellect; it respects the other person's capacity to reason.

This is why the ability to identify arguments matters. If you cannot tell the difference between a passage that contains an argument and one that merely makes claims — or one that persuades through emotion or repetition — you are vulnerable to being moved by things that do not actually give you reason to update your beliefs. The skill you are building in this lesson is foundational to intellectual self-defense.

The Aristotelian Lineage

The formal study of argument begins with Aristotle (384–322 BCE), a student of Plato and one of the most systematic thinkers in the Western tradition. His logical writings — collected under the title Organon (Greek: "instrument" or "tool") — established argument as a subject of rigorous inquiry. The Organon includes works on categories, on predication, on demonstration, and — most directly relevant to this course — on the syllogism, the canonical form of deductive argument that we will study in depth.

What Aristotle recognized is that reasoning has a structure that can be examined independently of its content. The same logical form can carry true premises or false ones, valid reasoning or invalid reasoning, and the formal properties can be evaluated on their own terms. That insight — that logic studies the form of arguments, not merely their content — is still the foundation of the discipline 2,400 years later. This is not a museum piece. Every time you identify whether a passage contains an argument, you are doing what Aristotle did systematically: attending to logical form.

Practice Problems

Part A — Recognition. For each passage below, write "Argument" or "Not an argument." If it is an argument, identify the conclusion in one sentence.

  1. "Water boils at 100°C at sea level. At higher altitudes, the atmospheric pressure is lower, so water boils at a lower temperature."
  2. "The library closes at 9 p.m. on weekdays and at 6 p.m. on weekends. Closed on holidays."
  3. "Why do migratory birds return to the same nesting sites each year?"
  4. "Do not leave the lid off the container; the solution will evaporate."
  5. "The bridge was built in 1887 and has not been significantly reinforced since. Therefore, it should be inspected before heavy vehicle traffic is permitted."

Part B — Analysis. Read the following passage and explain in 2–3 sentences whether it contains an argument. If it does, identify the premises and conclusion. If it does not, explain what kind of language use it is instead.

"That mountain range is simply stunning. The peaks rise above 4,000 meters and are snow-capped year-round. Glaciers flow down the eastern slopes, and in spring, the valleys fill with wildflowers."

Part C — Reflection. In your own words (3–5 sentences), explain the difference between the everyday meaning of "argument" and the logical meaning. Why does the distinction matter for this course?
