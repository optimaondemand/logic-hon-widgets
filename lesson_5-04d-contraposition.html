Lesson 5.04d
Contraposition

Learning Objective: Students will be able to apply the operation of contraposition to all four categorical statement types, correctly identify which types yield a valid contrapositive (A and O) and which do not (E and I), and explain the two-step procedure that produces the contrapositive.

Orientation

You have now studied three immediate inference operations: the Square of Opposition relationships, conversion, and obversion. Each rearranges or reexpresses a categorical statement in a logically specified way. Contraposition is the last of these operations. It is a distinct operation from both of the others — it does not go through obversion, and unlike obversion it never changes the statement's quality — but like obversion it works with term complements, and like conversion it swaps the positions of subject and predicate. The result is an operation that is valid for A and O statements, invalid for E and I — a pattern almost the mirror image of conversion's validity profile.

Intuitive Entry Point

In everyday conditional reasoning, most people immediately accept the following: "If it is a mammal, then it breathes air" entails "If it does not breathe air, then it is not a mammal." This move — replacing the original condition and result with their negations, in reverse order — is called the contrapositive, and it is logically equivalent to the original conditional. The categorical logic operation of contraposition is the same principle applied to A, E, I, and O statements. The fact that we find this move so natural in hypothetical reasoning is evidence that the underlying logical structure is genuinely equivalent — not a rhetorical trick.

The Contraposition Procedure

Contraposition forms the contrapositive of a categorical statement by two sequential steps. Unlike obversion, contraposition never changes the quality of the statement — "All" stays "All," "Some...are not" stays "Some...are not." Only the terms and their positions change.

Step 1 — Replace subject and predicate terms of the original with their complements: Substitute non-S for S and non-P for P, in the same positions they originally occupied. The quality and quantity are untouched.
Step 2 — Interchange the subject and predicate: Swap the positions of the two now-complemented terms.

The net effect: replace the subject with the complement of the original predicate, and replace the predicate with the complement of the original subject. The quality remains the same as the original throughout — it is never reversed, because obversion (which does reverse quality) is not a part of contraposition.

Written directly: for "All S are P," the contrapositive is "All non-P are non-S."

Type Original Contrapositive Valid?
A All S are P All non-P are non-S Valid — same truth value
E No S are P No non-P are non-S Invalid — not equivalent to the original
I Some S are P Some non-P are non-S Invalid — not equivalent to the original
O Some S are not P Some non-P are not non-S Valid — same truth value

Why A and O are valid: "All S are P" means every member of S is also a member of P — which is exactly the same as saying nothing outside P (nothing in non-P) can be a member of S, so everything in non-P must be a member of non-S. That is precisely "All non-P are non-S": the same fact, read from the other direction. Similarly, "Some S are not P" tells you there is some individual that is in S but in non-P; that same individual is therefore in non-P but not in non-S (since it is in S). That is precisely "Some non-P are not non-S."

Why E and I are invalid: "No S are P" tells you only that S and P do not overlap — it says nothing about whether S and P together cover the entire universe of discourse. "No non-P are non-S" would require exactly that (that everything is in S or in P), which the original never claimed. The reasoning fails the same way for I: "Some S are P" tells you S and P overlap somewhere, but says nothing about whether anything exists outside both classes — which is what "Some non-P are non-S" would require. In both cases, the complement-and-swap formula produces a statement that asserts something new the original never guaranteed.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — A Statement Contraposition

Original (A): "All whales are mammals."

Step 1 — Replace subject and predicate terms of the original with their complements: "All non-whales are non-mammals."

Step 2 — Interchange the subject and predicate: "All non-mammals are non-whales."

Contrapositive: "All non-mammals are non-whales."

Check: If everything that is a whale is a mammal, then anything that is not a mammal is certainly not a whale. The contrapositive states nothing beyond what the original claimed.

Example 2 — O Statement Contraposition

Original (O): "Some geometric figures are not squares."

Step 1 — Replace subject and predicate terms of the original with their complements: "Some geometric figures are non-squares."

Step 2 — Interchange the subject and predicate: "Some non-squares are geometric figures."

Contrapositive: "Some non-squares are not non-geometric figures" — equivalently, "Some non-squares are geometric figures."

Check: The original says some geometric figures fall outside the class of squares. The contrapositive says some of the things outside the class of squares are geometric figures. These are equivalent.

Common Error — Applying Contraposition to an E or I Statement

Attempting to form the contrapositive of "No reptiles are mammals" by the same formula gives "No non-mammals are non-reptiles" — but this is NOT equivalent to the original. As the reasoning above shows, "No S are P" only tells you S and P don't overlap; it tells you nothing about whether S and P together cover everything else, which is what the derived statement would require. If you apply the swap-and-complement formula mechanically to E or I statements, you will produce a statement that is not logically equivalent. Always check whether the statement type is A or O before treating the result of contraposition as valid.

Contraposition and Hypothetical Reasoning

The categorical contrapositive corresponds directly to the hypothetical contrapositive: "If P then Q" is logically equivalent to "If not-Q then not-P." This connection is not a coincidence — both follow from the same underlying principle about class inclusion. "All S are P" can be read as "For any x, if x is S then x is P," and its contrapositive "All non-P are non-S" reads as "For any x, if x is not P then x is not S." Recognizing this connection allows you to apply contraposition fluently in arguments expressed in conditional form, not just in the standard A/O categorical forms.

Connection to Argument

Contraposition is especially useful when an argument needs to be rewritten in a different direction to connect with other premises. If you have a premise of the form "All S are P" and you need a statement about non-P things, the contrapositive "All non-P are non-S" gives you exactly that, with no loss of logical content. Detecting when an arguer has illicitly applied contraposition to an E or I statement — treating a weaker result as equivalent to the original — is a valuable error-detection skill in analyzing complex arguments.

Practice Problems

Part A — Apply Contraposition. For each statement, state whether contraposition yields a valid equivalent. If valid, show the two steps and state the contrapositive. If invalid, explain which step fails and why.

  1. "All protons are positively charged particles."
  2. "No sedimentary rocks are igneous rocks."
  3. "Some philosophical arguments are not formally valid."
  4. "Some historical empires are civilizations that left written records."
  5. "All deciduous trees are trees that lose their leaves seasonally."
  6. "No inert gases are reactive elements."

Part B — Identify the Operation. Each item shows an original statement and a derived statement. Identify which immediate inference operation was used (conversion, obversion, or contraposition) and evaluate whether it was applied correctly.

  1. Original: "All vertebrates are animals with backbones." Derived: "All animals without backbones are non-vertebrates."
  2. Original: "No reptiles are endothermic animals." Derived: "All reptiles are non-endothermic animals."
  3. Original: "Some historical documents are not authentic records." Derived: "Some non-authentic records are historical documents."
  4. Original: "All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd numbers." Derived: "Some odd numbers are prime numbers greater than 2."

Part C — Synthesis. Take the statement "All valid syllogisms are arguments that preserve truth from premises to conclusion." Apply each of the three operations — conversion, obversion, and contraposition — to it in turn. For each, state whether the operation is valid for this statement type, show the result, and write one sentence explaining what the result tells us that the original statement does or does not tell us.

Looking Ahead: You now have a complete toolkit for immediate inferences: the Square of Opposition relationships, conversion, obversion, and contraposition. Next week brings a different kind of structural analysis — mood — which characterizes entire syllogisms by the sequence of statement types in their premises and conclusion, opening the door to a systematic account of which syllogistic forms are valid.
