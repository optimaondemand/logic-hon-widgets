Lesson 3.04d — Subalternation and Superalternation

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning  |  ELA.11.C.5.1 — Improve reasoning through revision

Learning Objective: Students will be able to state the rules of subalternation and superalternation, apply them to infer truth values of particular statements from universal statements (and vice versa), and complete a full inference analysis using all five Square of Opposition relationships together.

Where We Are

The three previous lessons covered the diagonal (contradictory) and horizontal (contrary and subcontrary) relationships on the Square. This lesson completes the Square with the vertical relationships: subalternation, which runs from the universal statements downward to the particular statements, and superalternation, which runs upward. Together, all five relationships now give you a complete inference map — the ability to determine, from any known truth value on the Square, what follows for each of the other three statements.

Intuitive Entry Point

Think about what it means to know something universal. If you know that every single mammal is warm-blooded, then you certainly know that some mammals are warm-blooded — the particular claim is guaranteed by the universal. The stronger claim implies the weaker. If all mammals are warm-blooded, then at minimum some are. The universal carries the particular inside it.

Now consider the reverse. If you discover that it is false that some mammals are warm-blooded — that not a single mammal is warm-blooded — what does this tell you about the universal? If no mammals are warm-blooded (the particular is false), then certainly not all mammals are warm-blooded. Falsity of the particular refutes the corresponding universal. The particular cannot be false while the universal is true — the universal would be exposed as wrong the moment any counterexample appears.

Subalternation: Truth Flows Downward

The term subalternation comes from the Latin sub (under) and alter (other) — the statement "under" the other in the vertical pairing. On the Square, the particular statements (I and O) are the subalterns of their corresponding universals (A and E).

Subalternation Rule: If the universal is true, the particular must be true.

— If A ("All S are P") is true, then I ("Some S are P") is true.
— If E ("No S are P") is true, then O ("Some S are not P") is true.

The reasoning: a universal claim covers all members of S. If all S are P, then certainly at least one S is P — the I statement follows necessarily. If no S are P, then certainly at least one S is not P — the O statement follows. The universal guarantees the particular because the particular asserts only the minimum that the universal guarantees.

Superalternation: Falsity Flows Upward

The superalternation relationship is the contrapositive of subalternation. If the universal's truth guarantees the particular's truth, then the particular's falsity guarantees the universal's falsity.

Superalternation Rule: If the particular is false, the universal must be false.

— If I ("Some S are P") is false, then A ("All S are P") is false.
— If O ("Some S are not P") is false, then E ("No S are P") is false.

The reasoning: if "Some S are P" is false — if not a single S is P — then certainly not all S are P. The universal cannot survive the falsity of its particular. If the minimal claim (at least one) fails, the maximal claim (all) fails more severely.

The Undetermined Cases

The vertical relationships do not work symmetrically. Truth flows from universal down to particular; falsity flows from particular up to universal. The remaining two cases — falsity of the universal and truth of the particular — do not determine their counterpart:

  • If A is false → I is undetermined. The A could be false because most S are P (I might be true) or because no S are P (I would be false). You cannot tell from A's falsity alone.
  • If I is true → A is undetermined. Knowing some S are P does not tell you whether all S are P. The particular affirmative is compatible with the universal affirmative being either true or false.
  • If E is false → O is undetermined. Same structure as the A–I case on the negative side.
  • If O is true → E is undetermined. Knowing some S are not P does not tell you whether all S are not P.

Worked Example — Subalternation

Given (TRUE): "All right angles are angles measuring exactly 90 degrees." (A statement)

By subalternation: "Some right angles are angles measuring exactly 90 degrees." (I statement) — True. Since the universal is true, the particular must be true. This is not a new discovery — it follows immediately from the universal by subalternation.

Worked Example — Superalternation

Given (FALSE): "Some triangles are figures with more than three sides." (I statement — false, since all triangles have exactly three sides)

By superalternation: "All triangles are figures with more than three sides." (A statement) — False. If the particular is false, the universal must be false. No triangle has more than three sides, so the "some" claim fails, and the "all" claim fails even more severely.

A Note on Classical vs. Modern Logic

Subalternation depends on the assumption that the subject class is non-empty. If "All S are P" is true but S is an empty class (there are no S's), then "Some S are P" would be false — because there are no members of S to be P. In classical (traditional) logic, we follow Aristotle in assuming that subject classes are non-empty. Under this assumption, subalternation holds. Modern (Boolean) logic does not make this assumption, and subalternation does not hold in the modern square. This course follows the classical interpretation.

Practice

Part A — Subalternation. For each universal statement, determine what follows by subalternation. Write the particular statement and its truth value.

  1. Given TRUE: "All Euclidean triangles are figures with interior angles summing to 180 degrees." → I statement?
  2. Given TRUE: "No composite number is a prime number." → O statement?
  3. Given FALSE: "All swans are white birds." → I statement? (apply superalternation logic)

Part B — Superalternation. For each particular statement, determine what follows by superalternation.

  1. Given FALSE: "Some equilateral triangles are scalene triangles." → A statement?
  2. Given FALSE: "Some prime numbers are even numbers greater than 2." → A statement?

Part C — Full Square Analysis. Use the complete Square of Opposition to determine the truth value of all four statements, given the starting condition. Use the same subject and predicate terms for all four statements. Name the relationship used for each inference.

Given: The E statement is TRUE: "No act of genuine deception is an act of honesty."

  2. I statement: "Some acts of genuine deception are acts of honesty." → Truth value and relationship?
  3. O statement: "Some acts of genuine deception are not acts of honesty." → Truth value and relationship?

Part D — Synthesis. In 4–5 sentences, explain why the phrase "truth flows downward, falsity flows upward" accurately describes the subalternation relationship. Use an original example to illustrate each direction. Do not merely restate the rule — explain why the rule makes logical sense.
