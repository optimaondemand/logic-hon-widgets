Lesson 1.02b — Parts of an Argument: Premises and Conclusions

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare the development of multiple arguments; evaluate their effectiveness and validity  |  ELA.K12.EE.3.1 — Make inferences to support comprehension

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the premises and conclusion of a given argument, using both indicator words and logical relationships, and to write arguments in standard form.

Orientation: Where We Are

We now know what an argument is and what it is for. The next task is to develop precision in reading arguments — to be able to look at a piece of prose that contains an argument and identify exactly which part is the conclusion and which parts are the premises. This is not a trivial skill. Arguments in natural language are often expressed in ways that obscure their structure, and the ability to reconstruct that structure clearly is foundational to everything that follows: evaluating validity, identifying fallacies, constructing syllogisms. This lesson introduces the two essential components of every argument and the standard form used to make their structure explicit.

Intuitive Entry: Support and What It Supports

Consider a simple situation: you want to convince a friend that the shorter hiking trail is the better choice. You might say: "The longer trail is muddy after rain, and it rained yesterday. The shorter trail is paved and offers the same views. Let's take the shorter trail." You have offered two reasons in support of a recommendation. The recommendation is what you are trying to establish; the reasons are what you are offering to support it.

Every argument has this shape: some statements are doing the supporting, and one statement is being supported. In logic, these roles have precise names and a formal relationship between them. Recognizing that relationship — not just in obvious cases but in complex prose — is the skill this lesson builds.

The Conclusion

The conclusion is the statement that the argument is attempting to establish. It is the claim the author wants you to accept on the basis of the reasoning. The word derives from the Latin concludere: "to close" or "to enclose." The conclusion is what the reasoning closes in on — the destination the argument is trying to reach.

Every argument has exactly one conclusion. (Some arguments may have sub-conclusions — intermediate claims that function as premises for a larger conclusion — but each argument, considered as a whole, is aiming at one target claim.)

When identifying the conclusion, the key question is: What is the author trying to get me to believe? What is the main claim being advanced? The conclusion is the answer to that question.

Conclusion indicator words — words that frequently signal that what follows is the conclusion — include:

  • therefore, thus, hence, so
  • it follows that, we may conclude that, which shows that
  • consequently, as a result, this proves that, this means that

The Premises

The premises are the statements offered as reasons for accepting the conclusion. The word comes from the Latin praemissa, from prae- (before) and mittere (to send): "what is sent before." Premises go before the conclusion logically — they provide the support that the conclusion rests on — even when the conclusion is stated first in a sentence.

An argument can have one premise or many. What makes something a premise is its function: it is being offered in support of the conclusion. The test is relational: is this statement being put forward as a reason to accept another statement?

Premise indicator words — words that frequently signal that what follows is a premise — include:

  • because, since, for, as
  • given that, on account of, inasmuch as
  • the reason is that, as evidenced by, in light of the fact that

Standard Form

Standard form is a convention for writing out an argument so its structure is completely explicit. Every premise is listed and numbered; the conclusion comes last and is labeled separately. This is the backbone of the course — nearly every analysis task this year will begin with translating an argument into standard form.

The convention:

Standard Form Template P1: [First premise]
P2: [Second premise]
P3: [Third premise, if any]
∴ C: [Conclusion]

The symbol ∴ (three dots in a triangle) is the traditional symbol for "therefore." It marks the conclusion. When you see it, you are looking at what the premises are trying to establish.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Argument with Clear Indicators

"Since the sample was collected at low tide, and since the species in question is only present in intertidal zones during low-tide periods, the sample almost certainly contains specimens of that species."

Identification: The word "since" appears twice, signaling two premises. The main claim being established is the conclusion. In standard form:

P1: The sample was collected at low tide.
P2: The species in question is only present in intertidal zones during low-tide periods.
∴ C: The sample almost certainly contains specimens of that species.
Example 2 — Argument with No Indicator Words

"The section of the wall on the north side shows more weathering than any other section. North-facing surfaces receive direct rain during the region's prevailing storms. That section will require repair first."

Identification: No indicator words appear. The reader must ask: what is being established, and what is being offered as support? The first two sentences are factual claims about the wall and weather patterns. The third sentence is the recommendation being argued for — it is what the first two statements are offered in support of. In standard form:

P1: The north-side section of the wall shows more weathering than any other section.
P2: North-facing surfaces receive direct rain during the region's prevailing storms.
∴ C: The north-side section will require repair first.

Note that the conclusion comes last grammatically here, which is natural. But the logical priority runs from premises to conclusion regardless of sentence order.

Common Error — Narration Mistaken for Argument

"The expedition set out in March. They crossed the first range by early April. By late April, the team had reached the plateau and begun their survey."

Analysis: This passage looks structured — one event leads to another — but there is no logical support relationship. No statement is being offered as a reason to accept another. The sequence is temporal (one thing happened after another), not logical (one thing gives grounds for believing another). This is narration, not argument. Converting it to standard form is not possible because there is no conclusion to place at the bottom — there is no claim being established through reasoning.

The Logical Relationship vs. the Grammatical One

A critical point deserves emphasis: the relationship between premises and conclusion is logical, not merely grammatical. The conclusion can appear at the beginning of a sentence, in the middle, or at the end. Consider: "We should take the highway, since the surface streets are flooded and the highway adds only four minutes." The conclusion ("we should take the highway") appears before the premises. The word "since" signals that what follows are the reasons — the premises — even though they come after the conclusion in the text.

Do not be misled by word order. Ask the logical question: which statement is being supported, and which statements are doing the supporting? The answer to that question gives you the structure of the argument, regardless of how the sentences are arranged.

Connection to Argument Assessment

Identifying premises and conclusions is not an end in itself — it is the prerequisite for everything that comes next. Before you can evaluate whether an argument is valid, you must know what the argument claims and what it rests on. Before you can identify a fallacy, you must be able to see whether the stated reasoning actually supports the stated conclusion. Standard form makes all of this explicit and auditable. When an argument is in standard form, every part of it is visible and can be examined on its own terms.

Practice Problems

Part A — Recognition. Each sentence below contains either a conclusion indicator or a premise indicator. Identify which type of indicator is present and what it signals in that sentence.

  1. "The reservoir is at 40% capacity; therefore, water restrictions are warranted."
  2. "Since the temperature dropped below freezing overnight, the pipes may have developed cracks."
  3. "We may conclude that the experiment was successful, given that all three variables produced the predicted results."
  4. "The fossil is located in a stratum dated to approximately 65 million years ago; hence the organism lived before the end-Cretaceous event."

Part B — Standard Form. Rewrite each argument in standard form (P1, P2, ∴ C). Some arguments have only one premise; some have two. Paraphrase if necessary to make each premise a clear, complete statement.

  1. "The train departs at 6:45, and it takes thirty minutes to reach the station, so we need to leave by 6:10 at the latest."
  2. "Birds that overwinter in a given location develop stronger cold tolerance than migratory populations. The sparrows in this area overwinter here. Therefore, they have stronger cold tolerance than migratory sparrows."
  3. "That bridge is rated for vehicles under 5 tons. This delivery truck weighs 7 tons. It should take the alternate route."

Part C — Inference (No Indicators). The following arguments contain no indicator words. Identify the conclusion and premises by reasoning about the logical relationship. Write each in standard form.

  1. "Light travels faster than sound. You see a lightning flash before you hear the thunder."
  2. "The roots of the oak tree have cracked the pavement near the sidewalk. City code requires property owners to repair pavement damage caused by trees on their land. The property owner is responsible for repairing this section of pavement."

Part D — Error Identification. Which of the following is NOT an argument? Explain in one sentence why.

  1. "The archive contains letters from 1843, 1847, and 1851, along with a set of maps from the same period."
  2. "The archive contains letters from 1843 through 1851. Since that period coincides with the construction of the railway, the archive likely documents the construction project."
