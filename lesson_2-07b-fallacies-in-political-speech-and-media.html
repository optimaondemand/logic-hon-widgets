Lesson 2.07b
Fallacies in Political Speech and Media

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify fallacies in political speech and news media, while maintaining awareness that fallacy-spotting does not replace substantive engagement with the arguments themselves.

Orientation: The Oldest Debate

Political speech is not a new environment for informal reasoning. It is, in fact, the original one. The Sophists whom Socrates opposed in fifth-century Athens were not philosophers in the academic sense — they were teachers of persuasion, hired by wealthy Athenians to teach their sons how to argue effectively in the public assembly and the courts. Socrates’ famous objection was that the Sophists taught their students how to win arguments without teaching them how to make their arguments true. The gap between effective and valid — between persuasive and correct — was his central concern. It remains ours.

Political speech and news media are not the same thing, and they should not be treated as identical. But they share an important feature: both are high-stakes environments where the appearance of argument matters enormously, where audiences are often reasoning quickly and under emotional pressure, and where fallacious reasoning — when it succeeds — can have significant real-world consequences. These are exactly the conditions under which your fallacy catalogue is most useful.

A note on balance. This lesson analyzes the structure of fallacious reasoning in political speech, not the content of any particular political position. The same fallacies appear across the political spectrum, across media organizations of every editorial orientation, and in the speech of public figures of all kinds. The goal here is structural analysis, not partisan critique. Where examples describe a type of argument, they describe it as a form — the kind of argument that could be made by any speaker, on any side. Keep that framing in place as you work through the lesson.

Case Studies: Fallacies from History and Fiction

Every fallacy this lesson touches on — straw man, ad hominem, false dilemma, appeal to popularity, red herring, correlation mistaken for causation — you have already studied and can already define. Political speech and news media do not introduce new fallacies; they are simply high-stakes settings where the fallacies you already know reappear, often spread across a longer public exchange rather than packed into a single sentence. Rather than re-defining each one, this section curates a case from history or fiction for each, so you can practice spotting the pattern somewhere more layered than a textbook example.

1. Straw Man — The Ratification Debates (1787–1788)

Case During the debate over ratifying the U.S. Constitution, some opponents argued that the proposed federal government would contain no meaningful check on its own power and would inevitably produce a tyranny no different from the monarchy the colonies had just thrown off.

The Constitution's defenders had in fact proposed a structure built specifically to limit power: enumerated federal powers, three separate branches, and a system of checks between them, with a Bill of Rights added within two years of ratification. Casting the proposal as "no protection at all" replaced a carefully constrained structure with an exaggerated version that was far easier to attack than the original.

2. Ad Hominem — Ignaz Semmelweis and Handwashing

Case In the 1840s, physician Ignaz Semmelweis presented data showing that maternity-ward deaths dropped sharply when doctors washed their hands before examining patients. Much of the medical establishment dismissed the proposal — not by examining the data, but by pointing to Semmelweis's abrasive manner and his standing as an outsider to the Viennese medical elite.

Whether Semmelweis was easy to work with has no bearing on whether his mortality statistics were accurate. Dismissing a claim by attacking the claimant's temperament, rather than his evidence, delayed a life-saving practice for decades. The case is a standard one in the history of medicine precisely because the cost of the fallacy was so measurable.

3. False Dilemma — Loyalist Rhetoric During the American Revolution

Case Some loyalist pamphleteers framed the colonial situation as a stark choice: remain loyal subjects of the Crown, or descend into lawless anarchy with no functioning government at all.

The framing suppressed the option that colonists actually pursued — organized self-governance under new constitutions and, eventually, a federal structure with its own checks on power. "Loyalty or anarchy" left out the entire category of "orderly self-government," which is precisely what happened.

4. Appeal to Popularity — Geocentrism Before Galileo

Case For centuries, the near-universal consensus that the Earth stood still at the center of the cosmos was treated as itself a reason to believe it — every reputable astronomer, philosopher, and institution agreed, so the model was assumed correct.

Universal agreement is a fact about belief, not a fact about the heavens. When Galileo's telescopic observations (published in the Sidereus Nuncius, 1610) conflicted with the consensus, the appropriate response was to examine the observations — not to treat the size of the existing consensus as itself a refutation.

5. Red Herring — The Socratic Dialogues

Case In Plato's Euthyphro, Socrates asks Euthyphro for a definition of piety. When Socrates presses on a weakness in the definition offered, Euthyphro repeatedly shifts to a different, related question — citing his own confidence and experience — rather than repairing or defending the definition under scrutiny.

Plato is dramatizing the move for philosophical purposes, but the pattern is exactly the one you will meet in live debates, press conferences, and interviews: a hard question is met not with an answer but with a pivot to an adjacent topic the speaker finds more comfortable. The practice question is always the same: what was the original question, and was it actually answered?

6. Correlation Framed as Causation — Ice Cream Sales and Drowning

Case A classic statistics example: monthly data show that ice cream sales and drowning deaths rise and fall together throughout the year. A careless headline could report that "ice cream consumption is linked to drowning."

Both variables are driven by a third factor — warm weather, which increases both ice cream purchases and swimming. Neither causes the other. News stories routinely report this kind of correlation using language ("linked to," "associated with") that implies causation without establishing it. Readers who stop at the headline absorb the causal inference; readers who check the methodology often find the correlation carefully hedged. Identifying which one you have read is a practical skill.

The Virtue Angle: Intellectual Courage

There is a specific intellectual virtue that this lesson calls for, and it is worth naming explicitly. Intellectual courage, as Lesson 2.08a will define it, is the capacity to apply the same standard of scrutiny to sources you are predisposed to agree with as to sources you are predisposed to distrust.

This is considerably harder than spotting fallacies in sources you already distrust. When a source you find credible and congenial commits a straw man, you are less likely to notice — not because you are dishonest, but because cognitive ease and prior trust soften critical scrutiny. The practical test of the skills in this lesson is not whether you can identify fallacies in sources you already dislike. It is whether you can identify them in sources whose conclusions you find correct or whose values align with your own. That application is the harder one, and it is also the more important one.

A Practical Framework for News Consumption

When consuming news, opinion writing, or political speech, two questions will take you far:

  1. What claim is being made? Identify the conclusion — the specific assertion the text is trying to establish in your mind.
  2. What reasons are given? Look for premises: facts, evidence, arguments that are actually offered in support of the conclusion.

If the answer to question two is "none" — if the text contains only assertions, emotional appeals, authority claims, or distraction — that is meaningful information. It does not necessarily mean the conclusion is false. It means the argument has not been made, and you are being asked to believe the conclusion without one. What you do with that information is your judgment to exercise.

Practice: Structural Identification

Read each item below. Identify (a) which fallacy from your catalogue the passage commits, (b) which fallacy category it belongs to (relevance, ambiguity, or presumption), and (c) one sentence explaining the logical gap.

  1. A newspaper headline reads: "Study finds link between late-night screen use and lower exam scores." The story itself notes that no controls for other variables were included in the data.
  2. A public figure, responding to a question about a program’s costs, says: "Let’s talk about what we’ve actually accomplished. This program has helped thousands of families." The question about costs is not addressed.
  3. A campaign statement says: "The overwhelming majority of citizens in this region support our position. The people have spoken." No source or methodology for the majority claim is given.
  4. A commentary piece argues at length against a version of an opposing policy that would require a specific dramatic feature — but the actual proposal under discussion does not include that feature.
