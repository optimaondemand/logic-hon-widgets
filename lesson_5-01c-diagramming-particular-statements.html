Lesson 5.01c — Diagramming Particular Statements (I and O)

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.3 — Analyze how an author develops and refines a claim; ELA.12.C.1.2 — Write arguments that clearly introduce a claim and provide evidence

Learning Objective: Students will be able to diagram the particular affirmative (I) and particular negative (O) categorical statements using X-mark conventions, and will be able to explain the logical difference between existential and universal claims.

Orientation

In the previous lesson, you learned to diagram universal statements using shading — the mark of an empty region. This lesson covers the particular statement types, I and O, which do not say that a region is empty but rather that a region is occupied: at least one thing exists there. For these, the correct convention is an X-mark, not shading.

Intuitive Entry

Suppose a naturalist says, "Some migratory birds visit this wetland in spring." She is not saying anything about all migratory birds — she is asserting that at least one such bird has been observed here. She is not emptying a region; she is populating one. The Venn convention for this is an X: it marks a region as having at least one occupant. How many? Unknown — possibly one, possibly thousands. The X asserts only that the region is not empty.

The I Statement: "Some S are P"

The I statement — particular affirmative — asserts that at least one member of class S is also a member of class P. It says nothing about whether most, many, or all S's are P; it only establishes that the overlap between S and P is not empty. At least one thing exists in both classes simultaneously.

Diagramming rule for I ("Some S are P"): Place an X in the overlap region (S ∩ P).

"Some S are P"
An X in the overlap (S ∩ P) — at least one thing is both S and P.

The X sits in the overlap region — the zone that both circles share. Nothing else in the diagram is marked. The I statement makes no claim about the left crescent (S outside P) or the right crescent (P outside S); those regions could be occupied or empty, and the I statement is silent about them.

Example — Diagramming an I Statement

Statement: "Some birds are flightless animals."

S = birds, P = flightless animals.

Diagram: Place an X in the overlap of the S circle (birds) and the P circle (flightless animals). This represents the existence of at least one bird that is also flightless — penguins, ostriches, emus, for instance. The statement does not tell us how many flightless birds there are, only that at least one exists.

The O Statement: "Some S are not P"

The O statement — particular negative — asserts that at least one member of class S falls outside class P. At least one thing belongs to S without belonging to P. The region of S outside P — the left crescent — is not empty.

Diagramming rule for O ("Some S are not P"): Place an X in the left crescent — the region of S that lies outside P.

"Some S are not P"
An X in the left crescent — at least one S is outside P.
Example — Diagramming an O Statement

Statement: "Some fish are not edible by humans."

S = fish, P = edible by humans.

Diagram: Place an X in the region of the S circle (fish) that lies outside the P circle (edible by humans). This marks the existence of at least one fish that is not edible by humans — pufferfish in untreated form, for instance. The statement says nothing about fish that are edible; it only asserts that some fish escape the edible category.

All Four Types: A Complete Summary

Summary Table
Type Statement Mark Region
A All S are P Shade Left crescent (S outside P)
E No S are P Shade Overlap (S ∩ P)
I Some S are P X Overlap (S ∩ P)
O Some S are not P X Left crescent (S outside P)

Notice that A and O use the same region (left crescent) but opposite marks — A shades it empty, O marks it occupied. Similarly, E and I use the same region (overlap) with opposite marks. This is not a coincidence: A and O are contradictories, as are E and I. Contradictories make opposite claims about the same region — if one is true, the other must be false. The Venn diagram makes this relationship visible immediately.

Common Error — X-marks in the Wrong Region for O Statements

Students frequently place the X for an O statement in the overlap rather than the left crescent. This confuses "Some S are not P" (O) with "Some S are P" (I). The key is the word "not": the O statement says some S members fall outside P — which is the left crescent, not the overlap. If you find yourself unsure, read the statement carefully: where does the S member end up — inside P or outside P?

Practice Problems

Part A — Diagram Description. For each statement, identify the type (A, E, I, or O), state the correct mark (shade or X), and name the region to mark.

  1. "Some amphibians are aquatic animals."
  2. "No integers are irrational numbers."
  3. "Some cities are not capitals."
  4. "All asteroids are objects orbiting the sun."
  5. "Some Renaissance painters are sculptors."
  6. "No penguins are capable of sustained flight."

Part B — Reading Diagrams. Describe in words a Venn diagram with an X in the left crescent. What categorical statement type does this represent? Write the statement using S and P as placeholders. Then: what statement type would turn this diagram into a contradiction by shading the same region?

Part C — Square Connection. From Q1, you know that A and O are contradictories, as are E and I. Using the summary table above, explain in 3–4 sentences why the diagram conventions reflect this relationship — specifically, how the marks for contradictory pairs differ and what that tells us about truth value.
