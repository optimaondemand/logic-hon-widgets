Lesson 5.01b
Diagramming Universal Statements (A and E)
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze the structure an author uses to organize a text  |  ELA.11.C.1.4 — Write arguments that demonstrate logical structure and valid reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to diagram the universal affirmative (A) and universal negative (E) categorical statements using the correct shading conventions, and will be able to explain why shading — not X-marks — is the appropriate representation for universal claims.

Orientation

You now know what the regions of a Venn diagram represent and what shading and X-marks mean. This lesson applies those conventions to the two universal statement types: the A statement ("All S are P") and the E statement ("No S are P"). These statements make claims about all members of a class — which means they are making claims about what does not exist in certain regions, and shading is the appropriate tool for expressing that absence.

Intuitive Entry

Suppose a museum curator announces: "Every artifact in this collection is authenticated." If you then check the shelf of items labeled "unauthenticated artifacts from this collection" — you would find it empty. The curator's claim, if true, implies that no member of the collection occupies the unauthenticated region. The shelf is bare. Universal claims work this way: they tell you certain regions are empty, not that they are full. That is exactly what shading communicates: "This region is empty — no members here."

The A Statement: "All S are P"

The A statement — universal affirmative — asserts that every member of class S is also a member of class P. It says nothing about members of P that are not S; it is silent about the P-only region. What it does say is that nothing belongs to S without also belonging to P. In other words, the region of S that falls outside P — the left crescent — contains no members. That region is empty.

Diagramming rule for A ("All S are P"): Shade the region of S that lies outside P (left crescent).

"All S are P"
The left crescent (S outside P) is shaded — empty. Every S is inside P.

Notice that the overlap region — written S ∩ P, using the symbol "∩" to mean "overlaps with" (the set of things belonging to both S and P at once) — is not shaded. The A statement does not tell us whether that region is occupied or empty. It only tells us the left crescent is empty. There may be many S's inside P, or very few — the A statement guarantees that none fall outside P, but says nothing further.

Example — Diagramming an A Statement

Statement: "All mammals are warm-blooded animals."

S = mammals, P = warm-blooded animals.

Diagram: Shade the region of the S circle (mammals) that lies outside the P circle (warm-blooded animals). This represents the claim that no mammal exists outside the warm-blooded region.

The overlap region (things that are both mammals and warm-blooded) is left unshaded and unmarked — the statement does not make a specific existential claim about that zone; it only forecloses the left crescent.

The E Statement: "No S are P"

The E statement — universal negative — asserts that no member of class S belongs to class P. The two classes are entirely separate; there is no overlap. The region where S and P intersect contains no members — it is empty.

Diagramming rule for E ("No S are P"): Shade the overlap region (S ∩ P).

"No S are P"
The overlap region (S ∩ P) is shaded — empty. Nothing is both S and P.

The E statement is symmetric: "No S are P" and "No P are S" say the same thing about membership. This is why the E statement converts simply — the diagram looks the same regardless of which class you call S and which you call P.

Example — Diagramming an E Statement

Statement: "No reptiles are warm-blooded."

S = reptiles, P = warm-blooded animals.

Diagram: Shade the overlap of the S circle (reptiles) and the P circle (warm-blooded animals). This represents the claim that no reptile falls in the warm-blooded region — the overlap is entirely empty.

Comparing A and E

Side-by-Side Comparison
Statement Claim Region Shaded
A: "All S are P" Every S is in P Left crescent (S outside P)
E: "No S are P" No S is in P Overlap (S ∩ P)
Common Error — Shading the Wrong Region for A Statements

A frequent mistake is to shade the overlap for an A statement, as if "All S are P" meant "S and P are the same." They are not the same — A only says every S falls inside P; it says nothing about whether there are P's outside S. Shading the overlap would incorrectly represent a biconditional claim ("All S are P and all P are S") — much stronger than the A statement actually asserts.

Always ask: which region does the statement say is empty? For A ("All S are P"), the empty region is the part of S that would be outside P — the left crescent.

Practice Problems

Part A — Diagram Description. For each statement, describe in words which region you would shade (do not draw — describe precisely). Identify the statement type (A or E) and name the region.

  1. "All migratory birds are animals that travel seasonally."
  2. "No prime numbers are even numbers." (Note: this is false for the number 2, but treat it as a categorical statement and diagram as given.)
  3. "All cephalopods are invertebrates."
  4. "No sedimentary rocks are igneous rocks."
  5. "All objects in free fall experience weightlessness."

Part B — Reading Diagrams. A classmate shows you a diagram with only the left crescent of the S circle shaded. Write the A or E statement that the diagram represents, using S and P as placeholders. Explain in one sentence why this could not be an E statement.

Part C — Explanation. In 3–4 sentences, explain why universal statements use shading rather than X-marks. What would it mean if you placed an X in a region instead of shading it when diagramming "All S are P"?

Looking Ahead: The next lesson applies Venn diagram conventions to particular statements — I ("Some S are P") and O ("Some S are not P") — which make existential rather than universal claims and require X-marks rather than shading.
