Lesson 7.01c
Evaluating Arguments in Real Texts and Media
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.3.3 — Analyze and evaluate the effectiveness of an author's argument; ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the reasoning in an author's argument; ELA.12.R.3.4 — Evaluate an author's reasoning; ELA.12.C.1.3 — Write to argue, using logical reasoning and relevant evidence; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning
Learning objective: Students will evaluate the logical quality of arguments found in real texts and media, distinguishing failures of validity from failures of premise defensibility, and distinguishing both from non-logical defects such as unclear writing or irrelevant content.

Orientation

The last two lessons developed the skill of identifying and mapping arguments in extended prose. This lesson addresses the next question: once you have correctly identified the argument, how do you evaluate it? Evaluation requires more than extracting the argument — it requires applying the analytical tools of the entire course to the extracted structure. This is where term logic, the square of opposition, syllogistic rules, and the fallacy inventory come together as a unified toolkit.

Intuitive Entry Point

You have probably noticed that people often argue past each other — one person criticizes an argument's conclusion while the other defends the argument's logic, and neither notices they are talking about different things. A conclusion can be false even when the argument for it is valid; a conclusion can be true even when the argument for it is terrible. These are distinct questions, and answering both requires knowing exactly what questions to ask.

A Framework for Logical Evaluation

Once an argument has been extracted from its source text, evaluation proceeds through three sequential questions. They must be asked in order, because the answer to each affects whether the next question is relevant.

The Three Evaluation Questions

  1. Is the argument valid? Does the conclusion follow necessarily from the premises? Apply the five rules of valid categorical syllogisms (and for compound arguments, evaluate each component syllogism). If the argument is invalid, identify the specific rule violated. Note: if the argument is invalid, you have found a formal defect — but you may still want to consider whether the argument could be charitably reconstructed into a valid form before rendering a verdict.
  2. Are the premises defensible? Validity alone does not make an argument persuasive. Each premise must be evaluated: Is it a claim one should accept? What would it take to establish it? What is the strongest objection to it? This is the domain of soundness evaluation. Note that "defensible" is not "obviously true" — many defensible universal claims are open to legitimate debate; what matters is whether a premise is supported or supportable.
  3. Is anything else wrong? A formally valid argument with defensible premises may still fail in other ways: it may be addressing the wrong question, or it may contain informal fallacies (such as ambiguous terms, equivocation, or a suppressed false premise), or its conclusion may be weaker than its premises establish. These are real logical defects, but they are distinct from formal invalidity and unsound premises.

Evaluating Arguments in Media

Arguments in newspapers, documentaries, debates, and online writing are almost never in standard form. They are typically enthymemes — missing premises are common — and they often embed informal fallacies within otherwise valid structures. The evaluation process thus requires reconstruction before evaluation:

  1. Extract the argument (separating argumentative from non-argumentative sentences).
  2. Reconstruct any missing premises charitably.
  3. Apply the three evaluation questions to the reconstructed argument.
  4. If the argument is invalid, determine whether the invalidity results from the stated structure or from the reconstruction — and whether a more charitable reconstruction would produce validity.

Example — Evaluating an Argument from a Science Editorial

"Systematic observation of natural phenomena is the foundation of reliable scientific knowledge. All knowledge that rests on such observation can be trusted to accurately represent the natural world. The findings of well-conducted scientific studies rest on systematic observation of natural phenomena. Therefore, the findings of well-conducted scientific studies can be trusted to accurately represent the natural world."

Evaluation:

Validity check: The argument is AAA-1 (Barbara). Major premise: All systematic-observation-based knowledge is reliable. Minor premise: Scientific study findings are systematic-observation-based. Conclusion: Scientific study findings are reliable. Barbara is a valid first-figure mood. The argument is formally valid.

Premise defensibility: The major premise is the most contestable — "All knowledge that rests on systematic observation can be trusted to accurately represent the natural world" is a strong universal claim. A number of scientific studies have later been found to have systematic error despite resting on observation. The claim might need to be qualified to "well-designed systematic observation conducted under controlled conditions." The minor premise — that well-conducted scientific studies rest on systematic observation — is considerably more defensible.

Other considerations: The argument uses "can be trusted" rather than "is always correct," which is a legitimate qualification. That choice makes the universal major premise more defensible, though still open to challenge.

Common Error — Confusing Disagreement with Invalidity

Students sometimes mark an argument as invalid because they disagree with its conclusion. This is a mistake. The three evaluation questions are distinct: an argument can be formally valid while having premises you reject. Disagreeing with a conclusion is not a logical objection unless you can show either that the argument is formally invalid or that one of the premises is indefensible. Logical evaluation requires keeping these questions separate.

Connection to Argument

This lesson completes the analytical cycle of the course. You now have the tools to move from encountering an argument — in any form, in any medium — to a rigorous verdict: this argument is valid and its premises are defensible, or it fails here for this specific reason. That is what it means to reason well about reasoning. The remaining two lessons of the course apply these skills in the constructive direction: not just evaluating arguments you encounter, but building valid arguments of your own.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Recognition). Identify whether the following argument defect is a failure of validity, a failure of premise defensibility, or another type of defect. Explain your answer in one sentence.

Argument: "No machines can feel pain. Computers are machines. Therefore, no computers can feel pleasure."

Problem 2 (Analysis). Evaluate the following argument using all three questions. For each question, give a verdict (yes/no) and a one-to-two sentence justification.

"All forms of reasoning that rely on unverifiable premises are unreliable for establishing factual claims. Metaphysical reasoning relies on unverifiable premises. Therefore, metaphysical reasoning is unreliable for establishing factual claims."

Problem 3 (Construction). Find a short argument in a textbook passage, a science or history article, or a documentary summary. Extract the argument, reconstruct any missing premises, apply all three evaluation questions, and write a one-paragraph assessment (five to seven sentences) giving your verdict and explaining it.
