Lesson 3.03d — Particular Negative Statements (O Statements)

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite strong and thorough textual evidence to support analysis  |  ELA.11.V.1.1 — Integrate academic vocabulary to achieve precise meaning  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify and write O statements, translate natural-language sentences into standard O-statement form, and explain the counterintuitive distribution of the predicate term — why P is distributed in the O statement even though the statement is particular.

Where We Are

You have now studied three of the four categorical statement types: A (All S are P), E (No S are P), and I (Some S are P). The O statement completes the system. It is the particular negative: a claim that some members of a class are excluded from another class. The O statement has the most counterintuitive distribution pattern of the four — its predicate is distributed even though the statement itself is particular. Understanding why that is true is the central work of this lesson, and it will sharpen your understanding of distribution itself.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider this statement: "Some of the students in this class are not enrolled in mathematics." What does this tell you about the students it describes? It tells you that there exists at least one student who is, definitively, not in mathematics — they are outside the mathematics-enrollment class entirely. They are not partly in and partly out. They are excluded from the whole mathematics-enrollment class.

Now consider: can this statement be true while those specific students are still in mathematics? No. The whole point of the O statement is that the students it refers to are located outside — fully excluded from — the predicate class. That full exclusion is precisely what distributes the predicate term. The O statement speaks about all of mathematics-enrollment when it says that certain students have no part of it. This is not obvious, and it is worth reading twice.

The O Statement: "Some S are not P"

The O statement is the particular negative categorical statement. Its standard form is:

Some S are not P

where S is the subject term and P is the predicate term. The O statement asserts that at least one member of the S class does not belong to the P class.

The letter O comes from the Latin nego, "I deny." The second vowel of nego is O. The O statement is the weaker of the two negative types — it denies not that all of S is outside P (that is the E statement) but that some of S is outside P.

Why S Is Not Distributed

The subject S is not distributed in the O statement for the same reason it is not distributed in the I statement: the statement speaks only about some members of S. The remaining members of S — those who might actually be P — are left unaddressed. The O statement makes no claim about all members of S.

"Some birds are not eagles" does not say anything about all birds — only about some of them. There are birds that are eagles; the statement is simply silent about those. S is undistributed.

Why P Is Distributed — The Counterintuitive Result

This is the difficult part. The predicate P is distributed in the O statement, even though the statement is particular and speaks about only some members of S. The reasoning requires attention.

When the O statement says "Some S are not P," it means that those particular S members are excluded from the P class — all of it. The exclusion is total, not partial. For those S members, not one subdivision of P applies to them. They are outside the entire P class, not just outside some part of it.

The key test for distribution: Does the statement make a claim about all members of the class named by that term? For P in the O statement: yes. For those S members being described, the statement says they are excluded from every member of the P class. The statement covers all of P — at least in the negative direction — when it makes its exclusion claim. Therefore P is distributed.

Compare this to the A statement: "All dogs are mammals." The predicate "mammals" is not distributed — the statement says something about dogs, but leaves open the question of whether mammals that are not dogs exist and what they are like. The A statement puts S inside P without saying anything about the rest of P.

The O statement does the opposite. It puts some S outside P — entirely outside, touching no part of P. The claim, for those S members, covers the full extent of P.

Worked Example 1 — Distribution in the O Statement

Statement: "Some geometric figures are not triangles."

Subject (S): geometric figures    Predicate (P): triangles

S distribution: Not distributed — we are speaking only about some geometric figures (circles, squares, pentagons...), not about all of them.

P distribution: Distributed — for the geometric figures in question, the statement says they are excluded from the entire triangle class. No portion of "triangles" applies to them. The full extent of the predicate class is covered by the exclusion.

Worked Example 2 — Contrasting A and O Distribution

A statement: "All cats are mammals." — S (cats) distributed; P (mammals) NOT distributed. The statement places cats inside the mammal class but says nothing about the mammals that are not cats.

O statement: "Some cats are not mammals." (False, but formally illustrative.) — S (cats) NOT distributed; P (mammals) IS distributed. For the cats described, they are excluded from the entire mammal class.

The pattern: universal statements always distribute their subject. Negative statements always distribute their predicate. Particular affirmatives distribute neither; universal affirmatives distribute only the subject.

Common Error — Attempting to Convert the O Statement

A student writes: "Some arguments are not valid. Therefore, some invalid arguments exist." Then continues: "Some valid things are not arguments."

Analysis: The O statement does not convert simply. "Some S are not P" does not produce "Some P are not S." To see why: S is not distributed in the O statement, but in the converted form, S would appear as the predicate where it would need to be distributed for the inference to hold. Converting an O statement as though it were an I or E statement is a formal fallacy — illicit conversion. Only A (to its I form, partial conversion) and I and E statements convert in the ways studied.

Venn Diagram for the O Statement

An X sits in the part of S that falls outside P. At least one member of S is not a member of P. The X is placed in the S-only region, not the overlap — it marks something entirely outside all of P, which is exactly why P is distributed here even though the statement is particular.
"Some geometric figures are not triangles": the X marks a figure lying outside the triangle circle entirely.

Natural-Language Forms of the O Statement

  • "Some X are not Y" → already standard
  • "Not all X are Y" → "Some X are not Y"
  • "Not every X is Y" → "Some X are not Y"
  • "There are X that are not Y" → "Some X are not Y"
  • "A few X are not Y" → "Some X are not Y"
  • "Some X lack Y" → "Some X are not Y" (where Y is rephrased as a noun class)

The translation "Not all X are Y" → "Some X are not Y" is important and often missed. "Not all" is not the same as "none" — it is the negation of the universal, which gives you the particular negative.

The Complete Distribution Summary

With all four statement types now in view, here is the full distribution table. Study this carefully — it is used throughout Q2 when checking the validity of syllogisms.

The pattern, once you see it: universal statements distribute their subject; negative statements distribute their predicate. Particular affirmatives distribute neither; universal affirmatives distribute the subject only; particular negatives distribute the predicate only; universal negatives distribute both. This is not arbitrary — it follows directly from the logic of scope and exclusion.

Why This Matters for Argument

The O statement plays a critical role in the Square of Opposition, which you will study in Week 9. Specifically, the O statement is the contradictory of the A statement: if "All S are P" is true, "Some S are not P" must be false, and vice versa. This relationship — and the fact that a single O statement can refute a universal A claim — is one of the most powerful tools in formal argumentation.

The O statement is also the form in which most counterexamples are expressed. "All swans are white" (A) is refuted by "Some swans are not white" (O) — and finding that one Australian black swan was enough to make the O statement true. When you evaluate an argument that rests on an A premise, the question to ask is always: does an O counterexample exist?

Practice

Part A — Recognition. For each statement, identify whether it is an O statement. If not, identify which type it is.

  1. "Some arguments are not sound."
  2. "No fallacies are valid arguments."
  3. "Not every logical term is unambiguous."
  4. "All valid arguments preserve truth."
  5. "There are philosophers who are not logicians."

Part B — Distribution. For each O statement, identify S and P. Confirm that S is not distributed and P is distributed. For P, write one sentence explaining why the predicate is distributed despite the statement being particular.

  1. "Some historical arguments are not sound."
  2. "Some geometric figures are not regular polygons."
  3. "Some premises in the original argument are not true statements."

Part C — Translation. Translate each sentence into standard O-statement form. Identify S and P.

  1. "Not all arguments contain explicit premises."
  2. "There are students who are not enrolled in any elective course."
  3. "Some classical texts have never been translated into modern languages."
  4. "A few of the scientists' conclusions were not supported by the evidence."

Part D — Synthesis. For each pair, identify both statement types (A/E/I/O). Then write a brief explanation of how the distribution of terms differs between the two statements.

  1. (a) "All metals conduct electricity."    (b) "Some metals are not conductors of electricity."
  2. (a) "No mammals are reptiles."    (b) "Some mammals are not warm-blooded creatures." (Note: this is false, but evaluate it formally.)
