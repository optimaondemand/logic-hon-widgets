What Is Mood?

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the mood of any standard-form categorical syllogism, express it as a three-letter code in the correct order (major premise → minor premise → conclusion), and distinguish mood from figure as two independent properties of a syllogism.

Orientation

You now have a thorough understanding of the internal structure of a categorical syllogism: three terms (major, minor, middle), two premises identified by which term they contain, and a conclusion whose subject and predicate determine the term roles throughout. In Week 3 you learned that the position of the middle term across the two premises determines the syllogism's figure. This week introduces a second structural property — mood — which characterizes the syllogism from a completely different angle. Figure describes where terms are positioned; mood describes what kinds of categorical statements appear in the syllogism. Together, figure and mood give a complete formal characterization of any categorical syllogism's structure.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider two musical pieces: both are in sonata form, with the same number of movements and the same key relationships between sections. They share a formal architecture. But one is in a major key throughout and the other alternates between major and minor — and that tonal character profoundly affects how the music works. Two syllogisms can share the same figure (the same arrangement of terms) but differ in the types of statements they use. A syllogism built entirely from universal affirmatives ("All ... are ...") operates differently from one that mixes universal negatives and particular affirmatives — even if the terms are arranged identically. The mood is the tonal signature of the syllogism's statement types.

What Is Mood?

The mood of a categorical syllogism is the ordered sequence of statement types in its three component statements, read in the standard order: major premise first, minor premise second, conclusion third. Since each statement is one of four types (A, E, I, or O), the mood is expressed as a three-letter code using those letters.

The word mood (Latin: modus, meaning manner, mode, or measure) was adopted by medieval logicians to describe this property. It is a measure of the syllogism's character in terms of the quality and quantity of its statements. Aristotle himself identified which combinations of statement types produce valid inferences in each figure — in the Prior Analytics, he worked through the possibilities systematically, and the medieval tradition organized his findings into the named mood-figure pairs we still use today.

Reading a Syllogism's Mood

To determine a syllogism's mood:

  1. Confirm the syllogism is in standard form (major premise first, minor premise second, conclusion last).
  2. Identify the statement type of the major premise: A, E, I, or O.
  3. Identify the statement type of the minor premise.
  4. Identify the statement type of the conclusion.
  5. Write the three letters in order as a single code.

If a syllogism is not in standard form, convert it to standard form first (identify the conclusion, identify which premise contains the major term, place that premise first). The mood code only means something in relation to standard form — an out-of-order syllogism will yield a different mood code even if its logical structure is unchanged.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — AAA Mood
Major premise: All mammals are warm-blooded animals.
Minor premise: All whales are mammals.
Conclusion: Therefore, all whales are warm-blooded animals.

Mood: Major premise = A; Minor premise = A; Conclusion = A. Mood code: AAA.

Example 2 — EIO Mood
Major premise: No reptiles are warm-blooded animals.
Minor premise: Some lizards are reptiles.
Conclusion: Therefore, some lizards are not warm-blooded animals.

Mood: Major premise = E; Minor premise = I; Conclusion = O. Mood code: EIO.

Example 3 — AII Mood
Major premise: All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd numbers.
Minor premise: Some numbers in set Q are prime numbers greater than 2.
Conclusion: Therefore, some numbers in set Q are odd numbers.

Mood: A, I, I. Mood code: AII.

Common Error — Reading Mood Before Confirming Standard Form

If the premises are not arranged in standard form — major premise first — reading mood directly from the text will yield a wrong code. Always identify which premise is the major (the one containing the predicate of the conclusion) and which is the minor before assigning letter positions. A syllogism written with the minor premise first will look like it has a different mood than it actually has, once correctly arranged.

Mood vs. Figure: Two Independent Properties

Mood and figure describe two completely different aspects of a syllogism's structure. Figure describes the position of the middle term across the two premises (which role — subject or predicate — M plays in each). Mood describes the type of each statement (its quantity and quality). Two syllogisms can have the same figure and different moods; they can have the same mood and different figures. What makes a syllogism valid or invalid is the specific combination of mood and figure — neither property alone determines validity. This is the key insight that the next two lessons develop.

Practice Problems

Part A — Identify the Mood. For each syllogism in standard form, state the mood as a three-letter code.

  1. All birds are vertebrates. All eagles are birds. Therefore, all eagles are vertebrates.
  2. No inert gases are reactive elements. All noble gases are inert gases. Therefore, no noble gases are reactive elements.
  3. All deciduous trees are trees that lose their leaves. Some trees in the arboretum are deciduous trees. Therefore, some trees in the arboretum are trees that lose their leaves.
  4. No fish are warm-blooded animals. Some aquatic organisms are fish. Therefore, some aquatic organisms are not warm-blooded animals.
  5. Some historical figures are philosophers. All Stoics are historical figures. Therefore, some Stoics are philosophers.

Part B — Mood and Standard Form. Each argument below is not in standard form. Rewrite it in standard form, then state the mood.

  1. Some ocean currents are warm currents. All warm currents are currents that affect climate. Therefore, some ocean currents are currents that affect climate. (Check: is this already in standard form? Identify major and minor premises.)
  2. All acts of deliberate deception are acts that violate trust. No scientific reports from this institution are acts of deliberate deception. Therefore, no scientific reports from this institution are acts that violate trust.
  3. Some mathematical objects are infinite sets. All infinite sets are mathematical objects that cannot be fully enumerated. Therefore, some mathematical objects are objects that cannot be fully enumerated.

Part C — Reflection. Can two syllogisms have the same mood but different figures? Write one example pair showing a syllogism with mood AAA in Figure 1 and another syllogism with mood AAA in Figure 2. (You will need to recall the figure patterns from Week 3: Figure 1 has M as subject of the major premise and predicate of the minor; Figure 2 has M as predicate of both premises.) Indicate whether each is valid — and if they differ, what does that tell you about the relationship between mood and validity?

Looking Ahead: Now that you can read a syllogism's mood, the next lesson combines mood with figure to give a complete formal characterization of any categorical syllogism — the foundation for identifying valid and invalid forms.
