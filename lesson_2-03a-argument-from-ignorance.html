Lesson 2.03a
Argument from Ignorance
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments; evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the argument from ignorance (argumentum ad ignorantiam), explain why absence of evidence does not establish truth or falsity, and distinguish this fallacy from legitimate procedural rules such as the legal presumption of innocence.

Orientation: Where We Are

Week 3 introduced informal fallacies and examined why relevance is the central requirement for a premise to do real argumentative work. You looked at several fallacies of relevance — including the ad hominem and appeals to force, popularity, and pity — all of which share a common defect: their premises fail to connect to the conclusion in any logical way. Week 4 continues with this category. The argument from ignorance is one of the most commonly encountered fallacies of relevance, and it has a distinctive structure worth studying carefully: instead of offering a positive reason, it attempts to shift the burden of proof by pointing to what has not been demonstrated.

Intuitive Entry: "You Can't Prove It's Wrong"

You have probably encountered arguments like these. Someone states an unusual claim, and when pressed for evidence, responds: "Well, you can't prove it isn't true." Or the reverse: someone assumes a claim is false simply because no one has yet established that it is true. Both moves show up regularly in everyday conversation.

Consider two exchanges:

"There is no proof that this ancient site was not built by a civilization we haven't yet discovered. So it probably was."
"No study has confirmed that this supplement improves memory. Therefore it doesn't work."

Both arguments feel as though they are saying something meaningful. In the first, the absence of a disproof seems to open a door. In the second, the absence of proof seems to close one. But notice what neither argument actually provides: a positive reason to believe the conclusion. Both are attempting to use a gap in our knowledge as if it were evidence. That is the move this lesson is about.

The Formal Concept: Argumentum ad Ignorantiam

The fallacy is called the argument from ignorance, from the Latin argumentum ad ignorantiam — literally, "argument directed at ignorance" or "argument from not-knowing." The term ignorantia in this context does not mean foolishness; it means lack of knowledge or information. The fallacy was named and discussed in the classical tradition and received systematic treatment by John Locke (1632–1704) in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), where he listed it as one of the common argumentative moves people use to avoid engaging with evidence. The underlying logical error, however, is older: it trades on a confusion between two very different things.

The fallacy takes one of two general forms:

Form A

No one has proven that X is false.
Therefore, X is true.

Form B

No one has proven that X is true.
Therefore, X is false.

In both forms, the premise describes an epistemic situation — a state of what is known or unknown — and the conclusion draws a claim about reality. The error is that a gap in our knowledge does not tell us anything about what is actually the case. The world does not change based on whether we have investigated it. Something either is or is not true, independently of whether anyone has demonstrated it yet.

The key principle this fallacy violates is sometimes stated this way: absence of evidence is not the same as evidence of absence. Not finding proof that X is true does not prove X is false. Not finding proof that X is false does not prove X is true. The absence of a demonstration is a fact about our knowledge, not about the thing being discussed.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Clear Case (Form A)

Argument: "No scientist has ever proven that there is no life on other planets. Therefore, extraterrestrial life exists."

Why it is a fallacy: The premise is true. Scientists have not proven that no life exists elsewhere in the universe. But this is a statement about the limits of current investigation, not a statement about the universe itself. The universe is very large, most of it has not been examined, and absence of a disproof is not positive evidence for the existence of extraterrestrial life. The conclusion does not follow from the premise.

Example 2 — Clear Case (Form B)

Argument: "No peer-reviewed study has confirmed that this ingredient causes harm. Therefore it is safe."

Why it is a fallacy: The absence of confirming studies may reflect that the research has not been done, that results have not yet been published, or that the harm is difficult to measure over short time frames. It does not establish that no harm exists. Acting as though an untested substance is safe because it has not been proven harmful confuses the limits of investigation with a positive result.

Common Confusion — Legal Presumption of Innocence

The objection: "Isn't 'innocent until proven guilty' the same thing? The law says that if guilt hasn't been proven, the person is innocent. That seems like an argument from ignorance."

Why it is not the same thing: The legal presumption of innocence is a procedural rule, not a logical argument about what is true. It tells courts how to act in the absence of sufficient proof: err on the side of not punishing the accused. It does not assert that the accused is actually innocent. A person may be factually guilty but acquitted because the evidence presented was insufficient. The legal standard governs decisions; it does not make claims about reality. Arguing from ignorance, by contrast, moves from "not proven" to "therefore true" (or "therefore false") as a logical conclusion about how things are. The distinction is between a rule for action under uncertainty and an assertion about what is actually the case.

Connection to Argument Structure

The argument from ignorance is a fallacy of relevance because the stated premise — that something has not been proven — is irrelevant to whether the conclusion is true. Recall what relevance requires: a premise must offer genuine support for the conclusion; it must give you a reason to think the conclusion is more likely to be true. A gap in knowledge does not do this. It tells you only about the state of investigation, not about the subject of the argument.

You can use this understanding to evaluate arguments when you encounter them. When someone says "no one has shown this is false," the appropriate response is to ask: what positive evidence does support the claim? The fact that something has not been refuted is not by itself a reason to accept it. Similarly, when someone argues that a claim is false because it has not been confirmed, the appropriate response is to ask whether the matter has actually been investigated and what a genuine test would look like. The burden of proof lies with the person making a positive claim, not with those who have not yet investigated it.

Practice Problems

Recognition. For each item below, determine whether the argument commits the argument from ignorance. Explain your answer in one or two sentences.

  1. "No one has ever proven that deep-sea creatures larger than known species do not exist. So there must be unknown giant creatures in the ocean."
  2. "Researchers have tested this drug in three clinical trials and found no evidence that it reduces symptoms. It does not reduce symptoms."
  3. "The fact that no historians have documented this event does not mean it didn't happen."

Analysis. For each argument below, (i) identify which form of the fallacy is present (Form A or Form B), (ii) state the implicit assumption being made about absence of evidence, and (iii) explain what a legitimate argument for the same conclusion would need to provide instead.

  1. "No one has shown that this ancient manuscript is a forgery. It must therefore be authentic."
  2. "There is no proof that this process increases efficiency. We should assume it makes no difference."

Construction. Write a short paragraph (4–6 sentences) in which you make an argument that might initially seem like an argument from ignorance but is not. Then explain, in a second paragraph, what distinguishes your argument from the fallacy. Your facilitator can help you check whether you have drawn the line correctly.

  1. Write your argument and your explanation as described above.
