Lesson 5.03c — The Four Figures of the Syllogism

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.3.3 — Explain how an author's perspective shapes style and meaning; ELA.12.C.1.3 — Write arguments that use logical structure and valid reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify the figure of a categorical syllogism in standard form and explain how the position of the middle term determines each figure.

Orientation

You can now identify the three terms of a syllogism and convert any argument to standard form. The next question is: how does the middle term sit within the two premises? Its position — whether it appears as subject or predicate in each premise — defines the figure of the syllogism. Figure is one of two structural properties (the other is mood, coming in a later lesson) that together determine whether a syllogism is valid.

Intuitive Entry

Think of a relay race. Each runner hands a baton to the next. The middle runner is the link: she receives the baton from the first runner and passes it to the third. How she positions herself in relation to the first and third runners determines the shape of the handoff. In a syllogism, the middle term (M) plays the same role — it receives from one premise and passes to another. But the position M occupies (subject or predicate in each premise) can vary, producing four distinct structural shapes. Those shapes are the four figures.

What a Figure Is

The figure of a syllogism is determined entirely by the position of the middle term (M) in the major and minor premises. In each premise, a term occupies either the subject position or the predicate position. Since M appears in both premises, there are four possible combinations:

Figure 1

Major: M — P
Minor: S — M
Conc.: S — P

M is subject of major premise, predicate of minor premise.

Figure 2

Major: P — M
Minor: S — M
Conc.: S — P

M is predicate of both premises.

Figure 3

Major: M — P
Minor: M — S
Conc.: S — P

M is subject of both premises.

Figure 4

Major: P — M
Minor: M — S
Conc.: S — P

M is predicate of major, subject of minor.

The conclusion is always written S — P (subject of conclusion — predicate of conclusion) regardless of figure. Only the arrangement of M in the premises varies.

A Brief History of the Four Figures

Aristotle, in the Prior Analytics, identified three figures of the syllogism. He recognized that Figure 4 was logically possible but considered it an indirect or awkward arrangement — effectively the inverse of Figure 1. Most Scholastic logicians working in the Aristotelian tradition treated Figure 4 as a legitimate fourth figure, though there was debate. It is sometimes attributed to Galen of Pergamon (2nd century CE), though the attribution is disputed. By the high medieval period, all four figures were standard in the Scholastic curriculum, and logic textbooks from the 13th century onward treated them as a complete system. The mnemonic devices for valid moods — Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and so on — were organized by figure in these texts.

Identifying the Figure: Procedure

To determine the figure of a syllogism in standard form:

  1. Confirm the syllogism is in standard form (major premise first).
  2. Identify the middle term (M) — the term that appears in both premises.
  3. In the major premise: is M the subject or the predicate?
  4. In the minor premise: is M the subject or the predicate?
  5. Match the pattern to the four figures above.
Example 1 — Figure 1
Major premise: All mammals are warm-blooded.
Minor premise: All dogs are mammals.
Conclusion: Therefore, all dogs are warm-blooded.

Analysis: Middle term = mammals. In major premise, mammals is the subject. In minor premise, mammals is the predicate. Pattern: M–P / S–M → S–P. This is Figure 1.

Example 2 — Figure 2
Major premise: No fish are warm-blooded.
Minor premise: All dolphins are warm-blooded.
Conclusion: Therefore, no dolphins are fish.

Analysis: Wait — the conclusion here has "dolphins" as subject and "fish" as predicate, so P = fish and S = dolphins. The middle term = warm-blooded. In major premise: "No fish are warm-blooded" — wait, let me re-identify. Major premise contains P (fish): "No fish are warm-blooded." M (warm-blooded) is the predicate of the major premise. Minor premise contains S (dolphins): "All dolphins are warm-blooded." M is the predicate of the minor premise. Pattern: P–M / S–M → S–P. This is Figure 2.

Example 3 — Figure 3
Major premise: All crows are black.
Minor premise: All crows are birds.
Conclusion: Therefore, some birds are black.

Analysis: Conclusion: subject = birds (S), predicate = black (P). Middle term = crows. In major premise: crows is the subject. In minor premise: crows is the subject. Pattern: M–P / M–S → S–P. This is Figure 3.

Example 4 — Figure 4
Major premise: All vertebrates are animals with backbones.
Minor premise: All animals with backbones have a nervous system.
Conclusion: Therefore, some things with a nervous system are vertebrates.

Analysis: Conclusion: S = things with a nervous system, P = vertebrates. Middle term = animals with backbones. In major premise: M is the predicate. In minor premise: M is the subject. Pattern: P–M / M–S → S–P. This is Figure 4.

Common Error — Confusing Figure with Validity

Figure is not the same as validity. Each figure contains both valid and invalid syllogistic forms. Knowing the figure does not tell you whether the argument is valid — that depends on the figure combined with the mood (the pattern of A, E, I, O statement types in the premises and conclusion). Figure is one axis; mood is the other. Together they uniquely identify every categorical syllogism and determine its validity.

Practice Problems

Part A — Figure Identification. Place each syllogism in standard form (if not already) and identify its figure (1, 2, 3, or 4).

  1. All philosophers are careful thinkers. All Stoics are philosophers. Therefore, all Stoics are careful thinkers.
  2. No triangles are quadrilaterals. All squares are quadrilaterals. Therefore, no squares are triangles.
  3. All whales are mammals. All whales are aquatic animals. Therefore, some aquatic animals are mammals.
  4. All carnivores are meat-eaters. Some meat-eaters are domesticated animals. Therefore, some domesticated animals are carnivores.
  5. No reptiles are endothermic. All snakes are reptiles. Therefore, no snakes are endothermic.

Part B — Explanation. In 3–4 sentences, explain why knowing the figure of a syllogism is analytically useful. Why isn't it sufficient to just know whether the argument is valid or invalid?

Part C — Construction. Write one original syllogism in each of the four figures. Each syllogism should be in standard form, and you should label the figure for each. Use neutral subject matter (science, mathematics, history, or everyday objects).

Looking Ahead: Figure tells us the structural shape of a syllogism; mood tells us what types of statements (A, E, I, O) fill that shape. The next unit on inferences will deepen your understanding of what each statement type can and cannot support, preparing you to master mood and the valid syllogistic forms.
