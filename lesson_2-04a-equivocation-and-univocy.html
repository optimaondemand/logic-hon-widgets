Lesson 2.04a
Equivocation and Univocity
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments; evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning  |  ELA.11.V.1.1 — Integrate academic vocabulary to accurately convey ideas  |  ELA.11.V.1.2 — Apply knowledge of etymology and word relationships to determine meaning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to define univocity and explain why it is required for valid argument; identify and analyze the fallacy of equivocation by locating the term that shifts meaning across premises; and distinguish genuine equivocation from cases where a repeated term is used univocally.

Orientation: A New Category

This lesson opens a new chapter in the study of informal fallacies. The last three lessons (2.03a, 2.03b, 2.03c) completed the category of fallacies of relevance — errors where a premise fails to be logically relevant to the conclusion. Beginning here, the course turns to a different family: fallacies of ambiguity. These are errors that arise not from irrelevance but from imprecision in language. The premises may appear relevant; the structure may look valid. The problem is that a word or phrase is quietly doing double duty, carrying two different meanings at once. When that happens, an argument that seems to work actually falls apart the moment the ambiguity is made explicit.

Intuitive Entry: Words That Live Two Lives

Consider the word "bank." In one sentence it names the edge of a river; in another, a financial institution. Or consider "light": not heavy in one context, not dark in another. You already know this about language. Words regularly carry more than one meaning, and you switch between meanings automatically, guided by context.

Now ask a sharper question: what happens when an argument depends on a word that carries two meanings, and the argument works only if the reader does not notice the switch? The premises look as though they are talking about the same thing; the conclusion appears to follow. But the word that connects them means one thing in the first premise and something different in the second. The argument is built on an illusion of sameness. This is the core of today's lesson.

Univocity: The Requirement for Sound Argument

Before examining the error, it helps to name the standard that good arguments must meet. A term is used univocally (from Latin unus, "one," and vox, "voice" — "one-voiced") when it carries exactly the same meaning every time it appears in an argument. If "justice" appears in both a premise and the conclusion, it must mean the same thing in both places. If "strength" is used in two premises, the same sense of "strength" must apply throughout. Univocity is not a stylistic preference; it is a logical requirement. Arguments connect claims through shared terms, and if a shared term does not actually share a meaning, the connection breaks.

The requirement of univocity follows directly from what an argument is. An argument uses a term as a logical bridge: "all A are B; C is an A; therefore C is a B." That bridge holds only if "A" means the same thing each time it crosses. If the "A" in the first premise is a different concept from the "A" in the second premise, the argument does not actually connect the two claims. It only appears to.

Equivocation: The Fallacy of the Shifting Term

The fallacy of equivocation (from Latin aequus, "equal," and vox, "voice" — literally "equal-voiced," suggesting something that sounds the same while meaning two different things) occurs when an argument exploits a word or phrase that carries two meanings, using it in one sense in one part of the argument and in a different sense elsewhere. Because the word sounds identical throughout, the shift is easy to miss. The argument appears to have a valid structure. It does not.

Equivocation is one of the most subtle informal fallacies precisely because the error is hidden inside the language rather than in the logical form. A relevance fallacy is detectable by asking whether the premise has anything to do with the conclusion. Equivocation requires a different diagnostic: you must ask whether every key term means the same thing each time it appears.

Aristotle identified equivocation in his Sophistical Refutations as one of the primary weapons of the sophist — the skilled verbal manipulator who aims to appear to reason well rather than actually to reason well. In Aristotle's analysis, equivocation is dangerous precisely because it is verbal rather than structural. A trained logician, attentive to form, could miss it if not also attending carefully to meaning. The remedy Aristotle prescribed was precise definition: fix the meaning of every key term before the argument begins, and equivocation has nowhere to hide.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — The Parking Sign

P1: The sign said "fine for parking here."
P2: If something is fine, it is acceptable.
C: Therefore, parking here is acceptable.

The equivocation is on "fine." In P1, "fine" means a monetary penalty. In P2, "fine" means satisfactory or permissible. The word sounds identical in both premises, but the argument depends on smuggling one meaning in through the first premise and a different meaning in through the second. Make the shift explicit and the argument collapses: "The sign said parking here carries a penalty; a penalty is acceptable; therefore parking here is acceptable" — which is obviously not a valid inference.

Example 2 — The Classic "Nothing" Argument

P1: Nothing is better than complete happiness.
P2: A good meal is better than nothing.
C: Therefore, a good meal is better than complete happiness.

This is one of the most studied examples of equivocation in the logical tradition. In P1, "nothing" functions as a universal quantifier: no thing that exists surpasses complete happiness. In P2, "nothing" functions as a concrete noun: having no food at all. These are two entirely different uses of the word. If you substitute the actual meanings, the argument reads: "No existing thing surpasses complete happiness; a good meal surpasses having no food at all; therefore a good meal surpasses complete happiness." The premises no longer connect. The conclusion does not follow. The argument works only in the English sentence where "nothing" can silently switch roles between the two premises.

Example 3 — Law and Human Behavior

P1: What is done in accordance with natural law is just.
P2: Predators killing prey is done in accordance with natural law.
C: Therefore, predators killing prey is just.

The equivocation here is on "natural law." In P1, "natural law" refers to the tradition of moral or juridical principles derived from reason and human nature (the sense used in ethics and jurisprudence). In P2, "natural law" refers to descriptive regularities in the physical world — the laws of ecology and biology. An argument that equivocates on "natural law" in this way does not establish anything about justice; it merely exploits the shared name of two different concepts.

Non-Example — Univocal Use of a Term Across Two Premises

P1: All rational animals are capable of moral reasoning.
P2: Human beings are rational animals.
C: Therefore, human beings are capable of moral reasoning.

The term "rational animals" appears in both P1 and P2. A student encountering this argument for the first time might wonder whether "rational" shifts meaning. It does not. In both premises, "rational animal" carries the classical sense established in Aristotle's definition of the human being: an animal whose characteristic activity involves reason. The term is used univocally throughout. This is a valid syllogism, not a case of equivocation. The fact that a term repeats across premises is not by itself suspicious; what matters is whether the meaning holds constant.

Common Error — Mistaking Vagueness for Equivocation

Equivocation requires that a term carry two distinct, identifiable meanings and that the argument switch between them. A term that is merely vague or imprecise — one where the meaning is fuzzy but not doubled — is a different problem. If someone argues "Excellence takes effort; this student shows effort; therefore this student shows excellence," the imprecision is in what counts as "excellence" and what counts as sufficient "effort," but neither term is doing the work of two distinct meanings in two distinct premises. Calling this equivocation would be a misdiagnosis. Reserve the label for cases where you can point to two specific, different meanings that the term carries in different parts of the argument.

Connection to Argument Structure

Equivocation matters beyond identifying a fallacy. It shows why careful definition is not merely an academic formality but a structural necessity. When terms are defined precisely at the outset of an argument, equivocation is prevented by construction: there is no room for a term to shift because its meaning has been fixed. This is why philosophical arguments — at their best — open with a definition of key terms. It is also why cross-examination in legal and academic contexts so often focuses on pinning down what a speaker means by a critical word.

Notice also the relationship to validity. An equivocating argument is not merely weak or improbable; it is structurally invalid once the ambiguity is made explicit. If "X" in P1 means A and "X" in P2 means B, then the argument actually contains two different terms (A and B), not one. The surface form of the argument vanishes when the terms are replaced with their actual meanings. Equivocation is thus a concealed form of invalidity — one hidden in language rather than in logical form.

Practice Problems

Recognition

  1. Each argument below either commits equivocation or is free of it. For each, identify (i) the term you suspect of shifting and (ii) whether the shift actually occurs. If it does, name the two different meanings.
    • (a) P1: Only man is rational. P2: No woman is a man. C: Therefore, no woman is rational.
    • (b) P1: This claim is groundless. P2: What is groundless cannot be supported. C: Therefore, this claim cannot be supported.
    • (c) P1: All actions that promote well-being are good. P2: Exercise promotes well-being. C: Therefore, exercise is good.
    • (d) P1: Sharp knives are dangerous. P2: A sharp student is one with keen perception. C: Therefore, a student with keen perception is dangerous.

Analysis

  1. Take the "nothing/happiness" argument from Example 2 and rewrite each premise by substituting the actual meaning of "nothing" in that premise. Write out the result. Explain in one or two sentences why the rewritten version makes the invalidity visible in a way the original does not.
  2. A classmate argues: "Equivocation is the same as lying, because the person using it knows the word means two things." Evaluate this claim. Is equivocation always intentional? Does it matter whether it is intentional for the purpose of identifying whether a fallacy has occurred? Explain your reasoning.

Construction

  1. Construct an original equivocation fallacy. Choose a word that carries two genuinely distinct meanings (do not use any example from this lesson). Build a two-premise argument that exploits the shift. Label the word, identify its two meanings, and explain why the argument fails once the shift is made explicit.
  2. Construct a two-premise argument that uses a potentially ambiguous word univocally throughout. Your argument should be valid and the term should appear in both premises. After writing the argument, write one sentence confirming that the term carries the same meaning in both places. This is a demonstration of what equivocation-free argument looks like.
  3. Aristotle wrote that the remedy for equivocation is precise definition. Choose one of the equivocating arguments from problem 1 and rewrite it so that it is equivocation-free — either by defining the key term at the outset or by replacing the ambiguous term with two clearly different terms that make the argument's actual content plain. Briefly note whether the repaired argument is valid.
Looking Ahead: Equivocation involves a single term that carries two meanings. The next two lessons in this category examine related but distinct fallacies: amphiboly (ambiguity arising from grammatical structure rather than word meaning) and the fallacies of composition and division (errors that arise from shifting between claims about parts and claims about wholes).
