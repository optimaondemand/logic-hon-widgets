Lesson 5.04a
Immediate Inferences
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze the structure an author uses to organize a text; ELA.11.R.2.3 — Analyze an author's choices in establishing and advancing a point of view; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to distinguish immediate inferences from syllogistic inferences, identify which relationships on the Square of Opposition license immediate inferences, and derive correct immediate inferences from given categorical statements.

Orientation

Since Week 3 of this quarter, you have been working with the full structure of the categorical syllogism: three terms, two premises, a conclusion. A syllogism is a mediated inference — it reaches its conclusion through the middle term, which connects the two premises. This week introduces a different and simpler kind of inference: the immediate inference, which derives a conclusion from a single categorical statement without any middle term at all. You already built the conceptual foundation for this in Quarter 1, Week 9, when you learned the Square of Opposition. That structure now becomes a working tool for systematic inference.

Intuitive Entry Point

Suppose someone tells you, "Every student in this room passed the exam." You did not need to run any further investigation — you can immediately conclude, just from that one statement, that it is false that no student in this room passed. You can also conclude that at least some students passed. And you know that the statement "Some students did not pass" must be false. All of these conclusions follow from a single statement, with no additional premises and no middle term connecting anything. You are already performing immediate inferences.

Everyday reasoning is full of moves like this. When someone says "Not all birds can fly," you instantly understand they mean some birds cannot fly, and you do not require further argument. The logical tradition made these moves explicit and systematic — which is the work of this week.

What Is an Immediate Inference?

An immediate inference is an inference drawn from a single categorical statement to another categorical statement, without any additional premise and without a middle term. The word immediate comes from the Latin immediatus — without a medium, without an intermediary. In syllogistic reasoning, the middle term is the medium; an immediate inference skips that mediation entirely and moves directly from one statement to another.

Immediate inferences are valid only when the logical relationship between the original statement and the derived statement is one that guarantees the truth of the derived statement given the truth of the original. The Square of Opposition maps exactly those relationships for the four standard categorical statement types (A, E, I, O).

The Square of Opposition as a Map of Immediate Inferences

You studied the Square of Opposition in Q1 Week 9 as a diagram of logical relationships among statements sharing the same subject and predicate terms. Each relationship on the Square licenses a specific kind of immediate inference. Review the four relationships:

Relationship Statements Involved What It Licenses
Contradictory A ↔ O; E ↔ I Opposite truth values: if one is true, the other is false, and vice versa
Contrary A ↔ E Cannot both be true; can both be false. If A is true, E is false (and vice versa). If one is false, the other is undetermined.
Subcontrary I ↔ O Cannot both be false; can both be true. If I is false, O is true (and vice versa). If one is true, the other is undetermined.
Subalternation A → I; E → O Truth flows downward: if the universal is true, the corresponding particular is true. Falsity flows upward: if the particular is false, the universal is false.

These relationships are not mere conventions — they follow from the meaning of universal and particular quantifiers. If it is true that all members of a class have some property, it must be true that some do (subalternation). If it is false that any member has the property, it cannot be true that some do (contradiction). Aristotle worked out these relationships in the De Interpretatione, part of the Organon; the medieval Scholastics gave the relationships their standard names and organized them into the visual diagram we now call the Square.

Deriving Immediate Inferences: Procedure

To derive an immediate inference from the Square of Opposition:

  1. Identify the statement type (A, E, I, or O) of the original statement.
  2. Identify which relationship connects it to the target statement type.
  3. Apply the rule for that relationship to determine what the target statement's truth value must be.
  4. State the derived inference clearly, keeping the same subject and predicate terms.

Note: the immediate inferences licensed by the Square preserve the same subject and predicate terms but change the quantifier, the quality, or both. This is what distinguishes them from conversion, obversion, and contraposition — the other immediate inference operations you will study in the next three lessons — which may rearrange or replace terms.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Contradictory Inference

Original (A statement): "All planets in our solar system are bodies that orbit the sun."

Inference: What can we immediately conclude about the corresponding O statement — "Some planets in our solar system are not bodies that orbit the sun"?

Analysis: A and O are contradictories. If the A statement is true, the O statement must be false. The inference is immediate and certain: from "All planets orbit the sun" we can immediately conclude "It is false that some planets do not orbit the sun."

Example 2 — Subalternation

Original (E statement): "No mammals are cold-blooded animals." (Assume this is true.)

Inference: What follows immediately about the corresponding O statement — "Some mammals are not cold-blooded animals"?

Analysis: E and O are related by subalternation: if the E statement is true, the O statement must also be true. From "No mammals are cold-blooded," we immediately infer "Some mammals are not cold-blooded." (The universal claim guarantees the particular claim.) This inference is valid and immediate — no middle term is needed.

Common Error — Reversing Subalternation

Students sometimes infer upward: "Some mammals are not cold-blooded, therefore no mammals are cold-blooded." This reverses the direction of subalternation. Truth flows downward (from universal to particular), not upward. If the particular O statement is true, the universal E statement is undetermined — it might be true or false. The upward move is not a valid immediate inference.

Example 3 — Contrary Inference

Original (A statement, known to be false): "All rivers flow into oceans." (False, as some rivers are endorheic — they flow into inland basins.)

Target: What can we conclude about the E statement "No rivers flow into oceans"?

Analysis: A and E are contraries. Knowing that the A statement is false does not determine the E statement — contraries can both be false. We cannot immediately infer the truth value of E from the falsity of A alone. This distinguishes contrary from contradictory relationships.

Connection to Argument

Immediate inferences are tools for rapid logical reorganization. In evaluating extended arguments, you will often find that a conclusion stated as an O statement needs to be connected to a premise stated as an A statement — and the Square tells you instantly whether that connection is valid. More importantly, the relationships on the Square allow you to detect hidden contradictions: if an arguer asserts both an A statement and its corresponding O statement as true, they have contradicted themselves, and the Square makes that visible immediately. Aristotle valued this diagnostic power — the Square is not just a catalog of relationships but a tool for testing an argument's internal consistency without working through a full syllogism.

Practice Problems

Part A — Recognition. For each original statement, identify its type (A, E, I, or O), then state what the Square of Opposition tells us about the corresponding statement of the type indicated. Give the derived statement explicitly.

  1. Original: "All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd numbers." (A statement, known true)
    What is the truth value of the corresponding O statement? State the O statement and explain.
  2. Original: "No amphibians are warm-blooded animals." (E statement, known true)
    What is the truth value of the corresponding I statement? State it and explain.
  3. Original: "Some geological formations are not products of volcanic activity." (O statement, known true)
    What can we conclude about the corresponding A statement? State it and explain.
  4. Original: "Some planets are bodies with rings." (I statement, known true)
    What can we conclude about the corresponding E statement? Explain.

Part B — Analysis. Each item below presents an original statement and a claimed immediate inference. Evaluate whether the inference is valid. If not, explain precisely what went wrong.

  1. Original: "All ferns are vascular plants." (true)
    Claimed inference: "Some ferns are vascular plants." — Valid or invalid?
  2. Original: "Some mathematical proofs are indirect proofs." (true)
    Claimed inference: "No mathematical proofs are indirect proofs." — Valid or invalid?
  3. Original: "No inert gases form compounds easily." (true)
    Claimed inference: "Some inert gases do not form compounds easily." — Valid or invalid?
  4. Original: "Some historical documents are forgeries." (true)
    Claimed inference: "All historical documents are forgeries." — Valid or invalid?

Part C — Construction. Choose one of the four Square relationships (contradictory, contrary, subcontrary, subalternation). Write an original pair of categorical statements that illustrates the relationship. Then explain in 3–4 sentences why the relationship holds — what logical feature of the statements makes it so. Use a topic from science, history, or mathematics.

Looking Ahead: The Square of Opposition licenses immediate inferences by changing the quantifier and quality of a statement while keeping the subject and predicate in place. The next three lessons introduce three operations — conversion, obversion, and contraposition — that go further: they rearrange or replace the terms themselves. Each operation has its own rules about which statement types it applies to validly.
