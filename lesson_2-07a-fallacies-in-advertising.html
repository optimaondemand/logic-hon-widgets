Lesson 2.07a
Fallacies in Advertising
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the author’s use of rhetoric (ethos, pathos, logos, kairos)  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare the development of multiple arguments; evaluate their effectiveness and validity  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify specific informal fallacies in real advertising claims and explain how advertising exploits fallacious reasoning to influence consumer behavior.

Orientation: Where We Are

Over the past three weeks you built a detailed catalogue of informal fallacies — relevance fallacies, ambiguity fallacies, and presumption fallacies. You can now name them, recognize their structures, and explain why their reasoning fails. This week is different. The question is no longer What is this fallacy? but Where does it live? This lesson answers that question for one of the most prolific environments for fallacious reasoning in modern life: advertising.

Intuitive Entry: The Ad You Can Recite from Memory

You almost certainly have an advertisement you can recite from memory — a jingle, a slogan, a tagline repeated so often it is simply part of the furniture of your mind. You did not decide to remember it. It arrived and stayed. That is not an accident. Advertising is designed, tested, and refined with extraordinary precision. The people who write it are not careless. They are, in many cases, exceptionally skilled at what they do.

Here is a useful provocation: the techniques advertising uses to lodge itself in your memory are largely the same techniques that informal logic identifies as fallacies. The question this lesson asks you to hold is this — if you can name what an ad is doing at the level of reasoning, does that change anything? Does knowing the technique change how the technique affects you?

Why Advertising Is a Fallacy-Rich Environment

The goal of a logical argument is to establish a conclusion as true — to give the listener good reasons to believe something. The goal of an advertisement is to establish a behavior: a purchase, a brand preference, an emotional association. Truth is not irrelevant to advertising, but it is subordinate. This is why advertising is structurally predisposed toward fallacious reasoning. The shortcuts that informal fallacies represent — appealing to emotion, invoking authority rather than argument, exploiting ambiguous language — are exactly the shortcuts that get people to act quickly and without much deliberation. That is what advertising is designed to produce.

This matters for a reason that goes beyond the obvious. Most fallacious reasoning in ordinary conversation is accidental — the speaker is trying to reason well and simply failing. Advertising is different. The fallacies in advertising are, in most cases, deployed intentionally and skillfully. The people writing the copy know they are using an appeal to popularity, an appeal to authority, or a false dilemma. That knowledge is what makes advertising such a clear field of study for this catalogue.

The Fallacy Catalogue in Advertising: Six Cases

1. Ad Populum (Appeal to Popularity)

Advertising type A product claims that the vast majority of professionals in a relevant field use or recommend it. The implied argument: most people in this category choose it, so it must be the right choice.

The ad populum fallacy holds that something is true or correct because many people believe it or choose it. The logical gap is real: popularity does not establish truth, quality, or suitability for your particular situation. The advertisement is counting on you to conflate "widely recommended" with "correct for you." Notice, too, that such statistics are almost always carefully constructed — the population surveyed, the question asked, and the threshold for "recommends" are rarely disclosed.

2. Ad Verecundiam (Unreliable Authority)

Advertising type A well-known athlete endorses a financial product. A beloved entertainer promotes a pharmaceutical brand. The implied argument: this person is admirable and successful; therefore this product is worth using.

The appeal to authority is legitimate when the authority cited has genuine expertise in the domain in question. A cardiologist recommending a heart medication is relevant authority. A famous athlete recommending the same medication is not — the athlete’s expertise lies elsewhere. Celebrity endorsements exploit the emotional association between admiration for a person and trust in their product recommendations. The admiration is real; the logical transfer is invalid.

3. Appeal to Pity (Ad Misericordiam)

Advertising type A charity advertisement presents extended footage of suffering without offering any argument about the charity’s effectiveness, how donations are used, or what outcomes have been achieved. The implied argument: because suffering exists, you should give money to this organization.

The suffering shown in such advertisements is often real and the cause may be genuinely worthy. The fallacy lies not in mentioning suffering — suffering can be relevant evidence — but in using emotional distress as a substitute for an argument. A rigorous advertisement could make the case: "Here is the problem. Here is our approach. Here is our track record. Here is why your support will make a difference." Many such appeals skip all of that and go straight to the emotion. The pity you feel is real. The argument was never made.

4. False Cause (Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc)

Advertising type A testimonial claims that since the speaker began using a product — a supplement, a service, a device — their life in some dimension improved dramatically. The implied argument: using the product caused the improvement.

The post hoc fallacy infers a causal relationship from a temporal one: event B followed event A, therefore A caused B. Testimonials are almost always constructed this way. What they cannot tell you is whether the product actually caused the change, whether the change would have happened anyway, whether other variables were at work, or whether the testimonial was selected from a much larger pool of experiences including negative ones. The narrative is compelling. The causal argument has not been made.

5. False Dilemma

Advertising type An advertisement frames the choice as binary: use this product, or face the problem it supposedly solves. The implied argument: you either use our product or you suffer without it.

False dilemma presents exactly two options when in fact more exist. The advertising version is nearly ubiquitous. It excludes competitor products, the option of doing nothing, the option of a different solution, and the possibility that the problem the product solves is not actually a problem for you. The rhetorical power of the false dilemma is urgency: if the only alternative is suffering, delay seems irrational. Identifying the excluded middle dissolves that urgency.

6. Equivocation

Advertising type A product is labeled or described as "natural," implying that natural things are safe, healthy, or good. The implied argument: this thing is natural; natural things are good; therefore this thing is good.

Equivocation exploits a shift in the meaning of a key term. The word "natural" in everyday use carries strong positive connotations: unprocessed, unadulterated, wholesome. In a more rigorous sense, "natural" simply means occurring in nature — which includes arsenic, mercury, and numerous toxic compounds. The advertising claim exploits the positive connotation while technically making only the neutral claim. The same move appears with words like "pure," "clean," "simple," and "authentic" — each has a favorable colloquial meaning and a more neutral technical one.

Advertising and Aristotle’s Three Appeals

Aristotle’s Rhetoric identified three modes of persuasion: logos (appeal to reason and argument), pathos (appeal to emotion), and ethos (appeal to the character or credibility of the speaker). Kairos — timing and context — is sometimes added as a fourth. These are not fallacies in themselves; they are the legitimate building blocks of rhetoric. The question is always whether a given appeal is doing genuine argumentative work or substituting for it.

Advertising operates primarily on pathos and ethos. Logos appears mainly in the form of pseudo-argument: statistical claims that look precise but conceal their construction, comparative claims that don’t specify what they are compared to ("more refreshing," "cleaner," "better"), and conditional guarantees that are practically impossible to invoke. Understanding Aristotle’s framework gives you a map of what an advertisement is doing at the rhetorical level even as you engage with it at the emotional one.

The Dual Awareness

This lesson is not asking you to stop watching advertisements or to approach every commercial with aggressive suspicion. Advertising is part of the texture of modern life; the skills here are not about withdrawal from that environment but about double vision — the ability to hold both the emotional response and the logical analysis at the same time.

When you watch an advertisement and feel the pull of the appeal to popularity, the goal is not to dismiss that feeling. The goal is to also be able to say: "This argument structure is an ad populum. The popularity claim does not establish the conclusion they want me to draw." That simultaneous awareness — emotional response plus logical analysis — is one of the practical payoffs of this entire unit.

Practice: Identify the Fallacy Type

For each advertising description below, identify the fallacy at work and explain in one sentence why the reasoning fails. Write your answers before reading further.

  1. A sportswear company runs an advertisement showing a celebrated surgeon wearing its brand during surgery. A voiceover says: "The people who demand the best choose [brand]."
  2. A weight-loss product advertisement says: "Either you take control of your health with our system, or you stay where you are." It does not mention any other approaches.
  3. A shampoo advertises that it is "unlike our competitors, free from harsh chemicals," implying the alternative is exposure to something dangerous.
  4. A technology company testimonial: "My productivity doubled after switching to this platform." No data, no comparison group, no timeframe is provided.

When you are ready: identify (a) the fallacy name, (b) the fallacy category (relevance, ambiguity, or presumption), and (c) the specific logical gap. The categories were introduced in Weeks 3–5.
