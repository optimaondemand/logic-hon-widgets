Lesson 7.02a
Constructing a Valid Argument
Learning objective: Students will construct a valid categorical syllogism for a chosen philosophical position by selecting a valid mood and figure, formulating appropriate premises, and verifying validity using the five rules.

Orientation

The whole semester has built toward this point. You began by learning what an argument is and what makes it valid or invalid. You studied terms, statements, the square of opposition, syllogistic moods, figures, named forms, immediate inferences, enthymemes, compound arguments, and the skills of argument analysis. Now you construct. The direction of reasoning reverses: instead of starting with an argument and asking whether it is valid, you start with a position you want to establish and build the argument that establishes it.

Intuitive Entry Point

When someone needs to convince you of something in a serious conversation — not just assert it, but genuinely support it — they often reach for a general principle first: "In general, X is the case. And this particular thing is an X. So this particular thing is the case too." That movement from general principle to particular case is the oldest and most natural form of deductive argument. Aristotle called it the syllogism, and the construction of a syllogism for a philosophical position is exactly what this lesson teaches.

The Construction Procedure

Seven Steps for Constructing a Valid Syllogism

  1. State your conclusion clearly as a categorical statement. Which type is it — A, E, I, or O? Identify its subject term (S) and predicate term (P).
  2. Choose a middle term (M) — the concept that will connect S and P. The middle term must appear in both premises and must be distributed in at least one of them.
  3. Choose a valid mood and figure. Given your conclusion type and your intended middle term, select a valid mood from the course tables (Barbara, Celarent, etc.) that will produce the conclusion type you need. Recall: only certain moods produce A, E, I, or O conclusions.
  4. Formulate the major premise (the premise containing P) according to the mood you selected. Ensure the statement type matches the first letter of the mood code.
  5. Formulate the minor premise (the premise containing S) according to the mood. Ensure the statement type matches the second letter of the mood code.
  6. Write the full syllogism in standard form and confirm that M, S, and P each appear in the correct positions for the figure you selected.
  7. Verify using the five rules. Run through each rule: (1) undistributed middle, (2) illicit major, (3) illicit minor, (4) exclusive premises, (5) affirmative from negative / existential fallacy. The syllogism must pass all five.

Worked Example

Position: "Mathematical truths are not contingent."

Step 1. Conclusion in categorical form: "No mathematical truths are contingent things." This is an E statement. S = mathematical truths; P = contingent things.

Step 2. Middle term: "things that depend on circumstances for their truth." The idea is that mathematical truths do not depend on circumstances, while contingent things do.

Step 3. To get an E conclusion, I need a mood whose third letter is E. From Figure 1: Celarent (EAE-1). Major premise type = E; minor premise type = A; conclusion = E.

Step 4. Major premise (E, contains P): "No things that depend on circumstances for their truth are contingent things" — this is awkward. Simplify M: let M = "things whose truth is independent of circumstances." Then: "No contingent things are things whose truth is independent of circumstances." But Celarent major is E: No M are P — so: "No things whose truth is independent of circumstances are contingent things." ✓

Step 5. Minor premise (A, contains S): "All mathematical truths are things whose truth is independent of circumstances." ✓

Step 6. Standard form:
Major: No things whose truth is independent of circumstances are contingent things. (E)
Minor: All mathematical truths are things whose truth is independent of circumstances. (A)
Conclusion: No mathematical truths are contingent things. (E)

Step 7. Five-rules check: (1) M (things independent of circumstances) — distributed in major (E distributes both terms) ✓; (2) P (contingent things) — distributed in conclusion (E distributes both terms), distributed in major ✓; (3) S (mathematical truths) — distributed in conclusion (E), distributed in minor (A distributes subject) ✓; (4) Both premises are not both negative ✓; (5) Conclusion is negative; one premise (major) is negative ✓. Valid.

Common Error — Choosing M Too Vaguely

The most common construction failure is a middle term that is too broad or too vague to carry the logical weight of the argument. If M does not precisely connect S and P — if it is compatible with S in a different sense than it is compatible with P — the argument will appear valid in form but will commit equivocation. Choose M with enough precision that its meaning is identical in both premises.

Connection to Argument

The construction procedure makes explicit what all good argumentative writing does implicitly. When you write a thesis essay, you are arguing for a conclusion. The logical skeleton of that essay — when stripped of elaboration and illustration — should be a valid argument whose premises are defensible. The construction exercise here is the foundation of the Final Essay: you will build three interlocking valid syllogisms to establish a philosophical position. Every step of this procedure reappears in that project.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Recognition). The following syllogism has been constructed but contains an error in the middle term that causes equivocation. Identify the error and explain why it undermines the argument.

Major: All sharp things cut through resistance.
Minor: All witty remarks are sharp things.
Conclusion: All witty remarks cut through resistance.

Problem 2 (Analysis). Use the seven-step procedure to verify whether the following constructed argument is valid. Identify the mood and figure, state the distribution status of all three terms in both premises and the conclusion, and apply all five rules.

Major: No incomplete systems of reasoning are sufficient for formal proof.
Minor: Some natural-language arguments are incomplete systems of reasoning.
Conclusion: Some natural-language arguments are not sufficient for formal proof.

Problem 3 (Construction). Choose one of the following positions and construct a valid categorical syllogism establishing it. Use the seven-step procedure. State your mood and figure, write the full standard-form syllogism, and run all five rules.
  • Position A: "All valid deductive arguments preserve truth."
  • Position B: "Some acts of reasoning are not infallible."
  • Position C: "No circular arguments establish their conclusions independently."
Looking Ahead: Lesson 7.02b addresses constructing compound arguments — how to link multiple syllogisms so that the conclusion of one serves as a premise of the next, building toward a philosophical position that requires more than one inferential step.
