Contraries and Subcontraries
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning  |  ELA.11.C.5.1 — Use revision to strengthen reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to state the rules governing contraries (A–E) and subcontraries (I–O), apply those rules to determine truth values or recognize that a truth value is undetermined, and explain why these relationships are weaker than contradictories.

Where We Are

Lesson 3.04b established that contradictories always determine each other — knowing the truth value of one member of a contradictory pair tells you with certainty the truth value of the other. The two relationships in this lesson — contraries (the top horizontal of the Square) and subcontraries (the bottom horizontal) — work differently. They provide partial information rather than complete information. They tell you something in some cases, and nothing in others. Understanding when a relationship yields a definite answer and when it yields "undetermined" is what this lesson is about.

Contraries: The A–E Relationship

Contraries occupy the top of the Square: the A statement (All S are P) and the E statement (No S are P) share the same subject and predicate terms but make opposite claims at the universal level — one asserts complete inclusion, the other asserts complete exclusion.

The Rule of Contraries (A–E):

Contrary statements cannot both be true, but can both be false.

— If A is true, E is false.
— If E is true, A is false.
— If A is false, E is undetermined (could be true or false).
— If E is false, A is undetermined (could be true or false).

Why can contraries both be false? Consider: "All birds can fly" (A) and "No birds can fly" (E). Both are false — penguins and ostriches cannot fly, so the A fails; eagles and sparrows certainly can fly, so the E fails. There is a middle ground between complete inclusion and complete exclusion. That middle ground is precisely what makes "cannot both be true" hold while "can both be false" also holds.

The inference from truth goes one direction clearly: if one universal is true, the other is false. A class cannot be entirely inside P and entirely outside P at the same time. But the inference from falsity is blocked: if the A is false, the E might be false (as in the bird example) or it might happen to be true (consider: "All birds are mammals" is false; "No birds are mammals" is also true). You cannot determine E from the mere falsity of A.

If you know this (A–E)……then this follows
A is TrueE is False
E is TrueA is False
A is FalseE is Undetermined
E is FalseA is Undetermined
Worked Example — Contraries (from truth)

Given (TRUE): "No integer is simultaneously odd and even." (E statement)

What follows for A: "All integers are simultaneously odd and even." — This is False. Since the E is true, the A must be false. The complete exclusion (E) rules out the complete inclusion (A).

What if E were false instead? If "No integer is simultaneously odd and even" were false (which it is not, but hypothetically), we could not determine whether "All integers are simultaneously odd and even" is true or false — that would require additional evidence.

Subcontraries: The I–O Relationship

Subcontraries occupy the bottom of the Square: the I statement (Some S are P) and the O statement (Some S are not P). They are the particular counterparts of the contraries — and their rule is, in a sense, the mirror image.

The Rule of Subcontraries (I–O):

Subcontrary statements cannot both be false, but can both be true.

— If I is false, O is true.
— If O is false, I is true.
— If I is true, O is undetermined (could be true or false).
— If O is true, I is undetermined (could be true or false).

Why can both be true? "Some birds can fly" (I, true) and "Some birds cannot fly" (O, also true). Both particular claims are true simultaneously — the bird class is large enough to include both flyers and non-flyers. There is no contradiction in saying "at least one is P" and "at least one is not P" when the subject class has members of both kinds.

Why cannot both be false? If "Some S are P" is false, it means the intersection of S and P is empty — no S is P. In that case, every S is outside P, which means "Some S are not P" must be true. Conversely, if "Some S are not P" is false, it means every S is inside P, which forces "Some S are P" to be true. The falsity of one particular always implies the truth of the other.

If you know this (I–O)……then this follows
I is FalseO is True
O is FalseI is True
I is TrueO is Undetermined
O is TrueI is Undetermined
Worked Example — Subcontraries (from falsity)

Given (FALSE): "Some prime numbers are even numbers greater than 2." (I statement — false, since all even numbers greater than 2 are composite)

What follows for O: "Some prime numbers are not even numbers greater than 2." — This is True. Since the I statement is false, the O must be true. In fact, all prime numbers other than 2 are odd, so many primes are not even numbers greater than 2.

Common Error — Inferring Subcontrary Truth from Truth

A student reasons: "Some birds can fly (I is true). Therefore, some birds cannot fly (O is true)."

Analysis: This happens to be correct in fact — but the inference is not valid from the I–O relationship alone. The truth of I does not determine the truth of O. What if the I statement were "Some mammals have hearts" (true)? The O would be "Some mammals do not have hearts" — which is false, since all mammals have hearts. From I being true, O is undetermined. You cannot infer the O from the I; you can only infer the O from the falsity of the I. This is the most common error made with subcontraries.

Contraries vs. Contradictories: A Critical Comparison

Students frequently confuse contraries and contradictories. The key distinction:

  • Contradictories (A–O, E–I): Completely determine each other in all cases. If one is true, the other is false; if one is false, the other is true. No undetermined cases.
  • Contraries (A–E): Only determine each other from truth, not from falsity. If one is true, the other is false. But if one is false, the other is undetermined.

The practical consequence: if someone shows that "All ravens are black" is false, you cannot conclude "No ravens are black." You can only conclude "Some ravens are not black" (the contradictory O). Jumping from the falsity of A to the truth of E is the contrary error — and it is one of the most common reasoning mistakes in arguments about universal claims.

Practice

Part A — Contraries. For each problem, apply the contrary rule to determine what follows. Write "True," "False," or "Undetermined" and explain in one sentence.

  1. Given TRUE: "All squares are rectangles." → What follows for "No squares are rectangles"?
  2. Given FALSE: "All arguments contain hidden premises." → What follows for "No arguments contain hidden premises"?
  3. Given TRUE: "No prime number greater than 2 is divisible by 2." → What follows for "All prime numbers greater than 2 are divisible by 2"?
  4. Given FALSE: "No historical figure was both a scientist and a philosopher." → What follows for "All historical figures were both scientists and philosophers"?

Part B — Subcontraries. For each problem, apply the subcontrary rule.

  1. Given FALSE: "Some triangles have four sides." → What follows for "Some triangles do not have four sides"?
  2. Given TRUE: "Some historical arguments are not valid." → What follows for "Some historical arguments are valid"?
  3. Given FALSE: "Some mammals are cold-blooded." → What follows for "Some mammals are not cold-blooded"?

Part C — Distinguishing Relationships. For each pair of statements, identify the Square relationship (Contradictory, Contrary, or Subcontrary) and apply the appropriate rule.

  1. "All valid arguments are sound arguments" (given TRUE) → "No valid arguments are sound arguments"
  2. "No equilateral triangle is scalene" (given TRUE) → "Some equilateral triangles are scalene"
  3. "Some premises are true" (given FALSE) → "Some premises are not true"
  4. "Some arguments are deductive" (given TRUE) → "Some arguments are not deductive"
Looking Ahead: Lesson 3.04d completes the Square by examining the two vertical relationships — subalternation and superalternation — which connect universal statements to their corresponding particular statements. These relationships explain why truth flows downward (from universal to particular) and falsity flows upward (from particular to universal).
