Lesson 7.01d — The Five-Step Validity Check: Venn Diagram Practice

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze the structure an author uses to organize a text; ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the reasoning in an author's argument; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to apply a condensed five-step method — standard form, diagram, shade, read, verdict — to test the validity of any categorical syllogism using a three-circle Venn diagram, and distinguish this validity check from the five-step procedure used elsewhere this week to map arguments in text.

Orientation

This week is named for practice, and this lesson is where that practice turns back to the syllogism itself. Elsewhere in Week 8 you are using a five-step mapping procedure to extract the structure of arguments embedded in prose — a different skill for a different purpose. This lesson consolidates a separate five-step method: a compact version of the full diagramming procedure you learned in Lessons 5.02b–5.02d, condensed to its essential moves so you can apply it quickly and reliably to any syllogism you encounter, whether it is handed to you in standard form or buried inside a paragraph.

Intuitive Entry Point

A pilot runs through a pre-flight checklist not because each step is hard on its own, but because skipping a step under time pressure is exactly how errors happen. The five-step validity check works the same way: none of the five moves is new to you, but running them in a fixed order every time is what keeps you from skipping the diagramming or jumping to a verdict before the diagram is finished.

The Five-Step Validity Check

Step 1 — Standard Form. Write both premises as A, E, I, or O statements, with a clear subject (S), predicate (P), and middle term (M). Paraphrase if the original wording is not already in "All/No/Some S are/are not P" form.
Step 2 — Draw and Label. Draw a three-circle Venn diagram: S at the left, P at the right, M at the bottom-center, all three overlapping.
Step 3 — Shade, Then Mark. Diagram every universal premise first (shading the region it declares empty); diagram every particular premise second (placing an X, on a boundary if the sub-region is still ambiguous).
Step 4 — Read the Conclusion. Check the region the conclusion is about. For a universal conclusion, is that region fully shaded? For a particular conclusion, is there an X definitely inside it?
Step 5 — State the Verdict. If the conclusion is already shown, the syllogism is valid. If it is not — if you would need to add a mark not licensed by the premises — it is invalid. Say which regions decided it.

Worked Examples

Example 1
All valid arguments preserve truth from premises to conclusion.
This argument does not preserve truth from premises to conclusion.
Therefore, this argument is not valid.

Step 1: Let S = this argument, P = valid arguments, M = arguments that preserve truth from premises to conclusion. Major: All P are M. Minor: This argument (S) is not M — an O-type claim about S and M, "Some S are not M" restricted to the single case at hand. Conclusion: Some S are not P (this argument is not valid).

Steps 3–5: The major premise shades P outside M. The minor premise places an X for "this argument" in S, outside M (since it is stated not to preserve truth) — safely in the S-only region, away from any shaded area. The conclusion needs an X in S∩P-outside-M. The X sits in S-only, not inside P at all, which is stronger than what the conclusion needs — it confirms this particular argument is outside P entirely. VALID. This is Modus Tollens wearing categorical dress.

Example 2
Some effective study habits are not widely recommended techniques.
All widely recommended techniques are evidence-based methods.
Therefore, some effective study habits are not evidence-based methods.

Step 1: S = effective study habits, P = evidence-based methods, M = widely recommended techniques. Minor (O): Some S are not M. Major (A): All M are P.

Steps 3–5: The major premise (universal) is diagrammed first: shade M outside P. The minor premise (particular) places an X for "some S are not M" — but S-outside-M splits into two sub-regions (outside P, and inside P), and neither has been shaded, so the X must sit on the boundary between them, as shown. The conclusion needs an X definitely in S-outside-P. The X here is not definitely there — it could equally be in the part of S that is outside M but inside P. INVALID. The premises do not rule out that possibility, so the conclusion is not shown.

Connection to Argument

Running these five steps in order, every time, is what turns "does this feel valid?" into "is this valid?" — a mechanical answer that does not depend on how persuasive the argument sounds. That reliability is exactly what you will need across the next two weeks, when you apply this same check to arguments embedded in real texts and to the syllogisms underlying your own Final Essay.

Practice Problems

For each syllogism, run all five steps. Show your standard-form translation, describe or sketch your diagram, and state your verdict with justification.

  1. All planets that orbit within the habitable zone are worlds capable of supporting liquid water. Some worlds discovered by this survey are not planets that orbit within the habitable zone. Therefore, some worlds discovered by this survey are not worlds capable of supporting liquid water.
  2. No argument that begs the question provides independent support for its conclusion. This argument provides independent support for its conclusion. Therefore, this argument does not beg the question.
  3. Some historical treaties were not honored by every signatory. All treaties honored by every signatory are treaties that achieved their stated purpose. Therefore, some historical treaties are not treaties that achieved their stated purpose.
Looking Ahead: With both five-step procedures now firmly in hand — mapping an argument's structure in text, and checking a syllogism's validity with a diagram — you are ready to combine them on the extended passages in the rest of this week's work, and to carry both into the Final Essay project beginning next week.
