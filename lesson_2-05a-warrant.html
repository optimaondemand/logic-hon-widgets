Lesson 2.05a
The Warrant: What Connects a Premise to a Conclusion

Learning Objective: Students will be able to explain why an argument can fail even when its premises are relevant to the conclusion and free of ambiguity, identify when a premise lacks independent warrant, and recognize this failure as the root of begging the question.

Orientation: Where We Are

You have now studied two of the three major categories of informal fallacy. Fallacies of relevance (Lesson Group 2.03) fail because their premises do not actually bear on the conclusion. Fallacies of ambiguity (Lesson Group 2.04) fail because the meaning of a key term shifts between premise and conclusion. It would be natural to assume that an argument avoiding both of these defects — an argument whose premises are relevant and whose terms are used consistently (univocally) — must be a good argument. This lesson shows why that assumption is false, and in doing so opens the third and subtlest category of informal fallacy: fallacies of presumption.

Intuitive Entry: A Premise That Only Sounds Supported

Consider this exchange. Someone claims: "You can trust this scheduling software — it was built by a highly reliable development team." You ask the natural follow-up question: "How do you know the team is reliable?" The answer comes back: "Because everything they build is trustworthy, including this software."

Look closely at what happened. The premise ("the team is reliable") is relevant to the conclusion ("the software is trustworthy") — a reliable team's work really would give you reason to trust its output. No term shifted meaning along the way; "trustworthy" and "reliable" are being used consistently throughout. And yet the argument has gone nowhere. The only reason offered for believing the premise was the conclusion itself. Strip away the appearance of two separate claims, and there is really just one claim, propping itself up.

This is the failure this lesson is about. It is not a failure of relevance, and it is not a failure of ambiguity. It is a failure of warrant: the premise was never given a reason to be believed that did not already assume the conclusion.

The Formal Concept: Warrant for a Premise

A premise has warrant (from Old French warant, "guarantee" or "authorization") when there is a reason to accept it that does not depend on already accepting the argument's conclusion. Warrant, in this sense, is not about whether the premise connects logically to the conclusion — that is a question of relevance. It is about whether the premise itself has earned its place as something you should believe, independently of the very claim it is being used to support.

This requirement has a long history under a different name. In the Posterior Analytics, Aristotle (384–322 BCE) argued that genuine scientific demonstration must proceed from premises that are true, primary, and — crucially — better known than the conclusion: known on grounds that do not run through the conclusion itself. An argument that reverses this order, drawing its only support for a premise from the conclusion the premise is meant to establish, has the structure of demonstration without the substance of it. It looks like reasoning because it has the right shape — premises leading to a conclusion — but nothing has actually been shown, because nothing outside the circle of the argument itself was ever offered as support.

Put simply: one would not accept the premise if one did not already accept the conclusion. When that is true of an argument, the argument fails no matter how relevant its premises are and no matter how carefully its terms are used. This is what "lacking warrant" means, and it is the defect at the heart of every fallacy of presumption.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Circularity Within a Single Sentence

Argument: "This restaurant must be good, since it's so popular — and it's popular because it's good."

Analysis: The premise offered for "it's good" is "it's popular," and the premise offered for "it's popular" is "it's good." Each claim is being used to support the other. Neither one is warranted by anything outside this pair. A diner who has never eaten there has been given no actual information about the food.

Example 2 — Circularity Spread Across a Longer Passage

Argument: "You can trust this scheduling software — it was built by a highly reliable development team. And we know the team is reliable, because everything they build, including this software, is trustworthy."

Analysis: Here the circularity is harder to spot because the two claims are separated by several sentences and dressed in different vocabulary ("reliable team," "trustworthy software"). The premise ("the team is reliable") is relevant to the conclusion, and no term is ambiguous. But when asked why the premise should be believed, the only answer given loops back to the conclusion. This is the same defect as Example 1, spread out enough to disguise it.

Example 3 — A Premise With Genuine (if Imperfect) Warrant

Argument: "This product has been sold for thirty years without a single reported injury, so it is probably safe."

Analysis: This is not circular. The premise — a thirty-year record with no reported injuries — is a fact that can be checked without first assuming the product is safe: sales records and injury reports are independent sources. The warrant here is contestable (injuries can go unreported, so the inference is not airtight), but it is a genuine, independent warrant, not a borrowed one. Contrast this with Examples 1 and 2, where no independent source was ever offered at all.

Common Confusion — "But the Premise Really Is Relevant"

Students sometimes object: "The premise in Example 2 really would support the conclusion, if it were true. Isn't that enough?" It is not, and the reason is important. Relevance tells you that if the premise is true, it would bear on the conclusion. Warrant tells you whether you have any actual grounds for thinking the premise is true. An argument can pass the relevance test and the univocity test — the premise plainly bears on the conclusion, and no word changes meaning — and still fail, because the only route to believing the premise runs through the conclusion. Relevance and clarity of meaning are necessary for a good argument; they are not sufficient. Warrant is a third, independent requirement, and it is the one fallacies of presumption violate.

Why This Matters: The Root of Begging the Question

Fallacies of relevance fail at the level of the premise-to-conclusion connection: the premises, even if true, do not relate to the conclusion. Fallacies of ambiguity fail at the level of meaning: a term shifts between premise and conclusion. Fallacies of presumption fail at a third level entirely: the premises are relevant and univocal, but at least one of them has no warrant except the conclusion itself.

The most direct form this takes is begging the question (Latin petitio principii), which you will study by name in the next lesson. Begging the question is not simply "assuming what you're trying to prove" in some vague sense — it is precisely this: an argument in which a premise's only support is the conclusion, so that accepting the premise already requires having accepted the conclusion. Learning to ask "would I accept this premise if I did not already accept the conclusion?" is the single most useful question you can ask when an argument feels suspiciously airtight.

Practice Problems

Recognition. For each argument below, determine whether the premise has independent warrant or whether the only support for the premise is the conclusion itself. Explain your answer in one or two sentences.

  1. "The committee's decision must be fair, since the committee only ever reaches fair decisions."
  2. "He has been a runner for fifteen years, so he should have no trouble completing this race."
  3. "This translation is the most accurate one available, because it captures the poem's true meaning — and we know it captures the poem's true meaning because it is the most accurate translation available."
  4. "The weather forecast predicts rain tomorrow, so the outdoor ceremony will probably be disrupted."

Analysis. For each argument below, identify the premise whose warrant is in question, and explain what independent evidence (evidence that does not presuppose the conclusion) would need to exist for the premise to be genuinely warranted.

  1. "This novel must be a masterpiece — it says so right on the cover, and the publisher wouldn't print that unless it were true."
  2. "The new debate coach is clearly excellent, since the team has been winning constantly under her — and they've been winning constantly because she's clearly an excellent coach."

Think Further.

  1. Explain, in your own words, why an argument can satisfy both the relevance requirement and the univocity requirement and still fail. What does the warrant requirement add that those two do not already cover? Write a paragraph defending your answer.
