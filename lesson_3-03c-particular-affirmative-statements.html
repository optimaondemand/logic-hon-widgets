Lesson 3.03c — Particular Affirmative Statements (I Statements)

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite strong and thorough textual evidence to support analysis  |  ELA.11.V.1.1 — Integrate academic vocabulary to achieve precise meaning  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify and write I statements, translate natural-language sentences into standard I-statement form, explain why neither the subject nor predicate term is distributed, and understand what "some" asserts — and does not assert — in the context of categorical logic.

Where We Are

You have now studied both universal statement types: the A statement (All S are P) and the E statement (No S are P). Both speak about every member of the subject class without exception. The I statement introduces a different logical register: the particular. It makes a claim about some — at least one — member of a class, not about all of them. This is a shift in scope, and it changes the distribution of both terms in a fundamental way.

The I and O statements are often called "weaker" than their universal counterparts, but this is imprecise. They are not weak — they are making a different, less sweeping claim. Understanding why the distribution changes requires holding clearly in mind what "some" actually means in formal logic, which is not quite what it means in ordinary speech.

Intuitive Entry Point

You are told: "Some professional chess players are also accomplished musicians." You have no trouble accepting that. It is a modest claim — it asserts only that the two groups overlap, that at least a few people fall into both categories. You are not being told that most chess players are musicians, or that all of them are, or that chess and music require similar mental abilities. The claim is precisely as modest as it sounds: there is some overlap.

Now notice what you cannot infer from this claim. You cannot conclude that "some musicians are chess players" — even though in fact that might be true. Wait — actually, you can conclude that, and we will show why shortly. But you cannot conclude that "most musicians play chess," or that "chess playing causes musical ability." The I statement asserts exactly one thing: the intersection of two classes is not empty. Nothing more.

The I Statement: "Some S are P"

The I statement is the particular affirmative categorical statement. Its standard form is:

Some S are P

where S is the subject term and P is the predicate term. The I statement asserts that at least one member of the S class also belongs to the P class. It does not say how many — just that the number is greater than zero.

The letter I comes from the Latin affirmo, "I affirm." The second vowel of affirmo is I. The "I" statement is the weaker of the two affirmative types — it affirms not a complete inclusion (A) but a partial one.

The Meaning of "Some"

In formal categorical logic, "some" means "at least one." This is a technical stipulation, and it differs from ordinary speech in an important way. In conversation, "some" often implies "some but not all" — if a friend says "some of the cookies are chocolate chip," you naturally infer they are not all chocolate chip. In logic, no such implication holds. "Some philosophers are Greeks" is compatible with "All philosophers are Greeks" — the particular does not contradict the universal; it is simply a weaker claim.

This matters for argument construction. If you use an I statement as a premise, you have made the most modest possible affirmative claim about the relationship between two classes. You can strengthen it later with evidence, but you cannot deduce more from it than it contains.

Why Neither Term Is Distributed

Distribution follows from what a statement claims about all members of a class. In the I statement:

  • S is not distributed. The statement speaks only about some members of S — those that overlap with P. The remaining members of S are unaddressed. We are not making any claim about all members of S.
  • P is not distributed. The statement places some S inside P, but it says nothing about P's other members. It does not claim that all members of P are touched by this assertion. Many members of P may have nothing to do with S, and the I statement is silent about them.

The result: the I statement is the least committal of the four types. It asserts only that two circles overlap — not how much, not whether one includes the other, not that the overlap covers all members of either class. It is the minimum affirmative claim you can make in categorical logic.

Worked Example 1 — Distribution in the I Statement

Statement: "Some historians are novelists."

Subject (S): historians    Predicate (P): novelists

Distribution: S (historians) is not distributed — we are not speaking about all historians, only about some of them. P (novelists) is not distributed — we are not saying anything about the full class of novelists; many novelists have nothing to do with the subset of historians under discussion.

Diagram: Two partially overlapping circles — a region of historians who are also novelists, a region of historians who are not novelists, and a region of novelists who are not historians. The I statement asserts only that the overlap region is not empty.

Worked Example 2 — Why the I Statement Converts Validly

Original: "Some historians are novelists."

Converted: "Some novelists are historians."

The general test for whether a conversion is valid is this: swapping subject and predicate is valid only if no term ends up distributed in the converted statement that was not already distributed in the original. Swapping positions can force a term into a distributed position — the subject of a universal statement is always distributed, and the predicate of a negative statement is always distributed — and if that happens to a term that was not distributed before the swap, the conversion has claimed more than the original statement authorized.

Apply that test here. In "Some historians are novelists," neither S (historians) nor P (novelists) is distributed. After conversion, "Some novelists are historians" is still a particular affirmative statement, so neither of its terms is distributed either — historians, now in predicate position, is not distributed, because the predicate of a particular affirmative is never distributed. No term became newly distributed. Nothing was claimed that the original did not already authorize, so the conversion is valid. "Some S are P" and "Some P are S" say exactly the same thing: the intersection of the two classes is non-empty.

This is also why it is not enough to check only whether the predicate is distributed. The A and O statements do not convert simply, but for different reasons in each case — you will work through the O statement's case in the next lesson and in this week's assignment. The reliable test is always the same: does the swap distribute a term that was not distributed before?

Common Error — Reading "Some" as "Some but Not All"

A student reasons: "Some mammals are aquatic creatures. Therefore, some mammals are not aquatic creatures."

Analysis: This does not follow. In formal logic, "some" means "at least one" — it does not exclude the possibility that all members of S are P. "Some mammals are aquatic" is compatible with "All mammals are aquatic," even though we know independently that all mammals are not aquatic. You cannot derive a particular negative from a particular affirmative. Inferring "some but not all" from "some" smuggles in information that the statement does not contain.

Common Error — Inferring the Universal from the Particular

A student reasons: "Some birds can fly. Therefore, all birds can fly."

Analysis: The I statement never supports the corresponding A statement. The particular is a weaker claim than the universal; the stronger does not follow from the weaker. This is the fallacy of hasty generalization in formal dress — moving from "some" to "all" without warrant.

Natural-Language Forms of the I Statement

I statements appear in many surface forms:

  • "Some X are Y" → already standard
  • "There are X that are Y" → "Some X are Y"
  • "A few X are Y" → "Some X are Y"
  • "Many X are Y" → "Some X are Y" (in standard form — "many" reduces to "at least one")
  • "Most X are Y" → "Some X are Y" (in standard form — "most" reduces to "at least one"; the quantitative precision of "most" is informal and has no direct equivalent in categorical logic)
  • "X can be Y" → often translatable as "Some X are Y"

Note: "many" and "most" are both translated as "some" in standard categorical form. This is a simplification — categorical logic does not have a quantifier for "most." Acknowledge this when it matters for the argument being analyzed.

Existential Import

The I statement has what logicians call existential import: it asserts that the subject class is non-empty. "Some historians are novelists" only makes sense if there are historians. This becomes relevant in the Square of Opposition, which you will study in Week 9, because it affects the inferential relationships between the four statement types. For now, note that the I statement is the minimum claim of existence in categorical logic — it says that at least one S exists and is P.

Why This Matters for Argument

The I statement is the typical form of an empirical generalization in its early stage. When a scientist reports "Some metals conduct electricity at room temperature," she is making an I statement. When a historian notes "Some revolutions have produced democratic governments," he is making an I statement. These are cautious, defensible claims — their strength lies in the fact that a single confirmed instance makes them true.

In formal argument, the I statement functions as an existential premise: it establishes that a category is not empty and that two categories overlap. This is often the minimum required to support a more specific conclusion. Recognizing when an argument depends on an I statement helps you see how vulnerable or how solid its empirical foundation is.

Practice

Part A — Recognition. For each statement, identify whether it is an I statement. If not, explain why.

  1. "Some arguments contain hidden premises."
  2. "All observed cases of X have been followed by Y."
  3. "A few classical texts address the theory of knowledge directly."
  4. "No fallacy is a valid form of argument."
  5. "Many students find formal logic challenging at first."

Part B — Distribution. For each I statement, identify S and P and confirm that neither is distributed. Give a one-sentence explanation for why P is not distributed.

  1. "Some arguments are sound."
  2. "Some mammals can survive in Arctic temperatures."
  3. "Some medieval philosophers were also mathematicians."

Part C — Translation. Translate each sentence into standard I-statement form. Identify S and P.

  1. "There are logicians who were also theologians."
  2. "A few of the original arguments in the document were valid."
  3. "Many rivers in this region are navigable by large vessels."
  4. "People can be both honest and tactful."

Part D — Conversion. Convert each I statement. Confirm that the conversion is logically equivalent.

  1. "Some scientists are historians."
  2. "Some geometrical figures are not regular polygons." (Note: does this convert simply? Why or why not?)
