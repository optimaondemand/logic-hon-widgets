Identifying Arguments in Prose
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning  |  ELA.K12.EE.3.1 — Make inferences to support comprehension  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare the development of multiple arguments; evaluate their effectiveness and validity  |  ELA.K12.EE.2.1 — Read and comprehend grade-level complex texts proficiently

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify arguments in prose passages, distinguish argumentative from non-argumentative writing, and extract the conclusion and premises in standard form.

Orientation: Where We Are

The previous lesson gave you the formal tools: the definitions of premises and conclusion, the indicator words, the standard form convention. This lesson applies those tools to the real conditions of reading — prose that mixes argument with description, narration, emotional appeal, and background information. Extracting an argument from prose is a skill that improves only with practice. This lesson is weighted toward that practice. The concepts introduced here — including an initial look at deductive arguments — are brief; the bulk of the lesson is the work of extraction.

Intuitive Entry: Argument Embedded in Writing

Most writing you encounter does not label its parts. A news editorial, a scientific report, a historical analysis — these may contain genuine arguments, but the arguments are woven into sentences that also include background context, qualifications, examples, and narrative. A skilled reader must be able to pull out the logical skeleton: what claim is being established, and what is being offered as support for it? The surrounding material is important for understanding and evaluating the argument, but it is not part of the argument itself.

Think of it as separating the frame from the insulation. Both are in the wall; only one is doing the structural work.

Most Writing Is Not Argumentative

It is worth being explicit about this: the majority of written language is not argumentative. Descriptions report what is the case. Narratives recount sequences of events. Instructions tell you what to do. Explanations account for why something happened. Reports compile information. Expressions convey a speaker's attitudes. None of these constitute arguments unless they are organized to provide reasons for a conclusion.

This means that the first question to ask of any text is not "what are the premises?" but "is there an argument here at all?" The test has two conditions, both required:

  1. Is there a claim the author is trying to establish? (Not just report or describe — establish, defend, or prove.)
  2. Is there an attempt to give reasons for that claim?

If both conditions are met, there is an argument. If either is absent, there is not. Apply this test before doing anything else.

A First Look at Deductive Arguments

This course will return to this distinction in depth in Week 2, but a brief introduction is appropriate here. A deductive argument (from Latin deducere, "to lead down from") is one that claims its conclusion follows necessarily from its premises: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. There is no room for the conclusion to be false while the premises are true — the conclusion is, in a sense, already contained in the premises.

Example of a deductive argument:
All mammals are warm-blooded.
Dolphins are mammals.
Therefore, dolphins are warm-blooded.

If both premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. This is the mark of a deductive argument.

Many arguments you will encounter are not deductive — they offer evidence that makes a conclusion more or less probable, without guaranteeing it. Those are inductive arguments, and they will be treated in detail later. For now, the important distinction is that deductive arguments claim necessity: they claim the conclusion cannot be false if the premises are true.

Worked Examples: Argument Extraction

Example 1 — Argument Embedded in Description

"The university library has kept detailed usage logs for the past forty years. In-person reading-room visits have declined by roughly 3,000 per year since digital journal access became available in 2005. Several print journal subscriptions that were checked out regularly through the 1990s have not been checked out at all in the past five years. The way researchers access journal content has shifted measurably."

Analysis: Is there an argument? Apply the test. Is there a claim being established? Yes — the last sentence advances a claim about a measurable shift in how content is accessed. Are reasons offered? Yes — the preceding sentences describe data (declining visits, unused print subscriptions) that support this conclusion. The first sentence (background about the logs) is context, not a premise.

P1: In-person reading-room visits have declined by roughly 3,000 per year since digital journal access became available in 2005.
P2: Several print journal subscriptions that were checked out regularly through the 1990s have not been checked out at all in the past five years.
∴ C: The way researchers access journal content has shifted measurably.
Example 2 — Non-Argumentative Prose

"The Roman aqueducts supplied cities across the empire with fresh water. The Aqua Claudia, completed around 52 CE, stretched over 68 kilometers and was considered an engineering marvel. Roman engineers used a precise gradient — roughly 34 centimeters per kilometer — to allow gravity to move water without pumps. Many sections of the aqueduct system remained in use for centuries after the fall of the Western Empire."

Analysis: This passage reports facts about Roman aqueducts. There is no claim being established through reasoning — no conclusion that the reader is being invited to accept on the basis of other statements. Each sentence contributes to a description. This is informative non-argumentative prose. Applying the test: there is no central claim being defended; there is no attempt to provide reasons for such a claim. No argument is present.

Common Error — Confusing Explanation with Argument

"The lake froze over early this year because an unusual cold front moved through the region in October, bringing temperatures well below the seasonal average for three consecutive weeks."

Analysis: This passage uses "because," which is a premise indicator — but it is being used here to explain an event that has already occurred, not to provide a reason to believe a contested claim. The sentence tells us why the lake froze. It does not argue that the lake froze, or that anything else should be believed. Explanations and arguments share some vocabulary ("because," "since") but serve different purposes. An explanation accounts for a known fact; an argument supports a conclusion that might otherwise be in doubt. The distinction matters because an explanation is not a candidate for evaluation as valid or invalid — it is evaluated for accuracy. This is one of the trickiest distinctions in this unit. When you see "because," ask: is this accounting for a known fact, or providing grounds for accepting a claim?

Practice Problems

This lesson's practice set is intentionally longer. The skill of identifying arguments develops through repetition, not through reading an explanation once. Work through each passage carefully.

Instructions: For each passage, answer two questions: (1) Does this passage contain an argument? Write "Argument" or "Not an argument." (2) If it is an argument, write it in standard form (P1, P2, ∴ C). If it is not, write one sentence identifying what kind of writing it is instead (description, explanation, narration, bare assertion, etc.).

  1. Passage 1 "The Sahara Desert was not always a desert. Archaeological evidence and geological surveys indicate that between approximately 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the region received significantly more rainfall than it does today. Rock art depicting hippos, crocodiles, and cattle — none of which could survive in today's Sahara — has been found at dozens of sites across the region."
  2. Passage 2 "Steel bridges require regular inspection because corrosion can develop in joints and load-bearing members that are not easily visible from the surface. Since corrosion in these structural elements compromises load capacity, a bridge with uninspected joints poses a risk to users. Regular inspection schedules are therefore necessary for public safety."
  3. Passage 3 "On the morning of the third day, the crew spotted land. They had been at sea for forty-one days and had rationed their fresh water to less than a liter per person per day during the final week. The navigator recorded the date, time, and coordinates in the ship's log."
  4. Passage 4 "The population of the town in 1880 was 3,400. By 1900, it had grown to 11,200. The railway line connecting the town to the regional center was completed in 1883. The growth is almost certainly attributable to the railway, since no other major infrastructure or economic change occurred in that period."
  5. Passage 5 "Always calibrate the instrument before taking a measurement. Allow the sensor to warm up for at least ten minutes. Record three separate readings and use the average."
  6. Passage 6 "Migratory birds navigate using a combination of the sun's position, the Earth's magnetic field, and recognizable landmarks. This was established through a series of elegant experiments in which birds were raised in isolation from each other and in enclosures that blocked normal visual cues, yet still oriented consistently in the correct migratory direction."
  7. Passage 7 "The northern slope of the ridge receives less direct sunlight than the southern slope. Vegetation on north-facing slopes therefore tends to retain moisture longer and supports different plant communities — typically more shade-tolerant species — than south-facing slopes of similar elevation."
  8. Passage 8 "The telescope has been an invaluable tool for astronomy. Without it, our knowledge of distant galaxies would be severely limited. Modern telescopes can detect objects billions of light-years away."
  9. Passage 9 "The revised edition of the text is substantially longer than the original, includes three additional chapters, and has been reorganized to follow a chronological rather than thematic structure. Readers familiar with the first edition will find the new arrangement easier to follow and the additional chapters essential for understanding the later period."
  10. Passage 10 "Because the final exam is cumulative, students who did not review the early material are at a significant disadvantage. The early material includes the foundational vocabulary used in every subsequent unit. A student who does not know that vocabulary will struggle to follow the later arguments, even if they understood those arguments when they were first presented. Reviewing from the beginning is therefore the most efficient preparation strategy."
Looking Ahead: Week 1 has equipped you to identify arguments, distinguish them from other uses of language, name their parts, and write them in standard form. Week 2 introduces the formal properties by which arguments are evaluated: validity and soundness. With those tools, the question changes from "what is this argument?" to "is this argument any good?"
