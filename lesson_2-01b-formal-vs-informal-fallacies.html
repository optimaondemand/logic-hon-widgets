Formal vs. Informal Fallacies
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments, evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning  |  ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the author's use of rhetoric (ethos, pathos, logos)  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning to recognize fallacies isn't just an academic exercise—it's an essential part of thinking clearly, communicating effectively, and making good decisions.

Fallacies matter because they pretend to be good reasoning. If we can't tell the difference between a solid argument and a misleading one, we're more likely to believe things we shouldn't, or reject things we should accept.

Fallacies appear in:

  • News headlines and political speeches
  • Advertising and social media posts
  • Everyday conversations and debates

When we don't recognize fallacies, we're more vulnerable to manipulation. We might be persuaded by an argument simply because it sounds confident, appeals to our emotions, or repeats something we've heard before.

But spotting a fallacy allows us to pause and ask a better question:

"Does this actually support the conclusion, or just feel like it does?"

Fallacies also matter when we're the ones making arguments. If we want to reason well—and be taken seriously—we need to avoid faulty patterns ourselves. Learning to catch fallacies in our own thinking is a key part of becoming more rational, honest, and intellectually responsible.

In short, fallacies matter because truth matters, and reasoning is how we get there.
