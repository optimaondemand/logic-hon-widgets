Lesson 3.03b — Universal Negative Statements (E Statements)

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite strong and thorough textual evidence to support analysis  |  ELA.11.V.1.1 — Integrate academic vocabulary to achieve precise meaning  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify and write E statements, translate natural-language sentences into standard E-statement form, explain why both the subject and predicate terms are distributed, and distinguish the E statement from the A statement by the direction and completeness of the claim being made.

Where We Are

Lesson 3.03a introduced the A statement — the universal affirmative — and established the AEIO framework for categorical statements. You learned that in an A statement, the subject term is distributed and the predicate term is not. You also learned the mnemonic: the vowels of affirmo and nego name the four statement types in order of scope. Now you take the second step: the E statement, the universal negative — the categorical claim that two classes share no members whatsoever.

The E statement is in several ways the mirror image of the A statement. Where the A statement includes every member of S inside the P class, the E statement excludes every member of S from the P class entirely. That mirroring is not just visual; it has direct consequences for distribution, for inference, and for how the statement behaves inside an argument.

Intuitive Entry Point

Think about what it takes to say "No students in this school have failed every course they have ever taken." To prove this statement true, you would need to examine every student and confirm for each one that they have passed at least one course. One student who has failed every course they ever took would make the statement false. You need a clean sweep — not a single exception.

Now notice what is happening on the other side of the claim. When you say no student falls into the "failed every course" category, you are also, implicitly, making a claim that covers the entire "failed every course" category: you are saying that none of the members of that category are students here. The exclusion goes both ways. This mutual completeness of exclusion is what makes the E statement logically powerful — and it is why both its terms are distributed, which you will see is different from every other statement type except one.

The E Statement: "No S are P"

The E statement is the universal negative categorical statement. Its standard form is:

No S are P

where S is the subject term and P is the predicate term. The E statement asserts that the classes named by S and P are entirely separate — that the intersection of the two classes is empty. There is not a single thing that belongs to both S and P at once.

The letter E comes from the Latin nego, meaning "I deny." The first vowel of nego is E. This is the universal denial: not a partial denial, not a denial of most, but a complete denial of any membership overlap.

Why Both Terms Are Distributed

Recall the definition: a term is distributed in a statement when the statement makes a claim about all members of the class that term names. In the A statement ("All S are P"), only S is distributed — the predicate P's other members remain unaddressed. The E statement is different in a critical way: both S and P are distributed.

Here is the reasoning. "No S are P" does two things simultaneously:

  • It refers to every member of S and excludes them all from P. The statement covers all of S — so S is distributed.
  • For any member of P, the statement implies that no member of S belongs to it. The statement effectively rules out every member of P as a candidate for S. P is fully covered by the exclusion — so P is also distributed.

Another way to see this: "No dogs are reptiles" and "No reptiles are dogs" say exactly the same thing. The E statement is symmetrical — its subject and predicate can be swapped and the meaning is preserved. This is called simple conversion, and it works for E statements precisely because both terms are fully distributed. No other statement type converts simply while remaining equivalent in both directions.

Worked Example 1 — Distribution in the E Statement

Statement: "No mammals are cold-blooded creatures."

Subject (S): mammals    Predicate (P): cold-blooded creatures

Distribution: S (mammals) is distributed — the statement covers every mammal without exception, ruling out every one of them from the cold-blooded class. P (cold-blooded creatures) is also distributed — the statement implies that none of the cold-blooded creatures belong to the mammal class. Every member of the cold-blooded class is addressed: none of them qualify as mammals.

Diagram: Imagine two circles — "mammals" and "cold-blooded creatures" — with no overlap at all. The E statement draws them entirely apart. Compare this to the A statement diagram, where one circle is nested inside the other. Here, the circles do not touch.

Worked Example 2 — Simple Conversion

Original: "No prime numbers greater than 2 are even numbers."

Converted: "No even numbers are prime numbers greater than 2."

Both statements are true, and they say the same thing. Because both terms are distributed in the E statement, swapping them produces a logically equivalent statement. This cannot be done with the A statement: "All dogs are mammals" does not convert to "All mammals are dogs." Only E statements convert simply while preserving equivalence.

Common Error — Converting the A Statement

A student writes: "All squares are rectangles. Therefore, all rectangles are squares."

Analysis: This is the invalid conversion of an A statement. In the A statement, P is not distributed — the statement says nothing about all members of the P class. Converting an A statement as though it were an E statement is a formal fallacy called illicit conversion. Only E statements convert simply. Keeping the distribution of P in mind prevents this error.

Natural-Language Forms of the E Statement

E statements appear in many surface forms. The following expressions typically signal a universal negative claim:

  • "No X is Y" → "No X are Y"
  • "X is never Y" → "No X are Y"
  • "Not a single X is Y" → "No X are Y"
  • "X and Y are mutually exclusive" → "No X are Y" (and by conversion, "No Y are X")
  • "Without exception, X is not Y" → "No X are Y"

Standard-Form Translation

Standard form requires "No S are P" with both S and P as noun phrases. Negation embedded in the verb or adjective must be moved to the quantifier position.

Translation Examples

"Fish never breathe air."
→ S: fish. P: air-breathing creatures. Standard form: No fish are air-breathing creatures.

"Not a single valid argument has a false conclusion guaranteed by true premises."
→ S: valid arguments. P: arguments that guarantee a false conclusion from true premises. Standard form: No valid arguments are arguments that guarantee a false conclusion from true premises.

"Rectangles and circles are mutually exclusive figures."
→ S: rectangles. P: circular figures. Standard form: No rectangles are circular figures. (And by conversion: "No circular figures are rectangles.")

Venn Diagram for the E Statement

The overlap region between S and P is shaded (empty). Nothing belongs to both S and P. Unlike the A statement, this shading is symmetric — the diagram makes the same claim read in either direction, which is exactly why the E statement converts simply.
"No fallacy is a valid form of argument": the overlap between "fallacies" and "valid forms of argument" is shaded, because it is empty.

Distribution Summary for E and A Statements

At this point, you have two of the four statement types. Here is how they compare in terms of distribution:

ENo S are PYesYes
ISome S are P(see next lesson)(see next lesson)
OSome S are not P(see next lesson)(see next lesson)

Notice that the E statement is the only one — so far — that distributes the predicate. This matters enormously in syllogistic reasoning: distributed terms can be used in certain inference steps that undistributed terms cannot.

Why This Matters for Argument

The E statement makes a claim of complete separation between two classes. That is both its power and its vulnerability. Power: if the E statement is true, a great deal follows — neither class contains a single member of the other. Vulnerability: one counterexample destroys it. A single organism that is simultaneously a mammal and cold-blooded (and some do exist, unusually) would falsify "No mammals are cold-blooded."

In an argument, when you use an E statement as a premise, you are relying on a claim that nothing crosses the boundary between two categories. Before accepting such a premise, the habit of rigorous thinking demands a question: is this separation absolute, or are there edge cases? E statements often feel more obviously true than they are, because we tend to think of clear cases rather than borderline ones.

Practice

Part A — Recognition. For each statement, identify whether it is an E statement. If it is not, explain briefly why not.

  1. "No integers are irrational numbers."
  2. "All fish are cold-blooded creatures."
  3. "Not every argument contains a valid inference."
  4. "Never has a square had fewer than four sides."
  5. "Some logical fallacies are difficult to detect."

Part B — Distribution. For each E statement, identify S and P and explain in one sentence why both are distributed.

  1. "No prime numbers are composite numbers."
  2. "No act of genuine deception is an act of honesty."
  3. "No reptiles are warm-blooded animals."

Part C — Translation. Translate each sentence into standard E-statement form ("No S are P"). Identify S and P.

  1. "Fish never breathe through lungs."
  2. "Not a single member of the committee was present at the vote."
  3. "Triangles and quadrilaterals are mutually exclusive polygon types."
  4. "A conclusion that necessarily follows from true premises is never false."

Part D — Conversion. Convert each E statement and confirm that the conversion is logically equivalent.

  1. "No philosophers are athletes."
  2. "No valid syllogisms are arguments with contradictory premises and a true conclusion."
Looking Ahead: The A and E statements are universal — they speak about every member of a class without exception. Lessons 3.03c and 3.03d introduce the two particular statement types (I and O), which restrict their claim to "some" members. The particular statements have a surprising distribution pattern — especially the O statement — that becomes clear once you understand what "some" is actually asserting.
