Lesson 7.01b — Mapping Compound Arguments in Text

Learning objective: Students will identify and map compound arguments embedded in extended prose passages, producing labeled argument tree diagrams that correctly show main conclusions, sub-arguments, and premise relationships.

Orientation

Lesson 7.01a taught you to extract a single argument from surrounding prose. Most real arguments, however, are not single syllogisms — they are compound structures in which sub-arguments support individual premises of a larger argument. This lesson applies the compound argument analysis from Week 7 to extended texts, where the structural relationships are not labeled and must be discovered through careful reading.

Intuitive Entry Point

When you read a well-constructed essay or treatise, you feel that the argument is building — one point establishing something that another point then uses. That sense of accumulation is the logical structure working. Some paragraphs set up something a later paragraph relies on. Some claims appear both as conclusions (in one section) and as premises (in the next). The structural map of a strong argument has a shape, and learning to see that shape in prose is the skill this lesson develops.

The Five-Step Procedure for Mapping a Text Argument

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Read the passage fully before marking anything. Premature marking causes misidentification. The function of a sentence often depends on what comes after it.
  2. Identify the main conclusion. This is the claim the entire passage is trying to establish. Look for conclusion indicators (therefore, thus, consequently, it follows that) or ask: what would make all this reasoning worth doing?
  3. Identify main premises. These are the claims directly offered to support the main conclusion. They answer the question: what reasons are given for the main conclusion?
  4. For each main premise, ask whether it is itself supported. If a sub-argument is offered for a main premise, identify its premises and the intermediate conclusion. Label the intermediate conclusion (IC) and its supporting premises (SP).
  5. Draw the tree and classify premise relationships. Use brackets for linked premises (each individually insufficient); use separate arrows for independent premises (each sufficient on its own). Label the weakest node.

Worked Example

Passage

"All knowledge claims rest on some method of inquiry, whether that method is sensory observation, mathematical demonstration, or philosophical reasoning. A method is reliable only if it consistently produces accurate results when applied correctly. Mathematical demonstration, when applied correctly, consistently produces accurate results — the truth of a valid proof does not depend on the moment of its discovery or on who discovers it. Therefore, mathematical demonstration is a reliable method of inquiry. Since all reliable methods of inquiry produce genuine knowledge, mathematical demonstration produces genuine knowledge."

Step-by-Step Analysis

Main conclusion: "Mathematical demonstration produces genuine knowledge." (Last sentence; "since ... therefore" construction marks the conclusion.)

Main premises:

  1. "Mathematical demonstration is a reliable method of inquiry." (Fourth sentence — this is both a conclusion of the sub-argument and a premise of the main argument: an intermediate conclusion.)
  2. "All reliable methods of inquiry produce genuine knowledge." (Fifth sentence, first clause — a universal categorical premise.)

Sub-argument for the intermediate conclusion:

  • SP1: "A method is reliable only if it consistently produces accurate results when applied correctly." (Second sentence)
  • SP2: "Mathematical demonstration, when applied correctly, consistently produces accurate results." (Third sentence)
  • IC: "Mathematical demonstration is a reliable method of inquiry." (Fourth sentence)

Non-argumentative content: The first sentence provides background — it situates mathematical demonstration within a broader landscape of inquiry methods but is not a premise in the argument being made.

Tree structure:

  • MC: Mathematical demonstration produces genuine knowledge.
  • ↑ [IC + MP2] (linked — both are needed)
  • IC: Mathematical demonstration is a reliable method of inquiry.
  • ↑ [SP1 + SP2] (linked)
  • MP2: All reliable methods of inquiry produce genuine knowledge.

Weakest node: SP2 — the claim that mathematical demonstration consistently produces accurate results is the most open to challenge, since it depends on the claim that every correctly-applied proof is valid, which requires additional argument in cases of contested proofs.

Connection to Argument

Mapping a compound argument in text is the capstone applied skill of categorical logic. Once you can do this, you can take any written argument — philosophical, scientific, legal, or historical — and expose its entire logical skeleton: every premise, every sub-argument, every intermediate conclusion, and the main claim that everything is building toward. You can then identify exactly where to object most effectively, or exactly what would need to be established to strengthen the argument. That is what it means to think rigorously about reasoning.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Recognition). Apply the five-step procedure to the following passage. Identify the main conclusion, main premises, any sub-arguments with intermediate conclusions, and any non-argumentative sentences.

"All bodies in motion remain in motion unless acted upon by an external force. This principle — Newton's first law — is not an observation of any particular body; it is an idealization about all bodies. No real object in ordinary experience moves without eventually stopping, because friction and air resistance always act as external forces. Therefore, everyday experience can mislead us about what motion requires. Since any reliable method of scientific reasoning must be able to override misleading everyday experience, the methods of mathematical physics must sometimes override everyday experience."

Problem 2 (Analysis). Draw a labeled argument tree for the following passage. Identify whether the main premises are linked or independent, and flag the weakest node with a brief explanation.

"The syllogistic logic developed by Aristotle in the Prior Analytics is complete in the sense that every valid syllogistic argument can be derived from the four moods of the first figure. This was Aristotle's own view: he held that the first figure represented perfect syllogisms and that all valid moods in other figures could be reduced to them. Because Aristotle's logical system can express all valid syllogistic arguments, and because any logical system that can do this is adequate for systematic reasoning about classes of things, Aristotle's system is adequate for systematic reasoning about classes of things."

Problem 3 (Construction). Write a passage of five to eight sentences in natural prose that contains: (a) a main argument with two main premises; (b) a sub-argument supporting one of the main premises; (c) at least one non-argumentative sentence. Draw the argument tree for your passage separately.
