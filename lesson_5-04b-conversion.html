Lesson 5.04b — Conversion

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze the structure an author uses to organize a text; ELA.11.C.1.4 — Write arguments to support claims using valid reasoning; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to apply the operation of conversion to all four categorical statement types, correctly identify which conversions are valid (E and I), which require a weakened conclusion (A), and which are invalid (O), and explain the logical reason for each result.

Orientation

In the previous lesson you used the Square of Opposition to derive immediate inferences by changing a statement's quantity and quality while keeping its subject and predicate terms in place. The next three lessons introduce a different family of immediate inference operations: ones that rearrange or replace the terms themselves. Conversion is the simplest of these — it swaps the subject and predicate of a statement. Its rules follow directly from the concept of distribution you have been applying since Quarter 1.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider: "No squares are circles." Does it follow that "No circles are squares"? Most people would say yes — the two statements seem to say exactly the same thing, just from different directions. Now consider: "All dogs are mammals." Does it follow that "All mammals are dogs"? Clearly not — there are many mammals that are not dogs. Something has gone wrong. Both moves swap subject and predicate, yet one works and the other does not. The logical tradition calls this operation conversion, and the rules governing it explain precisely why swapping subject and predicate is sometimes valid and sometimes not.

What Is Conversion?

Conversion is the operation of forming a new categorical statement — the converse — by interchanging the subject and predicate terms of the original statement. The word derives from the Latin convertere, to turn around or reverse. The quality (affirmative or negative) of the statement is preserved; only the positions of the terms change.

Whether a conversion is valid depends on distribution. A term is distributed in a statement if the statement makes a claim about every member of the class it names. When you swap subject and predicate, you must not make a claim about more members of a class than the original statement warranted. If a term was undistributed in the original (the statement said nothing about all members of that class), then claiming something about all members of that class in the converse is an overreach — and the conversion is invalid.

Rules by Statement Type

Statement Type Conversion Result Valid?
E — No S are P Simple conversion No P are S Valid — same truth value
I — Some S are P Simple conversion Some P are S Valid — same truth value
A — All S are P Conversion by limitation Some P are S Valid but weaker — I statement results
O — Some S are not P Not valid — Invalid — no valid converse exists

Why E converts simply: In "No S are P," both S and P are fully distributed (the statement excludes every S from every P, and every P from every S). Swapping them makes no difference — both terms were already claimed in their entirety, so the converse says nothing new. The relationship is symmetric.

Why I converts simply: In "Some S are P," neither S nor P is distributed — the statement claims only a partial overlap. The overlap is symmetric: if some S are among P, then some P are among S. No overclaim is made in either direction.

Why A converts only by limitation: In "All S are P," S is distributed but P is not. "All dogs are mammals" tells us about every dog but says nothing about every mammal. Converting to "All mammals are dogs" would distribute mammals — making a claim about all of them — which the original never licensed. The valid move is to the weaker "Some mammals are dogs," which distributes neither term and stays within what the original warranted.

Why O does not convert: In "Some S are not P," P is distributed but S is not. Converting would produce "Some P are not S," which distributes S — but the original made no claim about all S. The conversion overreaches, and no valid converse of an O statement exists.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — E Statement, Simple Conversion

Original: "No inert gases are reactive elements."

Converse: "No reactive elements are inert gases."

Analysis: E converts simply and validly. Both terms are distributed in the original, so swapping them preserves the full meaning. The converse has the same truth value as the original.

Example 2 — I Statement, Simple Conversion

Original: "Some geometric figures are regular polygons."

Converse: "Some regular polygons are geometric figures."

Analysis: I converts simply. Neither term is distributed, and the overlap between geometric figures and regular polygons is symmetric. Valid.

Example 3 — A Statement, Conversion by Limitation

Original: "All whales are mammals."

Attempted simple converse: "All mammals are whales." — Invalid. This distributes "mammals" but the original made no claim about all mammals.

Valid converse by limitation: "Some mammals are whales." — Valid. This stays within what the original warranted: that the class of whales is entirely contained within mammals, so at least some members of mammals are whales.

Common Error — Illicit Conversion of an A Statement

The error of treating "All S are P" as equivalent to "All P are S" is sometimes called illicit conversion or the fallacy of the converse. It is one of the most common informal reasoning errors. "All poisonous mushrooms are mushrooms with white gills, therefore all mushrooms with white gills are poisonous" is a dangerous real-world instance of this fallacy. Many correlation-causation errors and hasty generalizations in everyday reasoning have this structure.

Connection to Argument

Conversion matters for argument analysis because people frequently reverse categorical statements without noticing whether the reversal is valid. Detecting illicit conversion — especially in A-statement arguments — is a key skill for evaluating inductive generalizations, scientific claims, and everyday conditional reasoning. Additionally, valid conversion is used deliberately in formal logic to rearrange the terms of a syllogism into a more workable form for analysis.

Practice Problems

Part A — Apply the Operation. For each statement below, state the type (A, E, I, or O), perform the conversion (or state that no valid conversion exists for O), and indicate whether the result is a simple conversion, conversion by limitation, or invalid.

  1. "No reptiles are warm-blooded animals."
  2. "All parallelograms are quadrilaterals."
  3. "Some prime numbers are odd numbers."
  4. "Some historical accounts are not reliable records."
  5. "No prime numbers less than 3 are even numbers." (Think carefully about this one.)
  6. "All living organisms are cells or collections of cells."

Part B — Evaluate the Inference. Each item presents an original statement and a claimed converse. State whether the conversion is valid (and of which type) or invalid, and explain in one sentence why.

  1. Original: "All copper conductors are electrical conductors." Claimed converse: "All electrical conductors are copper conductors."
  2. Original: "No sedimentary rocks are igneous rocks." Claimed converse: "No igneous rocks are sedimentary rocks."
  3. Original: "Some ancient philosophers are Stoics." Claimed converse: "Some Stoics are ancient philosophers."
  4. Original: "All equilateral triangles are equiangular triangles." Claimed converse: "Some equiangular triangles are equilateral triangles."

Part C — Construction. Write one original A statement, one original E statement, and one original I statement using topics from science or mathematics. For each, perform the correct conversion and explain whether it is simple, by limitation, or invalid. Use subjects and predicates that make the distribution issue clearly visible.

Looking Ahead: Conversion rearranges terms while preserving quality. The next operation, obversion, works differently: it changes quality and replaces the predicate with its complement, while keeping the subject in place. Remarkably, obversion is valid for all four statement types — a contrast with conversion worth examining closely.
