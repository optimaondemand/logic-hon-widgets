Lesson 2.05b
Begging the Question: The Circular Argument
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments; evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify begging the question in its simple and circular forms, explain why a circular argument provides no independent support for its conclusion, and distinguish this fallacy from arguments that merely contain false premises.

Orientation: Where We Are

In the previous lesson, you learned that a premise needs warrant — a reason to accept it that does not depend on already accepting the conclusion — and that an argument can have relevant, univocal premises and still fail if this requirement is not met. Fallacies of presumption occur when a premise lacks that independent warrant. The first specific fallacy in this category is begging the question. It does not fail because a premise is false, or because its warrant is merely contestable — it fails because the premise's only warrant is the conclusion itself. The premise secretly contains what the argument is supposed to prove, so the argument, however convincing it may seem, goes nowhere at all.

Intuitive Entry: The Self-Certifying Source

Suppose you ask how you can trust a particular reference manual, and someone tells you: "Because the manual says it is reliable, and you can trust what it says." You would immediately recognize the problem. The manual's own claim to be reliable cannot serve as the reason to trust the manual — that is precisely what needs to be established, not assumed. The source is certifying itself. No one who already doubted the manual's accuracy would be moved by this reasoning; it addresses only those who already believe the conclusion.

This pattern — using a source, claim, or principle to establish itself — is the core of begging the question. It appears in more elaborate forms in philosophical and everyday argument, but the fundamental structure is always the same: the argument begins where it needs to end.

The Formal Concept: Petitio Principii

The fallacy is known in Latin as petitio principii — from petitio, "a request" or "a petition" (from petere, "to seek" or "to ask for"), and principium, "a beginning" or "a first principle" (from princeps, "first"). The phrase is sometimes translated "request for the beginning" or "assuming the first principle." The idea is that the arguer is petitioning for — that is, simply taking for granted — the very starting point that needs to be established through the argument. The argument asks to begin from what it should arrive at.

Aristotle identified this fallacy in the Prior Analytics (Book II, Chapter 16) as one of the ways a dialectical proof can fail. He noted that a demonstration goes wrong when someone assumes as a premise the very thing to be demonstrated. This remains the standard definition: begging the question occurs when a premise in an argument assumes, restates, or otherwise presupposes the conclusion that the argument is supposed to establish.

A terminological note is worth making explicitly: in contemporary journalism and everyday speech, the phrase "begs the question" is frequently used to mean "raises the question" or "invites the question" — as in, "The new policy begs the question of how it will be funded." This usage is widespread but logically imprecise. In this course, "beg the question" retains its technical meaning: the fallacy of assuming the conclusion in a premise. The distinction matters, and you should be able to flag it when you encounter the popular misuse.

Why Circularity Provides No Independent Support

The central logical problem with begging the question is not that the conclusion is false — it may be true. The problem is that the argument provides no independent support for the conclusion. A premise supports a conclusion only if it is established separately from the conclusion. When the premise already assumes the conclusion, the two are not independent: accepting the premise requires having already accepted the conclusion, which means the "argument" adds nothing to whatever credence the conclusion already had.

Think of it this way. An argument is supposed to move a person from a state of not-yet-believing the conclusion to a state of believing it, by presenting reasons that can be evaluated on their own. A circular argument cannot do this. If you do not already believe the conclusion, you have no reason to accept the premise that embeds it. If you do already believe the conclusion, you did not need the argument. The argument functions, at best, as a reminder for the already-convinced. It cannot persuade a genuine skeptic, and it cannot constitute a proof.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Simple Restatement

"Dishonesty is always wrong because it is never acceptable to be untruthful."

Analysis: The premise ("it is never acceptable to be untruthful") is not a separate claim from the conclusion ("dishonesty is always wrong") — it is a restatement of the same claim in different words. "Dishonesty," "untruthful," and "wrong / not acceptable" are all close synonyms in this context. The argument has the surface appearance of support — a because-clause follows the conclusion — but the because-clause contains no independent content. Someone who doubted the conclusion would have equal reason to doubt the premise. No progress is made.

Example 2 — Circular Chain

"We can trust the historical record as preserved in this archive because professional archivists selected these documents, and we know the archivists are reliable professionals because their selections have been preserved in this trusted archive."

Analysis: This example involves a loop rather than a single restatement. Statement A ("the archive is trustworthy") is supported by Statement B ("the archivists are reliable"), and Statement B is supported by Statement A. Neither statement is established independently. The two claims support each other in a closed circle, which means neither is actually supported at all. Circular chains of this kind can be difficult to detect because the loop is spread across multiple sentences and can involve more than two claims. The diagnostic question is always: if I tried to use this argument to persuade someone who doubted all the claims simultaneously, where would I begin? In a genuine circle, there is no non-circular starting point.

Example 3 — Subtler Embedded Assumption

"Obviously, any careful reader of this text will recognize that the author's central argument is sound. After all, those who have read it carefully all agree that the argument succeeds."

Analysis: The conclusion is that the author's argument is sound. The premise is that careful readers agree it succeeds. But the premise embeds the conclusion in the word "careful": a "careful reader" is defined implicitly as one who reads in a way that produces agreement. Anyone who reads and disagrees is thereby classified as having not read carefully. The term "careful" is doing the work of excluding potential counterevidence. This is a more disguised form of begging the question, because the circularity is loaded into a single adjective rather than stated in full.

Common Error — Confusing Begging the Question with a False Premise

Consider this argument: "All cats are reptiles. My pet is a cat. Therefore, my pet is a reptile." This argument has a false premise (P1), but it does not beg the question. The premise and the conclusion are independent claims — P1 makes a claim about the biological category of cats, and the conclusion makes a claim about a specific pet. One does not presuppose the other; P1 just happens to be false.

Begging the question requires a specific logical relationship: the premise must presuppose or assume the conclusion. A false premise is simply inaccurate; a circular premise is inaccurate in a specific structural way. When you identify a potential case of begging the question, always ask: "Is the premise independently assertable, or does accepting it require having already accepted the conclusion?"

Connection to the Warrant

Recall from Lesson 2.05a that a premise has warrant only if there is a reason to accept it that does not depend on already accepting the conclusion. In a question-begging argument, the premise's only warrant is the conclusion itself. You would not accept the premise if you did not already accept the conclusion — which means the premise has, in effect, no independent warrant at all. The argument is internally closed: the only way it "works" is if you already grant what it is supposed to establish.

This is why begging the question is a fallacy of presumption rather than a fallacy of relevance. The premise is not irrelevant to the conclusion — it is, in a sense, too directly connected to it. The connection is not the relationship of independent support but the relationship of identity or entailment that runs the wrong direction. Presumption fallacies take for granted what must be established; here, the thing taken for granted is the conclusion itself.

A Note on Persuasive Force

Circular arguments can be psychologically effective. When stated confidently, with elaborate phrasing or apparent erudition, a circular argument can give the impression of a deep and complete case. It is easy to mistake the length or tone of an argument for its logical substance. The test of begging the question cuts through surface impression: identify the warrant, check whether the warrant presupposes the conclusion, and ask whether the argument could persuade a genuine skeptic who did not already accept the conclusion. If the answer to the last question is no, the argument is circular regardless of how convincing it sounds.

Practice Problems

Recognition. For each argument, identify whether it begs the question. If it does, state specifically which premise presupposes the conclusion and explain the logical relationship between them.

  1. "Freedom of expression is a fundamental right because every person has the inherent liberty to speak their mind."
  2. "All mammals have hearts. Whales are mammals. Therefore, whales have hearts."
  3. "This medication is effective because the clinical study confirms that it works, and we know the study is valid because it demonstrated the medication's effectiveness."
  4. "The best students are those who work hardest, which is why the highest-achieving students are the ones who put in the most effort."

Analysis.

  1. Explain in your own words why a circular argument cannot persuade a genuine skeptic. Your answer should address the concept of independent support from Lesson 2.05a.
  2. A classmate says: "If a circular argument has a true conclusion, what's the problem? The conclusion is still true." Write a response of 3–5 sentences explaining what the classmate is missing. Be precise about what an argument is supposed to do.

Construction.

  1. Write a clearly circular argument of your own on any neutral topic. Label the premise and conclusion, and identify the circular element explicitly.
  2. Take the circular argument you wrote in problem 7 and rewrite it as a non-circular argument for the same conclusion. What additional, independent premise did you need to add? What does this revision reveal about what the original argument was lacking?
Looking Ahead: The next lesson examines the complex question (plurium interrogationum) — a close relative of begging the question in which the unwarranted assumption is embedded not in a premise but in the form of a question itself, forcing any direct answer to concede something that has not been established.
