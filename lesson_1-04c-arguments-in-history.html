Arguments in History: The Western Tradition
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.2 — Analyze how the author develops and refines a central idea in arguments from the Classical Period  |  ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the author's use of rhetoric  |  ELA.11.R.2.4 — Compare development of multiple arguments; evaluate effectiveness and validity of reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to identify and reconstruct arguments found in historical Western texts, connecting the formal concepts of the week to the living tradition of argument they are inheriting.

Orientation: Not a Textbook Exercise

This week introduced formal tools — argument types, validity, soundness, Venn diagrams. It would be easy to think of these as invented for textbooks, useful only in class exercises. This lesson corrects that impression.

Formal argument has been practiced in the Western intellectual tradition for over two and a half millennia. The same logical structures you have been studying appear in Plato's dialogues, Aristotle's treatises, and the theological works of the medieval Scholastics. The thinkers who built that tradition were not applying someone else's logic textbook to their problems. They were discovering the rules of rigorous argument in the process of trying to answer the hardest questions they knew how to ask. You are not learning a technique; you are joining a conversation that is still in progress.

Intuitive Entry: The Difference Attention Makes

You have probably argued with someone — defended a position, given reasons, drawn a conclusion. What you were doing informally, the thinkers in this lesson did with extraordinary care and precision. The difference between casual argument and philosophical argument is not intelligence; it is attention to form. When you learn to reconstruct an argument in standard form, you are applying the same discipline Aristotle applied in the Prior Analytics and Aquinas applied in every article of the Summa Theologiae. Precision of this kind is a habit, and habits are formed by practice. That is what this lesson is for.

Three Historical Arguments

Socrates — Plato's Meno (c. 385 BCE)

Context

In the Meno, Socrates and Meno examine whether virtue can be taught. Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) wrote the dialogues in which Socrates appears; the historical Socrates left no writings of his own. The following argument is a simplified version of one of Socrates' central moves in the dialogue, reconstructed from Plato's text.

The Argument (paraphrased)

If virtue were a kind of knowledge, it could be taught — for all knowledge is teachable. But we observe that there are no reliable teachers of virtue: those acknowledged as virtuous men have not succeeded in making their own sons virtuous. Therefore, virtue is probably not a kind of knowledge.

Adapted from Plato, Meno, 89d–96c.

Standard Form

Reconstructed Argument P1: If virtue is a kind of knowledge, then virtue can be taught (because all knowledge is teachable).
P2: Virtue cannot be reliably taught — there are no consistent teachers of virtue, and virtuous men do not reliably produce virtuous sons.
C: Therefore, virtue is probably not a kind of knowledge.

Analytical Note

Notice that the conclusion is qualified: "probably." Socrates himself remains uncertain. The argument is best understood as inductive or abductive rather than strictly deductive: the absence of reliable teachers is evidence against virtue being knowledge, but it does not rule out the possibility with necessity. The premises most open to challenge: Is it actually true that all knowledge is teachable? And is the absence of reliable teachers sufficient evidence that the thing being taught is not knowledge, or might virtue be a kind of knowledge that is simply very difficult to transmit?

Aristotle — Politics, Book I (c. 335–323 BCE)

Context

In the Politics, Aristotle argues that the city-state (the polis) is natural — that human beings are, by their nature, political animals, not self-sufficient individuals who associate merely for convenience. This argument has shaped political philosophy from antiquity through the present. Aristotle (384–322 BCE), a student of Plato and tutor of Alexander the Great, founded the Lyceum in Athens and wrote systematically on logic, ethics, politics, metaphysics, biology, and rhetoric.

The Argument (paraphrased)

Every community exists for some good. The polis is the most complete of all communities, encompassing all others. Therefore, the polis aims at the highest good. Furthermore, a human being who cannot live in community — who needs no one — is either a beast or a god, not a human being. Since we are neither beasts nor gods, we are by nature social, and the polis is a natural institution.

Adapted from Aristotle, Politics, I.2, 1252a–1253a.

Standard Form

First Argument: The Polis and the Highest Good P1: Every community exists for the sake of some good; the highest community aims at the highest good.
P2: The polis is the highest and most complete form of human community.
C: Therefore, the polis aims at the highest human good.
Second Argument: The "Political Animal" P1: Any creature that can live entirely outside of human community is either below human nature (a beast) or above it (a god).
P2: Human beings are neither beasts nor gods.
C: Therefore, human beings cannot live entirely outside of human community — they are, by nature, political animals.

Analytical Note

The second argument is deductively valid: if both premises are granted, the conclusion must follow. The premise that invites the most scrutiny is P1 of the second argument — the claim that only beasts and gods are truly self-sufficient. Is this an empirical observation about human beings, or is it a definitional claim about what it means to be human? How one answers that question shapes how one evaluates the argument. Notice, too, that Aristotle does not present this as a value judgment — he presents it as a claim about nature. Reconstruction in standard form makes that move visible in a way that reading the prose alone might not.

Thomas Aquinas — Summa Theologiae, Prima Pars, Q.2, Art.3 (c. 1265–1274 CE)

Context

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), Dominican philosopher and theologian, presented five arguments for the existence of God — the "Five Ways" — in his Summa Theologiae. The First Way, the argument from motion, is among the most discussed deductive arguments in the Western tradition. Aquinas was writing in the Scholastic tradition, which took seriously both Aristotle's philosophy and the claims of Christian theology, and sought to show that reason and faith are not opposed. The version below is a simplified reconstruction; the full argument is more technically precise.

The Argument (paraphrased)

We observe that things in the world are in motion. Everything that is in motion is being moved by something else — nothing moves itself from a state of rest. If we trace this chain of movers back, we cannot regress infinitely; an infinite regress explains nothing, because there would be no original source of motion. Therefore, there must be a first mover that is not itself moved by anything — and this first, unmoved mover everyone calls God.

Adapted from Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, I, Q.2, Art.3.

Standard Form

The First Way (Argument from Motion) P1: Everything that is in motion is being moved by something other than itself.
P2: An infinite regress of movers is impossible — it would explain nothing, because there would be no original source of motion.
P3: We observe that things are in motion.
C: Therefore, there must be a first, unmoved mover — a source of all motion that is not itself moved by anything external to it.

Analytical Note

Each step of this argument follows from the previous by logical structure, and the form is deductively valid given the three premises. The premises most commonly challenged are P1 and P2. P1 raises questions in light of modern physics: does everything in motion require an external cause of that motion? Newton's first law of inertia suggests that an object already in motion continues moving without an external cause. P2 raises a metaphysical question: is an infinite regress actually impossible, or merely unfamiliar? Neither question is answered here. The task in this lesson is not to evaluate whether the argument succeeds — it is to reconstruct it clearly enough that evaluation becomes possible. You cannot challenge an argument you cannot see.

Why Standard Form Matters

Why go through the trouble of reconstructing these arguments in standard form? Because standard form forces precision. When Aquinas writes his argument, the logical structure is distributed across paragraphs of Latin scholastic prose, structured according to the question-and-reply format of the Summa. When Aristotle argues in the Politics, the reasoning is embedded in observations, digressions, and rhetorical qualifications. Reconstructing either in P1/P2/C form makes immediately visible three things: which claims are the premises (and therefore require their own justification), which claim is the conclusion (and therefore requires the premises to support it), and where the argument can be challenged.

The same structure that exposes these arguments to scrutiny also protects them from misrepresentation. A reader who disagrees with Aquinas's conclusion but has not reconstructed the argument clearly is in a weak position: they may be objecting to a rhetorical element, a translation choice, or a claim Aquinas did not actually make. A reader who has reconstructed the argument in standard form knows exactly which premise to target. Precision serves both the defender and the critic. This is why the tradition teaches argument analysis as a skill prior to taking positions on the arguments themselves.

The Conversation You Are Joining

The Western intellectual tradition is, in large part, a tradition of argument. Plato wrote dialogues rather than treatises because he believed the examined argument — tested, challenged, revised — was the only honest path toward knowledge. Aristotle built his treatises on explicit premises whose consequences he traced with care. Aquinas framed every theological question as an argument structure: objections, their replies, a central position defended by explicit reasons. These were not rhetorical performances. They were invitations to engage on specific, checkable grounds.

When you learn to reconstruct arguments in standard form, you are not just practicing a textbook skill. You are learning to engage the tradition on its own terms — to see what the great thinkers were actually claiming, why they thought those claims were supported, and where the reasoning is strong or fragile. The formal vocabulary you have learned this week — validity, soundness, premises, conclusions — is the vocabulary you need to participate in that conversation rather than merely observe it.

Practice Problems

Recognition

  1. Return to the Socrates argument above. Is the argument best classified as deductive, inductive, or abductive? Identify the specific feature of the argument — a word, a phrase, or the structure of the reasoning — that determines your classification.
  2. In Aristotle's second argument (the "political animal" argument), identify: (a) the two premises, (b) the conclusion, and (c) whether the argument is valid, assuming the premises are granted. Explain your answer to (c) in one or two sentences.

Analysis

  1. Aquinas's First Way includes the premise: "An infinite regress of movers is impossible." This is the premise modern readers most often question. Write 2–3 sentences: What would have to be true about the world for this premise to be false? If it is false, what happens to the conclusion?
  2. Socrates argues that because virtue has no reliable teachers, it is probably not knowledge. Suppose someone discovered a highly effective and consistent method for teaching virtue — one that worked reliably across many students and contexts. How would this discovery affect the argument? Would it prove the conclusion false, or would it only weaken it? Explain the difference.

Construction

  1. Choose any two of the three historical arguments above. For each, identify the one premise you find most open to challenge. Write 2–3 sentences for each: (a) What exactly is the premise claiming? (b) Why might it be challenged? (c) If it were false, would the argument still produce a valid conclusion? Would it produce any conclusion at all?
  2. Try this yourself. Think of any claim you believe is true. Write a deductive argument in standard form (P1, P2, C) for that claim. Use a neutral, non-controversial topic. Then evaluate your own argument: (a) Is it valid? (b) Are the premises actually true? (c) If a careful reader challenged one premise, how would you respond?
Looking Ahead: Next week begins the study of informal and formal fallacies — the ways arguments can go wrong. Having seen what a well-formed argument looks like, both in formal terms and in the hands of careful thinkers, you are now equipped to recognize when reasoning fails. Validity and soundness are the positive standards; fallacies are the systematic ways those standards get violated.
