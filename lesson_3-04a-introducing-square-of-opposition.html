Lesson 3.04a — Introducing the Square of Opposition

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.2.4 — Analyze claims and use evidence to evaluate them  |  ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning  |  ELA.11.C.5.1 — Improve writing and reasoning through revision

Learning Objective: Students will be able to describe the Square of Opposition, identify the four corners of the Square and the statement type at each corner, name the five inferential relationships the Square maps, and explain what it means for two statements to stand in a logical relationship on the Square.

Where We Are

You now have the full AEIO system: four categorical statement types, each with a known form, a known distribution pattern, and a known relationship to what it asserts about two classes of things. What you do not yet have is a map of how these four statement types relate to each other when they share the same subject and predicate terms. That map is the Square of Opposition.

The Square is the culmination of Week 8's work and the gateway to Q2. Every syllogism you will analyze in Q2 depends on understanding what follows from what. The Square teaches you exactly that — but at the level of single statements rather than multi-premise arguments. It answers the question: if I know whether one of these four statements is true or false, what can I infer about the other three?

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider the statement "All ravens are black birds." Now consider these three variations on the same terms:

  • "No ravens are black birds." (E)
  • "Some ravens are black birds." (I)
  • "Some ravens are not black birds." (O)

If you know that the A statement is true — that all ravens really are black — what follows about the other three? You immediately sense that the E statement must be false (you can't have all ravens black and also no ravens black). The I statement must be true (if all ravens are black, then certainly some are). The O statement must also be false (if all are black, you cannot have some that are not). You have just used the Square of Opposition informally. The Square is the formal system that makes these inferences rigorous and extends them to all four statement types, including cases where the initial truth value is uncertain.

The History of the Square

Aristotle laid the groundwork for the Square in his Prior Analytics and On Interpretation, where he analyzed the logical relations between universal and particular statements with the same subject and predicate terms. The Square as a geometric diagram — four corners, five relationships — was systematized by Apuleius (second century CE) and later by the medieval Scholastics who incorporated it into the standard logic curriculum. Porphyry's Isagoge and Boethius's commentaries helped transmit it to the Latin West, where it became a staple of university logic instruction from the twelfth century onward.

The Square is not merely a historical curiosity. It is a practical tool — a compressed map of the inferential consequences of categorical claims. Any time you evaluate whether a particular statement follows from a universal one, or whether one statement contradicts another, you are using the Square.

The Four Corners

The Square of Opposition is built on a single condition: all four statements must share the same subject term (S) and the same predicate term (P). The four corners correspond to the four AEIO types:

The two diagonals are the contradictories: A stands in the contradictory relationship to O, and E stands in the contradictory relationship to I. These are the two pairs for which knowing the truth value of one statement always tells you the truth value of the other, with no exceptions and no cases left undetermined.

The arrangement is not arbitrary. Affirmative statements (A and I) are on the left; negative statements (E and O) are on the right. Universal statements (A and E) are on top; particular statements (I and O) are on the bottom. This layout makes the five relationships visible as spatial relationships: two diagonals, one top horizontal, one bottom horizontal, and two verticals.

The Five Relationships — Overview

The Square maps five inferential relationships between pairs of statements. Each relationship specifies what follows when one statement is known to be true, false, or undetermined:

RelationshipPairKey Rule
ContradictoriesA ↔ O (diagonal)
E ↔ I (diagonal)		Cannot both be true; cannot both be false. Knowing one determines the other.
ContrariesA ↔ E (top)Cannot both be true; can both be false. If one is true, the other is false — but falsity of one leaves the other undetermined.
SubcontrariesI ↔ O (bottom)Cannot both be false; can both be true. If one is false, the other is true — but truth of one leaves the other undetermined.
SubalternationA → I (vertical)
E → O (vertical)		Truth flows downward: if the universal is true, the particular must be true.
SuperalternationI → (affects A)
O → (affects E)		Falsity flows upward: if the particular is false, the universal must be false.

These five relationships are studied in detail across Lessons 3.04b–3.04d. This lesson introduces the full architecture so you can see where each piece fits before examining each relationship in turn.

A Critical Condition: The Classical Square and Existential Import

The Square as described above follows the classical (traditional) interpretation of categorical logic, which assumes that the subject class is non-empty — that there are actually things that the subject term applies to. Under this assumption, all five relationships hold as described. This is the interpretation used in this course, consistent with Aristotle's original framework and the medieval Scholastic tradition.

Modern (Boolean) logic adopts a different interpretation that does not assume non-empty subject classes, and under that interpretation, subalternation does not hold. This is a genuine and important difference, but it is a topic for a later logic course. For now: we work within the classical Square, with the assumption that the subject class has at least one member.

Connection to Argument

Every formal argument built from categorical statements relies on the inferential relationships the Square maps. When you construct a syllogism in Q2, you are working with two premises and a conclusion — all of which are categorical statements. The rules that govern what follows from what in a syllogism are the rules of the Square applied across three statements at once. Understanding the Square thoroughly is the preparation that makes syllogistic reasoning intelligible rather than merely mechanical.

Practice

Part A — Architecture. Draw the Square of Opposition from memory. At each corner, write the statement type, its name (Universal Affirmative, etc.), and its standard form. Label all five relationships on the appropriate sides and diagonals.

Part B — Placing Statements on the Square. For each pair of statements below, identify which relationship on the Square they stand in (Contradictories, Contraries, Subcontraries, Subalternation, or Superalternation). Both statements in each pair share the same S and P terms.

  1. "All acts of courage are virtuous acts." / "Some acts of courage are not virtuous acts."
  2. "No philosophers are athletes." / "Some philosophers are not athletes."
  3. "All prime numbers greater than 2 are odd numbers." / "Some prime numbers greater than 2 are odd numbers."
  4. "No mammals are reptiles." / "Some mammals are reptiles."
  5. "Some geometric figures are triangles." / "Some geometric figures are not triangles."

Part C — Reflection. In 3–4 sentences, explain why the Square of Opposition requires all four statements to share the same subject and predicate terms. What goes wrong if the terms differ? Give one example to illustrate.

Looking Ahead: Lesson 3.04b examines the most powerful relationship on the Square — the contradictory — in detail. Understanding why contradictories cannot both be true and cannot both be false, and how to use that relationship to infer a statement's truth value from its contradictory, is the first formal inference skill of the Square.
