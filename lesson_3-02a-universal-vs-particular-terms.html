Lesson 3.02a
Universal and Particular Terms

Learning Objective: Students will identify whether a given term functions as universal, particular, or singular, and will explain how the scope of a term determines what a categorical statement can and cannot assert.

Orientation: Where We Are

In Lessons 3.01a and 3.01b, we distinguished terms by the kind of precision they carry — a univocal term means exactly one thing wherever it appears; an equivocal term carries multiple unrelated meanings; an analogous term carries related but proportionally different meanings across contexts. That distinction asks: how steady is this term's meaning?

Today we make a different and equally important distinction. We are no longer asking about meaning-stability; we are asking about scope of reference — that is, how much of a class a term reaches out and grabs. Does it claim something about every member of a class, or only some? Does it pick out one particular individual, or does it range over a kind? These questions lead us to the distinction between universal and particular terms — the conceptual pivot on which all of categorical logic turns.

Intuitive Entry Point

Consider the difference between these two things a coach might say before a game:

"Every player on this team gives one hundred percent."
"Some players on this team have played varsity before."

These are not merely different claims about quantity. They do something structurally different. The first statement commits the coach to something about each and every member of the roster — if a single player loafs, the statement is false. The second statement commits to almost nothing about the team as a whole: it says at least one player has varsity experience, and leaves everything else open. If half the team has never played varsity, the second statement is still perfectly true.

You already know how to use this distinction in everyday reasoning — you know intuitively that "some students were late" does not mean "all students were late." What you are about to learn is how to make that distinction precise enough to use in formal arguments.

Universal Terms

A universal term applies to every member of a class without exception. In Latin, the relevant word is universalis — literally "turned toward the whole" (uni- + vertere). When we use a universal term in a categorical statement, we are asserting something about the entire extension of that term — every single thing the term could be truly applied to.

Universal scope is typically signaled by quantifiers such as: all, every, each, any, no, none, never, always. The following are examples of universally quantified terms in use:

  • "All human beings possess reason."
  • "Every act of injustice harms the community."
  • "No contradiction can be true."
  • "None of the premises in this argument are relevant."

Notice that the word "no" produces a universal statement — it is not particular just because it is negative. A statement that says nothing belongs to a class is just as sweeping as a statement that says everything belongs to it. Both cover the entire extension. Universal terms come in two flavors: affirmative (all S) and negative (no S) — but both are universal in scope.

Particular Terms

A particular term applies to at least one — but not necessarily all — members of a class. From the Latin particularis, meaning "relating to a part." When we use a particular term in a categorical statement, we are asserting something about some portion of the extension, without specifying how large that portion is.

Particular scope is typically signaled by quantifiers such as: some, at least one, there exists, many, most, several, a few, not all. Examples:

  • "Some philosophers are also mathematicians."
  • "At least one courageous act has gone unrecognized."
  • "Several students arrived early."
  • "Most arguments contain unstated assumptions."

A crucial and often overlooked point: in standard categorical logic, "some" means at least one, not exactly some but not all. If it turns out that all philosophers are mathematicians, the statement "Some philosophers are mathematicians" is still true — it just understates the situation. Particular statements do not rule out the universal case; they merely do not assert it.

Singular Terms

A singular term refers to exactly one individual — a proper name, a definite description, or a demonstrative phrase pointing to a specific thing. Examples:

  • Proper names: Socrates, Aristotle, Rome, the Eiffel Tower
  • Definite descriptions: the first President of the United States, the conclusion of this argument
  • Demonstrative phrases: this act of courage, that premise, the argument before us

In Aristotelian categorical logic, singular terms are treated as universal in force when they appear as the subject of a proposition. The reasoning is straightforward: if "Socrates" refers to exactly one individual, then any statement about "Socrates" is implicitly about all of Socrates — the whole individual is included. "Socrates is mortal" leaves no part of Socrates outside the claim. For the purposes of testing validity, we handle singular statements just as we handle universal ones.

Common Terms

A common term (or general term) refers to a kind or class rather than an individual. "Human being," "argument," "virtue," "tree," "prime number" — these are common terms. They do not pick out any one thing; they name a type. Most of the work in categorical logic is done with common terms. Universal and particular statements are built by attaching a quantifier (all/some/no) to a common term.

The common/singular distinction and the universal/particular distinction operate on different axes. Here is a simple map:

TypeWhat it refers toTreated as
Universal common termEvery member of a classUniversal
Particular common termSome member(s) of a classParticular
Singular termExactly one individualUniversal (in logical force)

A Counterintuitive Point: Scope Is Not About Population

The universal/particular distinction is about the logical scope of the claim, not about how many things actually exist. This is easy to confuse, so it deserves its own treatment.

Example — Scope Without Existence

"All unicorns are magical creatures."

This is a universal statement. It covers every member of the class unicorns — which may be empty. If no unicorns exist, the statement is vacuously true: there is no unicorn that fails to be magical. The universality is logical scope, not a claim that many unicorns exist.

Example — Particular Scope Despite Large Numbers

"Some presidents of the United States were lawyers before entering office."

This is a particular statement — even though a large majority of presidents were in fact lawyers. "Some" makes a weak claim: at least one. The fact that the true proportion is large does not change the statement's logical form. It is still particular in scope.

A Trickier Case — "Only" and "None But"

"Only citizens may vote."

This looks particular at first, because it seems to single out a group. But "only" constructions are actually universal — they assert something about the entire class of voters (all of them are citizens). The correct standard-form translation is: "All those who may vote are citizens." This is a universal affirmative statement. The word "only" reverses the subject-predicate order but preserves universal scope. We will return to this kind of tricky case in Lesson 3.02b.

Connection to Argument

The universal/particular distinction is not a semantic curiosity — it is the engine of syllogistic reasoning. In a valid categorical syllogism, the rules governing which terms are distributed (universal in scope) or undistributed (particular in scope) determine whether an inference is valid. A syllogism that attempts to draw a universal conclusion from two particular premises commits a formal error. A term that appears as particular in a premise cannot be made universal in the conclusion.

Before you can check any of these rules, you must be able to look at a statement and immediately answer: is each term here universal or particular? That identification is the skill this lesson builds. Every formal validity check you will ever perform depends on it.

Practice Problems

Part A — Recognition: Identify the scope. For each term or statement, identify whether the subject term is universal, particular, or singular. If the statement is given, also note the basis for your classification (what word or phrase signals scope).

  1. "All acts of cruelty leave a mark."
  2. "Some arguments fail to provide evidence."
  3. "Aristotle taught at the Lyceum."
  4. "No valid argument has a false conclusion that follows from true premises."
  5. "Several of the premises in this argument are ambiguous."
  6. "The first theorem of Euclid concerns the construction of an equilateral triangle."
  7. "Most students find the concept of distribution counterintuitive at first."
  8. "Not all statements are arguments."

Part B — Analysis: Evaluate the scope claim.

  1. A student says: "'Some philosophers are wise' means fewer than half of philosophers are wise." Is the student correct? Explain why or why not using the precise logical meaning of "some."
  2. A student says: "'No politicians are philosophers' is a particular statement because it picks out politicians as a specific group." What has the student confused? Correct the error.
  3. "Only the diligent deserve success." Identify the apparent and the actual scope of this statement. Why does the word "only" create a trap for the untrained reader?

Part C — Construction: Produce your own examples.

  1. Write one universal affirmative, one universal negative, one particular affirmative, and one particular negative statement about the class of arguments. Each should be clearly in the correct scope.
  2. Choose any singular term (a person, a place, a specific historical event). Write a statement using that singular term as subject. Then explain why, for the purposes of categorical logic, it functions as a universal statement.
