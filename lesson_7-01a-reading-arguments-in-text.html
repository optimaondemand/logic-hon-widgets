Lesson 7.01a
Reading Arguments in Text
CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.3.3 — Analyze and evaluate the effectiveness of an author's argument; ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the reasoning in an author's argument; ELA.12.R.3.4 — Evaluate an author's reasoning; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning
Learning objective: Students will identify arguments embedded in extended prose passages, distinguishing argumentative sentences from non-argumentative context, background, and elaboration.

Orientation

For most of this course, arguments have been presented in standard form — explicitly labeled premises followed by a clearly stated conclusion. That is the logician's notation: a tool for analysis, not the way arguments actually appear in writing. Now that you can identify, analyze, and reconstruct formal arguments, you are ready to find them where they actually live: inside continuous prose. This week builds the applied skill that makes everything you have learned usable in practice — and this week also opens the Culminating Essay Project, where you begin building your own extended argument from the ground up.

Intuitive Entry Point

When you read a paragraph from a textbook, a newspaper editorial, or a philosophical treatise, you already sense that some sentences are doing argumentative work — they are reasons being offered, or conclusions being reached — while others are setting the scene, explaining background, or providing examples. You make that distinction without thinking about it. This lesson makes it explicit.

The core challenge is that not every sentence in a passage is a claim in the argument. Some sentences are context (background information that is not being argued for). Some are elaboration (development of a claim already made). Some are illustration (examples that show what is meant, not reasons for thinking it is true). Learning to sort argumentative sentences from non-argumentative ones is the first step in extracting the argument's logical skeleton from the prose that surrounds it.

Formal Concept: Argumentative vs. Non-Argumentative Sentences

The test: A sentence is argumentative if it serves as a premise or conclusion in the argument — that is, if removing it would either eliminate a reason being offered or remove the claim being supported. A sentence is non-argumentative if it provides context, elaboration, or illustration without itself being a reason or a conclusion.

Three types of non-argumentative sentences appear frequently in extended prose:

  • Background: information that orients the reader but is not itself a premise. Example: "Aristotle was born in 384 BCE in the northern Greek city of Stagira." This explains who Aristotle was; it is not a reason for any conclusion.
  • Elaboration: a sentence that develops or clarifies a claim already made, without adding new logical content. Example: "This means, in other words, that no valid syllogism can have two negative premises." The phrase "in other words" signals elaboration, not a new premise.
  • Illustration: an example offered to show what a claim means, not to prove it. Example: "Consider, for instance, the case of a horse, which is clearly an animal." The example shows what is meant by the universal claim; it does not establish the claim for all cases.

A useful test for distinguishing premises from elaboration is the suppression test: if you remove the sentence from the passage and nothing changes in the logical support for the conclusion, the sentence was elaborative. If the conclusion now has one fewer reason to support it, the sentence was a premise.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — A Short Philosophical Passage

"All living things require energy to sustain their biological processes. Plants are living things. Therefore, plants require energy to sustain their biological processes. This is why photosynthesis — the process by which plants convert sunlight into usable chemical energy — is so fundamental to plant biology."

Analysis:

  • Sentence 1: Argumentative — universal major premise.
  • Sentence 2: Argumentative — minor premise identifying the subject class.
  • Sentence 3: Argumentative — conclusion ("Therefore" signals this explicitly).
  • Sentence 4: Non-argumentative (elaboration) — it explains why the conclusion matters, but it does not add a new logical reason. Removing it does not weaken the argument.

The argument is a Barbara (AAA-1) syllogism: All M are P; All S are M; therefore, All S are P.

Example 2 — A More Complex Passage

"The Scholastic logicians of the 13th century preserved and refined Aristotle's logical works at a time when those texts had been largely lost to Western Europe. Their work was possible only because Islamic scholars had translated and commented on Aristotle during the intervening centuries. Without those translations, the Aristotelian tradition would have been inaccessible. Consequently, the continuity of formal logic in Western thought depended substantially on intellectual transmission across cultural boundaries."

Analysis:

  • Sentence 1: Argumentative — a historical premise about the Scholastics.
  • Sentence 2: Argumentative — a supporting premise that explains why the Scholastic work was possible.
  • Sentence 3: Argumentative — a conditional inference linking translation to accessibility (sub-argument premise).
  • Sentence 4: Argumentative — the main conclusion ("Consequently" is the conclusion indicator).

This passage is almost entirely argumentative — every sentence plays a logical role. That is not unusual in philosophical or historical writing, where authors argue in a sustained, economical way.

Common Error — Treating All Examples as Premises

Students often mark illustration sentences as premises because they seem to support the claim. But an example is evidence of what a term means, not proof that a claim is true universally. If a passage says "All mammals are warm-blooded — consider the dog, which maintains a constant body temperature," the dog example is not an additional premise; it is an illustration of what "warm-blooded" means in this context. The argument stands or falls without it.

Connection to Argument

The ability to extract an argument from continuous prose is the applied form of everything this course has built. You cannot analyze an argument's mood and figure, or identify its fallacies, or evaluate whether it is sound, until you have correctly identified what the argument actually is. Separating argument from context, elaboration, and illustration is the prerequisite for every other analytical move.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Recognition). Read the following passage. Identify each sentence as argumentative (premise or conclusion) or non-argumentative (background, elaboration, or illustration). For each argumentative sentence, say whether it is a premise or the conclusion.

"Geometry was one of the foundational subjects in Plato's Academy. Plato famously inscribed above the entrance: 'Let no one ignorant of geometry enter here.' Mathematical objects, for Plato, were eternal and unchanging. Because only what is eternal and unchanging can be an object of genuine knowledge, mathematical objects can be objects of genuine knowledge. Therefore, mathematics provides a model of what knowledge looks like — reliable, certain, independent of the shifting particulars of sense experience."

Problem 2 (Analysis). The following passage contains both argumentative and non-argumentative sentences. Extract the argument in standard form (premises + conclusion), omitting elaborative and context sentences. Then state how many premises the argument has and whether they are linked or independent.

"The medieval doctrine of the Great Chain of Being held that every existing thing occupies a fixed place in a hierarchy from the simplest matter to the divine. This was not merely a theological claim — it had logical implications. Any creature that reasons can examine the order of things. Humans are creatures that reason. Therefore, humans can examine the order of things. This capacity, it was widely held, distinguished humans from all other earthly creatures, though later thinkers debated the exact nature of that distinction."

Problem 3 (Construction). Write a short passage (4–6 sentences) in natural prose that contains exactly one syllogism. Include at least one non-argumentative sentence (background, elaboration, or illustration) alongside the argumentative sentences. Then, separately, extract and write the syllogism in standard form.
Looking Ahead: Once you can extract a single argument from prose, the next step is handling the more complex case: passages that contain multiple arguments — sub-arguments that feed each other and a main argument that draws on all of them.
