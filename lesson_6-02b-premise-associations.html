Lesson 6.02b — Linked Premises: Extended Practice

CPALMS Benchmarks: ELA.11.R.3.3 — Analyze and evaluate the effectiveness of an author's argument; ELA.11.R.3.4 — Evaluate the reasoning in an author's argument; ELA.12.C.1.3 — Write arguments using sound reasoning; ELA.K12.EE.1.1 — Cite evidence to explain and justify reasoning

Learning Objective: Students will be able to explain why the premises of a categorical syllogism are always linked rather than independent, apply the removal test to confirm that a syllogism's premises work only jointly, and consolidate the reconstruction and Venn-diagram skills from the past two weeks through extended practice.

Orientation

The previous lesson introduced the main argument / sub-argument distinction and the argument tree as a way to represent compound arguments visually. Before you build a tree for a compound argument built from categorical syllogisms, it helps to be explicit about one structural fact you have actually been relying on since Q2 Week 2: within a single categorical syllogism, the two premises always work as a team. Neither premise, alone, establishes anything. This lesson makes that fact explicit, and then uses it as the occasion for consolidated practice — reconstructing enthymemes, diagramming syllogisms, and building argument trees — before you extend to full chains next lesson.

Intuitive Entry Point

A suspension bridge is held up by two cables working together — neither cable alone would support the bridge's weight. If one cable snaps, the bridge falls regardless of the other. That is the structure of the two premises in a categorical syllogism: both are required, and neither does any work without the other.

Linked Premises

Linked premises are premises that must be taken together to support the conclusion — neither alone is sufficient. Every categorical syllogism you have studied has this structure by its very definition: the major premise ("All mammals are warm-blooded") does nothing to establish the conclusion without the minor premise ("Whales are mammals"), and vice versa. Remove either premise and the inference fails entirely — there is no partial credit, no weaker version of the conclusion left standing. In an argument tree, linked premises are indicated by connecting them with a bracket or bar before the arrow that leads to the conclusion — they are a unit.

The test for linkedness: remove one premise and ask whether the remaining premise still supports the conclusion on its own. If removing either single premise collapses the inference entirely, the premises are linked. Apply this test to any categorical syllogism and the answer is always the same — this is a structural fact about syllogisms, not something that varies from argument to argument.

A Note on Scope

You may encounter, in other contexts, the idea of "independent" or "convergent" premises — separate pieces of evidence that each provide some support for a conclusion on their own, so that losing one still leaves partial support standing (three witnesses independently placing a suspect at a scene, for instance). That pattern belongs to inductive argument, where a conclusion is made more or less probable by an accumulation of separate evidence. It does not apply to the categorical syllogisms this course is built on: a categorical syllogism has exactly two premises, and they are linked by definition. Keeping this distinction sharp matters, because blurring it invites you to treat a syllogism's premises as though one could be lost without cost — which is never true of a valid categorical argument.

Worked Examples: Consolidated Practice

Example 1 — Confirming Linkedness with the Removal Test

Argument: "No valid syllogism has two negative premises. This argument has two negative premises. Therefore, this argument is not a valid syllogism."

Premises: (P1) No valid syllogism has two negative premises. (P2) This argument has two negative premises.

Removal test: Remove P1 — does P2 alone support the conclusion? No; P2 merely describes the argument without evaluating it. Remove P2 — does P1 alone support the conclusion? No; P1 states a general rule but says nothing about this particular argument. Neither premise survives on its own. Linked.

Consequence for evaluation: To defeat this argument, you need to challenge only one of the two premises — but you must challenge at least one, since together they are what does the work.

Example 2 — Reconstructing an Enthymeme, Then Confirming Linkedness

Enthymeme: "This proof must be valid — it derives its conclusion strictly from the axioms."

Reconstruction (applying the seven-step procedure from Lesson 6.01c):

All arguments that derive their conclusion strictly from established axioms are valid arguments.
This proof derives its conclusion strictly from the axioms.
Therefore, this proof is valid.

Removal test: Remove the major premise — does the minor premise alone tell you the proof is valid? No, it only describes how the proof proceeds. Remove the minor premise — does the major premise alone tell you anything about this proof? No, it is a general rule with nothing yet to apply it to. Linked, exactly as the syllogism form requires.

Connection to Argument

Knowing that a syllogism's premises are always linked tells you immediately how to evaluate one: check both premises, because a flaw in either one is fatal to the whole. This is the core practical skill of dialectic, which Aristotle described in the Topics as the art of finding the questions that put an argument under the greatest pressure. In the next lesson, you extend this single-syllogism picture to full chains, where the conclusion of one linked pair becomes a premise for the next.

Practice Problems

Part A — Removal Test. For each syllogism, apply the removal test explicitly: state what happens to the inference when each premise is removed in turn, and confirm that the premises are linked.

  1. Major premise: "All equilateral triangles are equiangular triangles." Minor premise: "Triangle XYZ is an equilateral triangle." Conclusion: "Triangle XYZ is an equiangular triangle."
  2. Major premise: "No valid deductive argument has true premises and a false conclusion." Minor premise: "This argument has true premises and a false conclusion." Conclusion: "This argument is not a valid deductive argument."

Part B — Reconstruct, Then Test. Reconstruct each enthymeme into a full categorical syllogism (as in Lesson 6.01c), then apply the removal test to confirm the two premises are linked.

  1. "This sample must be a metal — it conducts electricity readily."
  2. "The treaty cannot be in force, since it was never ratified by the required number of signatories."

Part C — Diagram and Confirm. Take the syllogism you reconstructed in Part B, Problem 1, and diagram it on a three-circle Venn diagram (as in Lesson 5.02c–5.02d) to confirm it is valid. Then write two sentences explaining how the diagram itself demonstrates linkedness: what happens to the diagram's ability to show the conclusion if you erase the shading from only one of the two premises?
